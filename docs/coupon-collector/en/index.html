The puzzle

Picture this: a new 50-sticker album

In probability theory this is the coupon collector's problem. It comes in many forms: album stickers, toys in chocolate eggs, cards in packs of gum. The answer is always the same: the end of the collection costs the most.

  1. 1

    The album

    The album has 50 numbered slots. Each slot has its own sticker.

  2. 2

    The stickers

    Stickers come in sealed packs. You can't see which one you get: each of the 50 is equally likely, and every purchase is independent of the ones before.

  3. 3

    Duplicates

    If you already have a sticker, it becomes a duplicate and goes on a pile. For now there is nobody to swap with.

  4. 4

    The question

    On average, how many stickers do you need to buy to fill the whole album?

Intuition says

"There are 50 slots, so a bit more than 50 stickers, maybe 70"

In fact it takes 225 stickers on average, 4.5 times the number of slots. The first half of the album fills fast, and every remaining empty slot costs more than the one before.

Model assumptions

  • All 50 stickers are equally common.
  • Purchases are independent of each other.
  • No swapping and no buying specific stickers.

Swapping, rare stickers and buying the last ones directly are covered in round 5.

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Don't count the slots. Think about how often a purchase is wasted once the album is nearly full.

  2. 2

    Split the collection into stages and wait for each new sticker separately. With k slots empty, a new sticker comes up with chance k/50, so you wait 50/k purchases on average.

  3. 3

    The last sticker costs 50 purchases on average, the one before it 25, the third from the end about 17. Continue this row down to the first sticker and estimate the total.

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why the last stickers cost so much

The key is that the chance of a new sticker keeps falling. When only one is missing, just one purchase in 50 is the right one. Here are five ways to see it.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

The last sticker

Half the album is cheap, the end is expensive

The first 25 stickers34purchases on average
The last 10 stickers146purchases on average

When one sticker is missing, each purchase has a 1 in 50 chance of being it. On average you wait 50 tries for that, as many as there are slots. That one sticker alone costs what the naive estimate gives for the whole album.

Where duplicates come from

The fuller the album, the more repeats

The chance that the next sticker is new equals the share of empty slots. When the album is 90% full, nine purchases out of ten are duplicates. Intuition counts as if almost every sticker were new, which is why it answers "a bit over 50". Out of 225 stickers bought, 175 are duplicates on average.

Harmonic numbers

The sum 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n grows like a logarithm

nHₙln n + 0.5772n·Hₙ

Add up all the stages and you get n · (1/n + 1/(n−1) + … + 1/1) = n · Hₙ. The number Hₙ is called a harmonic number. It grows very slowly, roughly like ln n + 0.5772. So you need a little more than n · ln n stickers.

For formula lovers

The exact formula, an approximation and the spread

The average number of purchases to fill the album: each stage waits for a new sticker, and the stage averages add up.

E[T]=nn+nn−1+nn−2+…+n1 =n·Hn

Hₙ is the sum of reciprocals from 1 to n. It is handy to approximate it with the natural logarithm.

Hn=1+12+13+…+1n ≈ln⁡n+γ+12n,γ≈0.5772

For an album with 50 slots.

n=50:50·H50=50·4.4992=224.96

The spread around the average: the variance and the standard deviation σ.

Var[T]=n2·(1+14+…+1n2)−n·Hn n=50:σ≈62

Stage k starts when the album already has k−1 stickers. Each purchase is then new with probability (n−k+1)/n, so the wait for one takes n/(n−k+1) tries on average. The stage averages simply add up. The spread is wide: for 50 slots half of all albums are done within 214 stickers, while about one in twenty needs more than 340.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1700s

An old problem

Abraham de Moivre studied the problem in the 18th century, and Pierre-Simon Laplace later on. In the 20th century George Pólya returned to it, as did Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi, who described exactly how the results spread around the average.

0.5772

The Euler–Mascheroni constant

The gap between the harmonic number Hₙ and ln n tends to the constant γ ≈ 0.5772. It has been known for centuries, yet nobody knows whether it is irrational.

34 / 191

"Almost done"

For a 50-slot album the first half of the stickers takes 34 purchases on average, and the second half 191. That's why collections stay "almost done" for so long: the last few slots eat most of the money.

128

The last sticker in a big album

In a 640-slot album the last sticker takes 640 purchases on average. That's 128 packs of 5 stickers for a single empty slot.

325

Two albums aren't twice the price

If two friends fill 50-slot albums together and pass each other their duplicates, the pair needs 325 stickers on average, not 450. This version is known as the double dixie cup problem.

n ln n

In computer science

The same estimate appears when you ask how many random tests it takes to hit each of n cases at least once, or how many random throws until each of n bins holds something. In every case the answer is about n · ln n.

The puzzle

Three short questions

The cognitive reflection test was published by the psychologist Shane Frederick in 2005. It has three problems. Each has a fast answer that comes to mind on its own and a right answer you have to work out. The maths is primary-school level. The test checks something else: whether you stop to check your first thought.

  1. 1

    The bat and ball

    A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much is the ball?

  2. 2

    Machines and widgets

    Five machines make five widgets in 5 minutes. How many minutes do 100 machines need to make 100 widgets?

  3. 3

    Lily pads on a lake

    A patch of lily pads on a lake doubles in size every day. It takes 48 days to cover the whole lake. How many days does it take to cover half of it?

  4. 4

    The rule

    Answer the way you normally would. There are no tricks in the wording: each question means exactly what it says.

Intuition says

"10 cents, 100 minutes, 24 days"

The right answers are 5 cents, 5 minutes and 47 days. The fast answers are not random. Each one fits the numbers in the question but not the question itself.

Assumptions

  • Prices are exact, with no tax or discounts.
  • All machines work in parallel at the same speed.
  • The lily patch doubles exactly once a day.

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why the fast answer feels right

The mistake is not in the arithmetic. The brain answers an easier question in place of the one asked and does not check the result. Here are five ways to see how that happens.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

Plug the answer back in

Why 10 cents is so convincing

The total $1.10 splits on its own into one dollar and 10 cents. The phrase "a dollar more" nudges you to take that split. But the difference between $1.00 and $0.10 is 90 cents, not a dollar. The fast answer fits the numbers but not the condition.

Ball 10¢ → bat $1.10$1.20total: 10¢ too much
Ball 5¢ → bat $1.05$1.10total: it adds up
Think in rates

One machine, one widget, 5 minutes

The pattern "5, 5, 5" suggests "100, 100, 100". But the question gives the rate of one machine: 5 machines make 5 widgets in 5 minutes, so each machine makes one widget in 5 minutes. The 100 machines work in parallel, and each makes its own widget in the same 5 minutes.

The time depends only on how many widgets each machine has to make. Here it is one widget, so 5 minutes.

Work backwards

Half the lake one day before the end

Intuition halves 48 because it is used to linear growth. But the patch doubles every day. So if the whole lake is covered on day 48, half was covered the day before. On day 24 the patch covered just 1/16,777,216 of the lake, less than one sixteen-millionth.

Two systems of thinking

System 1 answers first

System 1: fast, automatic10¢arrives on its own, no effort
System 2: slow, effortful5¢needs you to stop and compute

In Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman describes thinking as the work of two systems. System 1 produces an answer instantly. System 2 could check it, but it is "lazy" and often just agrees. The cognitive reflection test catches exactly that moment.

Rigoroustables, charts, formulas

“Rigorous” is hidden on all puzzles.

For formula lovers

Three equations, three answers

Bat and ball: let the ball cost x. The bat costs 1.00 more, and together they cost 1.10.

ball=x:x+(x+1.00)=1.10 2x=0.10→x=$0.05

Machines and widgets: first the rate of one machine, then the time for 100 machines and 100 widgets.

rate=55·5=0.2widgets per machine per minute t=100100·0.2=5 min

The lily pads: the area on day d is 2 to the power d − 48. Half the lake is where that power equals 1/2.

area(d)=2d−48 2d−48=12→d=47 area(24)=2−24≈0.000006%

Each equation takes one line and is easy to solve. The hard part is writing it down at all instead of going with the first thought. The general rule for the ball: if the total is T and the difference is D, the ball costs (T − D) / 2, and the fast answer T − D is always exactly twice that.

Habits that catch the trap

  1. Plug the answer back in. 10¢ and $1.10 make $1.20. You see the error in a second.
  2. Find the rate of one unit. One machine, one cat, one hour.
  3. Work backwards. What was there one step before the known result?
  4. Ask which problem you solved. Is it the one that was asked?
  5. Distrust an answer that came too easily. That is exactly when to check.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

2005

Frederick's paper

Shane Frederick described the test in "Cognitive Reflection and Decision Making", published in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2005. He compared test scores with how people make choices about money, risk and waiting.

3

A very short test

The whole test is three questions and takes a few minutes. It needs almost no knowledge. It measures the habit of stopping to check the answer that came to mind first.

MIT

Even at top universities

In Frederick's data, MIT students had the highest average score of the groups he tested. Yet there too, as at Harvard and Princeton, many students gave the fast wrong answers.

2011

Thinking, Fast and Slow

Daniel Kahneman discusses the bat and ball in his 2011 book as an example of System 2 failing to check System 1. Kahneman won the Nobel prize in economics in 2002. He borrowed the terms "System 1" and "System 2" from the psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West.

47

Lily pads and duckweed

The third question uses the same idea as the duckweed lake puzzle on this site: with daily doubling, the second half fills in the last day.

× 2

Always off by a factor of two

In any problem of the form "together T, one costs D more", the fast answer T − D is exactly twice the right answer (T − D) / 2. With a total of $100 and a difference of $99, the ball costs 50 cents, not a dollar.

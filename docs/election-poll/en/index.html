Election polls 30 million voters. How many should you ask?1,068 is enough.

Before an election, pollsters ask people how they will vote and forecast the result. In a country of 30 million voters, how many random people do you need to ask for the forecast to land within ±3 points of the true result 95% of the time? Lock in your estimate, then check it in a game, on a simulation of typical mistakes and with the explanations.

Before an election, pollsters ask people how they will vote and forecast the result. For the forecast to land within ±3 points 95% of the time, 1,068 random people are enough, and it hardly matters how many voters the country has: 50 thousand, 30 million or 244 million, as in the US. What matters is that the sample is truly random. Millions of answers from "convenient" people miss by more than a thousand random ones.

The puzzle

A country, two candidates and one poll

Asking everyone is impossible, so pollsters ask a sample and judge the whole country by it. The question is how big that sample has to be.

  1. 1

    The country

    The country has 30 million voters. Two candidates run, A and B. We do not know what share candidate A will get. That is what we want to forecast.

  2. 2

    A random sample

    We pick people at random from the list of all voters: everyone is equally likely to be asked. Everyone asked says honestly how they will vote.

  3. 3

    The precision

    A's share in the poll must be within ±3 percentage points of the true share, and this must hold in 95 polls out of 100. That is the standard most published polls work to.

  4. 4

    The question

    How many people do you need to ask?

Intuition says

"At least one percent of the population, so hundreds of thousands"

And also: "the US needs more than a small country". In fact 1,068 people are enough for a country of 30 million and for the US alike. A poll's precision depends on how many people are asked, not on what share of the population they are.

Where the trap is

  • The calculation works only if the sample is truly random.
  • Asking people who have a landline, people who happen to walk by, or replacing those who do not answer means the sample is no longer random.
  • A bigger sample does not fix this kind of mistake. Round 3 shows it.

Round 4 · Explanations

A spoonful of soup and a crooked sieve

The precision of a random sample depends only on its size. A biased sample also carries a bias, and it does not shrink however many people you ask.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

The idea · a spoonful of soup

A bigger pot does not need a bigger spoon

To check whether soup has enough salt, you taste one spoonful. The spoon is the same for a five-litre pot and a twenty-litre cauldron. Only one thing matters: the soup must be stirred well.

A poll works the same way. Each person asked is a spoonful of well-stirred soup: the chance of hitting an A voter equals A's share in the country, whether it has 50 thousand voters or 244 million. So precision depends on how many people are asked, not on the size of the country.

Bias

Noise goes away, bias stays

A poll's error has two parts: random noise and bias. The noise does fall like 1/√n. Bias appears when some people are more likely to get into the sample than others and their views differ. It does not depend on the sample size.

A landline poll in our country gives 45.4% instead of 52.0%. With 1,068 people the noise is ±3 points, so there is still some chance of a lucky hit. With a million the noise is ±0.1 points, and the forecast confidently shows the wrong number, to a tenth of a point.

Substitution

Why you must not replace those who did not answer

A pollster calls a random person, who does not pick up. The easy thing is to call the next one. After 1,068 interviews the report looks fine: a full sample, no refusals. But really it is a sample of people who pick up the phone, and those are more often older. Our forecast drops to 48.9%, and the wrong candidate comes out ahead.

Substitution does not remove the bias, it hides it. The share of people who did not answer, and who they were, was the only sign of trouble. After substitution it is gone, and there is nothing left to correct the bias with.

The lead

A 2-point lead is a tie

The ±3 applies to one candidate's share. The lead of A over B comes from the same interviews: when A's share is too high by chance, B's is too low by the same amount. So the lead's margin is almost twice as large: ±6 points with 1,068 people.

A headline saying "A leads B by 2 points" from a poll of a thousand really means "we do not know who is ahead".

Why √n

Four times the people, half the error

A's share in the poll scatters around the true share with a spread of √(p(1 − p)/n). The spread falls like 1/√n: to halve the error you must ask four times as many people. That is why both ends of any ranking are taken by the smallest samples: see the most dangerous equation.

When size does matter

Only if you ask a sizeable share

The country's size enters the formula only through the correction √((N − n)/(N − 1)). It matters when we ask a large share of the voters. In a village of 1,000, 517 are enough for ±3 points: once we have asked half the village, we know a lot about the other half.

The maths

Regions cost more than the country

A thousand people are enough for the whole country. But to know the result in each of 50 states with the same precision, each state needs its own thousand, about 50 thousand in all. That is why exit polls in big countries ask tens of thousands: what costs money is not the country but the detail.

The design effect accounts for the fact that polls are rarely a simple random sample. An exit poll first picks polling stations, then people at them. Neighbours vote alike, so 20 interviews at one station carry less information than 20 from all over the country. A design effect of 2 means the sample must be doubled.

A share near 50% gives the largest spread, p(1 − p) = 0.25. If a candidate polls at 10%, 385 people give the same margin. So planning "for 50%" is a safe margin.

The logic

Quality beats quantity

In 2018 the statistician Xiao-Li Meng showed that any poll's error splits into three factors: the correlation ρ between whether a person got into the sample and their answer; the spread of views in the country; and √((N − n)/n). For a random sample ρ is zero on average. For a biased one even ρ = 0.005, an almost invisible link, is multiplied by a huge √(N/n).

Hence his big data paradox: once the sample is not random, the size of the country matters again, and the bigger the country, the worse. Millions of answers with a small correlation can be worth a few hundred random people.

Practice

You can only fix what you measured

Real pollsters know a perfectly random sample does not exist: some people do not pick up, some refuse. So they weight the answers. If the sample has half as many young people as the country, each young person's answer counts twice. The shares of age groups, education levels and regions are known from the census.

Weighting by age fully removes the landline, street and substitution biases in our country, because there the chance of being sampled depends only on age. But it does not help when the supporters of one candidate are the ones who refuse to answer: they can be any age. And the fix has a price: if the sample holds only a few dozen young people, their answers get a big weight and the forecast scatters more.

When weighting cannot save them, everyone misses together. In Britain's 2015 election the Conservatives beat Labour by 6.6 points, while the final polls showed almost a tie, YouGov among them with 10,307 interviews. The British Polling Council's inquiry named unrepresentative samples, which weighting did not fix, as the main cause.

Exit poll

How an exit poll works

In the US the exit poll for ABC, CBS, CNN and NBC is run by Edison Research. In 2024 it chose 279 polling places as a probability sample. Interviewers at the exits approached every nth voter, about 75 interviews per place. Early and mail voters were reached by phone, text and email.

Sampling by polling place gives a larger error than a simple random sample: by the exit poll's own table it is ±15 points for 100 voters instead of ±10. This is the design effect from the calculator at work.

In 2004 the exit poll overstated John Kerry: across 1,460 precincts the margin shifted 6.5 points his way on average. The Edison/Mitofsky report put it down to Kerry voters being more willing to take part. Had 56% of Kerry voters and 50% of Bush voters agreed, that would explain the whole error. It is our "shy voters" trap, the other way round.

Not just elections

Where else a biased sample hides

  • Reviews and ratings. Reviews come from people the product either delighted or enraged. The average rating is not what all buyers think.
  • User surveys. An in-app survey is seen only by people still using the app. Those who left will not say why.
  • Online votes. "85% of our readers are against" means only the view of those who chose to click.
  • A/B tests. A test on a "convenient" slice of traffic, say working hours only, gives a precise answer for the wrong audience.
Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

2.4M

The biggest poll, the biggest miss

In 1936 the Literary Digest mailed 10 million ballots and got about 2.4 million back. Its forecast: Alf Landon would win. Franklin Roosevelt took 60.8% and 523 of 531 electoral votes. The magazine drew its addresses from telephone books, club rosters, directories and similar lists.

3,000

Gallup forecast the mistake too

George Gallup did not just name the right winner. By asking 3,000 people from the same kinds of lists, he forecast in advance that the Digest would give Roosevelt 44%. The magazine showed 43%. That is the account in Freedman, Pisani and Purves's textbook "Statistics".

1948

"Dewey Defeats Truman"

Gallup's final poll had Thomas Dewey on 49.5% and Harry Truman on 44.5%. It came out the other way: Truman 49.5%, Dewey 45.1%. The early edition of the Chicago Daily Tribune ran a headline declaring Dewey the winner. Polling had stopped well before election day, and interviewers chose whom to ask within their quotas.

400

2.3 million answers worth 400

Xiao-Li Meng worked it out for the 2016 US election: answers from 1% of the voters, about 2.3 million people, with a correlation between taking part and the vote of just −0.005 have the same error as a truly random sample of 400. The sample size shrinks by 99.98%.

6%

Who still picks up

According to the Pew Research Center, 36% of people answered a typical telephone survey in 1997, 9% in 2012 and only 6% in 2018. Even so, Pew found weighted phone surveys still give accurate results on most questions.

73.0%

Ukraine's 2019 exit poll

In the second round of Ukraine's presidential election, the National Exit Poll (the Democratic Initiatives Foundation, KIIS and the Razumkov Centre) asked 14,561 people at 300 polling stations using secret ballots. It gave Volodymyr Zelensky 73.0%, against the official 73.22%. Almost 30 million voters were on the lists.

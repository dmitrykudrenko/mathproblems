Hats in a line 100 people, black and white hats. How many can surely name their own?99 of 100.

100 people stand in a line, one behind another, all facing forward. Each gets a black or a white hat. Everyone sees all the hats in front of them, but not their own and not those behind. Starting from the back, each says one word aloud: "black" or "white". Those who name the colour of their own hat win. They may agree on a strategy in advance. How many can be guaranteed to win?

It seems you cannot beat "half": the back person tips off the one in front, and so on in pairs. But the back person can say one word about everyone at once: whether the number of black hats they see is even or odd. After that, every next person knows their own colour for sure. Only the back person takes a risk, so 99 are guaranteed to win, and 99.5 on average.

The puzzle

A hundred hats and a hundred words

A classic interview puzzle for programmers: William Poundstone includes it in his book on Google's interview puzzles (2012). Do not confuse it with the 100 prisoners and boxes: there people cannot pass anything to each other, while here everyone hears every word.

  1. 1

    The line

    100 people stand one behind another, all facing forward. Number them from the back: person 1 stands behind everyone, person 100 at the front.

  2. 2

    The hats

    Each person gets a black or a white hat at random. Any combination is possible: all black, all white, anything in between.

  3. 3

    Who sees what

    Everyone sees every hat in front of them. Not their own, and not those behind. Person 1 sees 99 hats, person 100 sees none.

  4. 4

    The words

    In turn, from person 1 to person 100, each says one word aloud: "black" or "white". Everyone hears everything. Those who name the colour of their own hat win.

  5. 5

    The question

    The people agree on a strategy in advance. How many of them can be guaranteed to win, whatever hats they get?

Intuition says

"50: split into pairs, and the back one names the colour of the one in front"

Some say "0", since nobody sees their own hat. In fact 99 are guaranteed to win. Only person 1 takes a risk, with a 1/2 chance. The trick is that their word carries information not about one neighbour but about the whole line.

Assumptions

  • You may say only "black" or "white". No tone of voice, no pauses, no gestures: otherwise the puzzle becomes dull.
  • Nobody turns around or sees their own hat, say in a mirror.
  • Everyone hears every word and counts without mistakes.
  • A guarantee means the worst case: the hat-maker may know the strategy and hand out hats so that as few people as possible win.

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why 99, not 50 and not 100

Person 1's word looks useless: they do not know their own hat. But all 99 people in front hear it, and that is exactly what makes it so valuable.

Step by step

Person 1 says whether the black hats are even or odd, the rest count

← backfront →

The agreement: "black" from person 1 means "I see an odd number of black hats", "white" means even. Person 2 sees the same hats except their own. If their count has a different parity, the difference is their own hat, so it is black. After that, everyone adds to what they see the "blacks" already said by the people behind them, since all of those people named their own colour correctly.

The upper bound

Nothing can save person 1

What person 1 knows about their hat0bits
Their chance with any strategy1/2for random hats

Person 1 speaks first, so they hear nothing and see only other people's hats. Their word depends on those alone. A hat-maker who knows the strategy checks what they will say and gives them the other colour. So 100 cannot be guaranteed, and the best possible average is 99 + 1/2 = 99.5.

Why pairs do worse

A tip for one neighbour is heard by a hundred people

Pairs: people sacrificed50each tip serves one person
Even/odd: people sacrificed1one tip serves everyone

In the pair strategy, the back person of each pair spends their word on a neighbour, even though everyone hears it. Each such tip carries one bit about one person. The word "even or odd" is also one bit, but about the sum of all the hats at once. Everyone in front knows every term of that sum except one, and that is enough.

Every word is true

What you hear works like what you see

  • Person 37 sees hats 38 to 100.
  • They do not see hats 2 to 36, but they heard their colours: people 2 to 36 named their own hats correctly.
  • Person 1 said the parity of the sum of hats 2 to 100.
  • Only one term is unknown: their own hat.

This is induction: person 2 surely knows their colour, so their word is true. Then person 3 knows everything about hats 2 and 4 to 100, and so on up to person 100, who sees no hats at all but knows their own.

The average

99.5 against 75 against 50

In pairs the back one wins with chance 1/2 and the front one always: 50 · 1/2 + 50 = 75. At random everyone wins with chance 1/2: 100 · 1/2 = 50. With even/odd, 99 always win and person 1 wins with chance 1/2.

Sub-theory · arithmetic mod 2

Even and odd: XOR

⊕01

a ⊕ b ⊕ b = a
white = 0, black = 1

Write white as 0 and black as 1. The parity of a sum equals the XOR of all digits: 1 ⊕ 1 = 0, since two ones make an even number. The key property: XOR cancels itself, b ⊕ b = 0. So you can "take out" the known hats from the XOR of all of them, and the unknown one remains: hj = s ⊕ (heard) ⊕ (seen).

The same works for k colours: number them 0, 1, …, k − 1 and take the sum modulo k, that is, the remainder after dividing by k. Person 1 names the colour whose number is "the sum of everyone they see, mod k". Again n − 1 are guaranteed to win.

For formula lovers

One checksum for the whole line

Colours are numbered 0, …, k − 1, and hi is person i's colour. Person 1 sees hats 2…n and names the colour s, their sum modulo k.

s≡h2+h3+…+hn(modk)

Person j heard s and the correct colours of people 2…j − 1, and sees hats j + 1…n. Only their own is unknown.

hj≡s−(∑heard+∑seen)(modk) hj≡s−∑i=2j−1hi−∑i=j+1nhi(modk)

Two colours: subtraction mod 2 is the same as addition, and the formula becomes a chain of XORs.

hj=s⊕h2⊕…⊕hj−1⊕hj+1⊕…⊕hn

Result: everyone except person 1 is guaranteed to win, and person 1 wins with chance 1/k.

guaranteed=n−1=99 on average=n−1+1k=99.5

For 100 people and two colours, that is 99 guaranteed winners and 99.5 on average. No strategy can do better: person 1 knows nothing about their own hat whatever the plan.

Round 5 · Scaling up

More colours, guessing at once, the parity bit

Hats in a line open the door to three worlds. In mathematics they lead to modular arithmetic and Hamming codes. In logic they show how one check bit protects all the others. In practice the same XOR rebuilds a lost disk and catches a wrong digit in a card number.

Mathematics

k colours: the sum modulo k

From parity to remainders

Any number of colours, and again n − 1

Number the colours 0, 1, …, k − 1. Person 1 adds up the numbers of all the hats they see, takes the remainder after dividing by k and names the colour with that number. Each next person subtracts what they heard and saw, and the remainder gives the number of their own colour.

The guarantee is the same: n − 1. But person 1's chance drops to 1/k, so the average is n − 1 + 1/k. For 100 people and three colours that is 99⅓. Remainders mod k form a group: every number has an opposite, and that is why the known terms can be subtracted.

Ebert's game: three guess at once and may pass

Covering codes

Put all the mistakes in one basket

It seems you cannot beat 1/2 here: each person guesses right with chance 1/2. But the rule "if I see two alike, I name the other colour, otherwise I pass" wins in 6 hat sets out of 8, a 3/4 chance. It loses only when all three hats match, and then all three are wrong together.

Each guess is right in one hat set and wrong in the neighbouring one where only that hat differs. So right and wrong guesses come in equal numbers. A win needs just one right guess, and one losing set can soak up as many as n wrong ones. Hence the bound: the chance is at most n/(n + 1). For n = 3 that is 3/4, and a full search over all 81³ = 531,441 combinations of rules confirms that nothing does better.

For n = 2m − 1 the bound is reached by the Hamming code, the same one as in the poisoned wine puzzle: 7 players win in 112 of 128 hat sets (7/8), 15 players in 30,720 of 32,768 (15/16). Each player checks whether the hats could form a codeword, and if so names the colour that breaks it.

Logic

Someone slips: what does it cost?

10 people, the even/odd strategy. Click a person, and they will say the opposite of what they worked out. You can pick several.

One check bit

One check bit protects all the others

Person 1's word says nothing about person 1. It is a check bit: the parity of all the other hats. If you know the sum and all the terms but one, that one can be recovered. The rest of the strategy is this thought applied 99 times.

A curious consequence: one slip does not bring the line down. If person j in the middle of the line names the wrong colour, person j + 1 is wrong too, but their wrong word cancels the first mistake, and everyone after is right again. One mistake costs exactly two people. Check it with a click.

Practice

RAID 5: XOR rebuilds a lost disk

One stripe of the array: bytes from data disks A, B, C and the parity byte P = A ⊕ B ⊕ C. In RAID 5 the parity of different stripes sits on different disks, but the arithmetic is the same. Choose the disk that "burns out".

The parity bit

Person 1 works as a parity disk

The simplest data protection is a parity bit: one more bit is added to a group so that the number of ones becomes even. If one bit gets corrupted, the parity no longer matches and the error shows. This is how characters on serial links and memory in old computers were checked.

RAID 5 goes one step further. The failed disk is known: like the person in the line who knows it is their own hat that is unknown. Then parity does not just catch an error, it restores what was lost: B = A ⊕ C ⊕ P. The array survives the loss of any one disk while spending a single disk on protection, not a mirror copy of each.

The Luhn algorithm: a card's check digit

A sum modulo 10

A card number's last digit means nothing but a check

The last digit of a bank card number is a check digit, like person 1's word. It is chosen so that a special sum of all the digits is divisible by 10. Going from right to left, every second digit is doubled, and if the result exceeds 9, subtract 9. For the standard example 79927398713 the sum is 70, so the number is valid.

It is a sum modulo 10, like the sum of colours modulo k. It catches any single wrong digit and almost any swap of two neighbouring digits. It misses only 09 ↔ 90. The IBM engineer Hans Peter Luhn patented the algorithm, and card numbers are still checked with it, in payment forms for example, before the bank is even contacted.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1998

Hats from a thesis

The game in which players guess at once and may pass was described by Todd Ebert in his PhD thesis (University of California, Santa Barbara, 1998). It spread when Ebert, by then teaching computer science at UC Irvine, offered his students extra credit for a seven-player version, the "seven prisoners puzzle".

2001

Hats in The New York Times

On 10 April 2001 Sara Robinson wrote a New York Times article, "Why Mathematicians Now Care About Their Hat Color", about how Ebert's puzzle unexpectedly led to coding theory.

2002

Hats and covers

Hendrik Lenstra and Gadiel Seroussi, in the paper "On Hats and Other Covers" (IEEE ISIT, Lausanne, July 2002), linked the best strategies of the game to covering codes: sets from which any hat set can be reached by changing at most one hat.

1960

A patent on a check digit

Hans Peter Luhn of IBM filed for a "Computer for Verifying Numbers" in 1954 and received US patent 2,950,048 in 1960. The device checked numbers with the very algorithm used to check card numbers today.

1988

RAID is born

David Patterson, Garth Gibson and Randy Katz at Berkeley published "A Case for Redundant Arrays of Inexpensive Disks (RAID)". It describes several RAID levels, including RAID 5, where the parity blocks are spread across all the disks.

∞

An infinite line

If there are infinitely many people and each sees everyone in front, then with the axiom of choice there is a strategy under which only finitely many people are wrong. Gabay and O'Connor, graduate students at Cornell, found it in 2004. Christopher Hardin and Alan Taylor cover this and other strange versions in their book "The Mathematics of Coordinated Inference" (2013).

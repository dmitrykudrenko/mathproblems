The puzzle

Picture this: you've had a test

This puzzle is known as the false positive paradox, and the mistake intuition makes here is called the base rate fallacy. We look at how accurate the test is and forget how rare the disease is. Bayes' theorem shows how to work it out correctly.

  1. 1

    The disease

    1% of people have the disease, one person in 100. It has no visible symptoms.

  2. 2

    The test

    If a person is ill, the test says "positive" 99% of the time. That's the sensitivity. If a person is healthy, it says "negative" 99% of the time. That's the specificity. So only 1% of healthy people get a false positive.

  3. 3

    You

    You're tested during a routine check-up, with no complaints. So you're a random member of the population.

  4. 4

    The result

    The test is positive. What is the chance you're really ill?

Intuition says

"The test is 99% accurate, so I'm 99% likely to be ill"

In fact the chance is 50%. For every sick person who tests positive there is one healthy person with a false positive. Healthy people outnumber the sick 99 to 1, so even 1% of them adds up to as many positives as all the sick people together.

Model assumptions

  • The share of sick people, the base rate, is 1%.
  • Sensitivity 99%: the test finds 99 of 100 sick people.
  • Specificity 99%: 99 of 100 healthy people test negative.
  • You were picked at random, not because of symptoms.
  • Repeat tests err independently of each other.

The numbers are illustrative. Real tests vary in accuracy, and doctors also weigh symptoms and other results. Round 5 shows how that changes the answer.

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why an accurate test gives only 50%

The test answers "how likely is a positive result if a person is ill?". You want the reverse: "how likely am I to be ill if my result is positive?". These are different probabilities, and the base rate connects them.

Natural frequencies

Count people, not percentages

Ill people who test positive99of 100 ill
Healthy people who test positive99of 9,900 healthy

Take 10,000 people. 100 of them are ill, and the test finds 99. 9,900 are healthy, and 1% of them, 99 people, get a false positive. That's 198 positives in total, 99 of them ill. Exactly half.

The base rate

The rarer the disease, the more false alarms

The test is always the same here: 99% sensitivity and specificity.

Probability tree

Two branches, the same number of positives

The left branch is thin, since only 1% are ill, but the test almost always fires on it. The right branch is thick, since 99% are healthy, and even a rare error there yields as many positives.

Bayes' theorem

The formula behind it all

Bayes' theorem: the probability of disease given a positive test. The denominator is the share of all positive results.

P(S|+)=P(+|S)·P(S)P(+|S)·P(S)+P(+|H)·P(H)

Sensitivity and specificity 99%, 1% are ill.

=0.99·0.010.99·0.01+0.01·0.99 =0.00990.0198=50%

A second positive test: the previous answer, 50%, becomes the new base rate.

2 positive tests:0.99·0.50.99·0.5+0.01·0.5=99%

A rare disease: 1 ill person in 10,000.

1 ill in 10,000: 0.99·0.00010.99·0.0001+0.01·0.9999≈0.98%

S means sick, H means healthy. P(+ | S) is the test's sensitivity, P(+ | H) is 1 minus the specificity, and P(S) is the base rate. The denominator is the share of all positive results. When the disease is rare, the second term, the false positives, dominates it. For a second test, the previous answer, 50%, becomes the new base rate.

Odds and the likelihood ratio

A positive test multiplies the odds by 99

The odds of disease before the test: 1 ill person for every 99 healthy ones.

prior odds=1:99

How many times more likely a positive result is in an ill person than in a healthy one.

likelihood ratio=99%1%=99

A positive test multiplies the odds by 99.

posterior odds=1·99:99 =1:1→50%

A second positive test multiplies the odds by 99 once more.

after a second test=1·99:1 =99:1→99%

A positive result is 99 times more likely in an ill person than in a healthy one. So it multiplies the odds of disease by 99. But the starting odds were 1 to 99, and multiplying by 99 gives just 1 to 1. If 1 person in 10,000 has the disease, odds of 1 : 9,999 become 99 : 9,999, roughly 1 : 101.

Retests, rare diseases, spam and fraud

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

95%

The Harvard question for doctors

In 1978 Casscells, Schoenberger and Graboys put this question to 60 students and doctors at Harvard Medical School: a disease affects 1 person in 1,000, and the test has a 5% false positive rate. What is the chance that a person who tests positive is ill? The most common answer was 95%. Fewer than one in five gave the right answer, about 2%.

99 of 100

Natural frequencies help

The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer showed that people, doctors included, get the answer right far more often when the same problem is stated in counts of people ("99 of 100 ill people") instead of percentages and conditional probabilities. That's why round 2 shows a crowd, not a formula.

1 : 73 m

The Sally Clark case

In 1999 in the UK, Sally Clark was convicted over the deaths of her two baby sons. A paediatrician told the court that the chance of two sudden infant deaths in one family was 1 in 73 million. The figure came from squaring the chance of one such death, as if the two deaths were independent. On top of that, the rarity of the event was confused with the probability of innocence. This is the prosecutor's fallacy, a close relative of the base rate fallacy. The conviction was overturned in 2003.

1763

An essay published after his death

The English minister and mathematician Thomas Bayes died in 1761. His friend Richard Price found his work on inverse probability among his papers and presented it to the Royal Society. The essay was published in 1763. Pierre-Simon Laplace later gave the formula its familiar form.

2002

Bayesian spam filters

In 2002 the programmer Paul Graham published the essay "A Plan for Spam", proposing to filter mail with Bayes' theorem: every word in a message shifts the chance that it's spam. The idea caught on quickly. A filter faces the same problem as a medical test: the result depends on what share of all mail is spam in the first place.

41%

The taxicab problem

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave people this problem. In a city, 85% of cabs are green and 15% are blue. A witness to a night-time accident says the cab was blue, and he identifies colours correctly 80% of the time. The most common answer is that the cab is blue with probability 80%. The truth is about 41%: green cabs are so much more common that the witness mistakes a green cab more often than he correctly sees a blue one.

Glossary / Absolute and relative risk

Glossary · absolute and relative risk

A pill halves the risk. How many people does it help?

"Cuts the risk by 50%" sounds like rescue for half of us. But if two in a thousand fall ill without the pill and one with it, a thousand people swallow it and it helps one. Both descriptions are true. The first is relative risk: how many times the danger changed. The second is absolute: how many people it actually touches. Headlines love the first; decisions should rest on the second.

Pills that "double the risk"

October 1995, Britain. The Committee on Safety of Medicines sends an urgent letter to 190,000 doctors, pharmacists and public health directors and makes an emergency announcement to the media: third-generation contraceptive pills double, that is, raise by 100%, the risk of blood clots in the legs and lungs compared with the previous generation of pills.

Take 7,000 women on the new pills. How many of them get a clot precisely because their pills are new rather than old?

One extra clot: in one woman out of 7,000.

would have had a clot on the old pills toothe extra clot due to the new pills

The studies behind the warning had found that about one woman in 7,000 on the old pills had a clot, and two on the new ones. The risk really did double: +100% relative risk. But the absolute risk rose by 1 in 7,000, less than two hundredths of a percent. Try to find those two dots among the seven thousand.

That did not calm frightened women: many simply stopped taking the pill. By one estimate, England and Wales saw about 13,000 more abortions the following year, and about 800 extra conceptions among girls under 16. The irony is that pregnancy and abortion themselves raise the risk of clots more than the new pills did. A later analysis by Del Bono, Francesconi and Best estimated that in the five months after the warning, conception rates rose by more than 7% and abortion rates by more than 9%.

Sources: G. Gigerenzer et al., "Helping Doctors and Patients Make Sense of Health Statistics", Psychological Science in the Public Interest 8(2), 2008; A. Furedi, "The public health implications of the 1995 ‘pill scare’", Human Reproduction Update 5(6), 1999; E. Del Bono, M. Francesconi, N. Best, ISER Working Paper 2011-16.

What absolute and relative risk are

Absolute risk

The share of people who meet the event over some period: fall ill, get a side effect, buy. It is said as "so many in a thousand" or as a percentage.

without: 2 in 1,000 = 0.2% · with: 1 in 1,000 = 0.1%
absolute difference: 0.2% − 0.1% = 0.1 percentage points

Relative risk

The ratio of two absolute risks: how many times bigger or smaller the risk is in one group than in another. The number of people cancels out of it; only "so many times" is left.

0.1% ÷ 0.2% = 0.5 → the risk is halved, "down 50%"

A third number joins the two and best answers "so what does it mean for me?": the number needed to treat (NNT), how many people must take the treatment for one of them to avoid the illness. It is one divided by the absolute difference: 1 ÷ 0.001 = 1,000. If the treatment raises the risk, the same number is called the number needed to harm (NNH).

The same "minus 50%", different worlds

Relative risk doesn't know where you started. The treatment is the same in every row: it halves the risk, a 50% cut. Yet its benefit differs by a factor of hundreds.

Benefit in percent, harm in pieces

The slyest trick is to give the benefit as a relative number and the harm as an absolute one. "The drug cuts the risk of cancer by 48%, and clots occur in only 1% of those who take it" sounds like an obvious yes. Here is a real drug facts box for tamoxifen, from a trial of 13,000 women at high risk of breast cancer, over five years:

PlaceboDrugChangePer 1,000
Invasive breast cancer3317−48%16 fewer
Clot in a leg or lung510+100%5 more

Now both rows are in the same units. "Minus 48%" and "plus 100%" push in different directions, while 16 and 5 per thousand can honestly go on the same scales: for roughly every 63 women taking the drug, one fewer cancer; for every 200, one more clot. Which matters more is for the person to decide with a doctor. But you can only decide when both numbers are absolute.

Source: the "Prescription drug facts" box by L. Schwartz, S. Woloshin and H. G. Welch (2007), reproduced in G. Gigerenzer et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest 8(2), 2008. Relative numbers and NNT are computed from it.

When to use which

The rule: relative risk tells how strong an effect is; absolute risk tells how much it matters to you.

  • Whether to take a drug or a vaccineAbsolute risk and NNT, for the benefit and the side effects separately. "By 50%" without the starting risk says nothing.
  • Whether an effect is strong in itselfRelative. It is often similar across groups with different starting risks, which is why studies compare treatments with it.
  • A headline saying "twice as dangerous"Ask "out of how many?". Doubling a one-in-a-million risk gives one in five hundred thousand.
  • An A/B test in marketing"Conversion +20%" is a relative change. From 5% to 6% it is just one point, that is, 10 extra purchases per thousand visitors.
  • Budgets and planningAbsolute. A hospital, an insurer or a warehouse needs to know how many cases there will be, not by what factor they grew.
  • Benefit against harmBoth in the same units, best in people per thousand. Mixed units are a sign someone is persuading you.

Three more strange consequences

  • Minus 50% and plus 1% are the same factA new operation lowers mortality from 2% to 1%. Deaths fell by 50%. But survivors went from 98% to 99%, only 1% more. A relative change depends on what you count: deaths or lives.
  • Relative risk has a ceilingIf an event already happens to 60% of people, it cannot become three times as common: there is no more than 100%. The largest possible rise is 1/0.6 ≈ 1.67 times. Loud "five times" claims only happen with rare events.
  • The rarer the event, the louder the percentageFrom one case in a thousand to two is +100%. The same "+1 in a thousand" on top of 300 cases is only +0.3%. Relative numbers inflate small risks and hide changes in big ones.

The formulas

Let p₀ be the risk without the treatment and p₁ the risk with it (shares from 0 to 1).

relative risk RR = p₁ / p₀ · relative risk reduction RRR = 1 − RR

absolute risk reduction ARR = p₀ − p₁ = RRR · p₀ · NNT = 1 / ARR = 1 / (RRR · p₀)

The last formula is the whole paradox: the benefit to one person is the strength of the treatment times the starting risk. A headline reports only the first factor. For the current slider position above: RR = –, ARR = –, NNT = –.

The odds ratio is not the relative risk

Medical papers often show one more number, the odds ratio (OR). Odds are p / (1 − p), "how many cases per non-case". For rare events the OR almost equals the relative risk: from 2 to 1 in a thousand, OR = 0.4995 against RR = 0.5. For common events they part ways. If the risk falls from 40% to 20%, the risk has halved (RR = 0.5), but the odds went from 0.4/0.6 to 0.2/0.8, so OR = 0.375. Anyone who reads the OR as a relative risk sees "minus 62.5%" instead of "minus 50%".

A little history

The number needed to treat was proposed in 1988 by Andreas Laupacis, David Sackett and Robin Roberts in the New England Journal of Medicine, as a measure clearer to doctors than relative risk reduction. Gerd Gigerenzer, who made the 1995 British story a textbook case, points out it was not the first: similar pill scares happened in 1970 and 1977, and after each one the number of abortions rose. His advice to a news reader is short: whenever you hear "so many times", ask "how many in a thousand?".

Where it shows up

The false positive paradox

1% of people have a disease. The test is 99% accurate, and you test positive. What is the chance you're really ill?

The most dangerous equation

Disease is rarest in quiet rural counties. Clean air? Or something else?

The prosecutor's fallacy

Blood at a burglary matches the defendant. Such a match occurs in one adult in 10,000, and the city has 500,000 adults. How likely is he to be innocent?

The A/A test: two identical pages

The same page is shown as "A" and as "B", converting at 5%, with 1,000 visitors each. How often will one of two identical pages "win" by 20% or more?

Who gets the coupon?

A perfect model finds the 250 likeliest buyers, and they get discount coupons. How many purchases does that add?

The potato paradox

100 kg of potatoes are 99% water. Overnight some of the water evaporates, and now they are 98% water. How much do the potatoes weigh?

Is ice cream dangerous for swimmers?

On days when more ice cream is sold, more people drown. The link is real and shows up in the data. But ice cream has nothing to do with it: the heat pushes both. Correlation can show that two things move together, and cannot tell you why at all.

One number from −1 to +1

Correlation is written r. It shows how closely points line up along a straight line. The sign gives the direction, the size how tight the link is.

−1a perfect line going down
−0.5clearly "the more of one, the less of the other"
0no straight-line link
+0.5clearly "the more, the more"
+1a perfect line going up

Two more traps

Correlation is a useful number, but it fools people easily. Here are two more ways the data can say one thing and r another.

When to trust a correlation

  • It doesn't say what causes whatIce cream doesn't drown people. Look for a third variable pushing both, or test it with an experiment.
  • It only sees straight linesr ≈ 0 means "no straight-line link", not "no link". The parabola in the traps depends on x completely, and r is almost zero.
  • Look at the points, not just the numberOne far-off point can turn a cloud into a "strong link". A plot saves you from this in a second.
  • r = 0.5 doesn't mean "half the time"It is neither a percentage nor a probability. The share of variation explained by the straight-line link is r², just 25%.
Deepergeneralisations, theory, history

“Deeper” is hidden on all puzzles.

The formula

r = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / √( Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² · Σ (yᵢ − ȳ)² )

The numerator adds up products of deviations from the means. A point top right or bottom left adds a plus, bottom right or top left a minus. The denominator only scales the sum so that r doesn't depend on units and stays between −1 and +1. This also shows the weak spot: one far-off point gives a huge product and can outweigh all the rest.

Why the parabola has r = 0

cov(x, x²) = E[x³] − E[x] · E[x²] = 0 − 0 · E[x²] = 0

If the x values are spread symmetrically around zero, then E[x] = 0 and E[x³] = 0: every cube on the right has an equal negative one on the left. So the numerator is zero, even though y is completely determined by x. Correlation measures only the straight-line part of a link.

How to "remove" the heat with a formula

rxy·z = (rxy − rxz · ryz) / √( (1 − rxz²)(1 − ryz²) )

This is the partial correlation: the link between ice cream x and drownings y when the temperature z is held fixed. In the first simulation ice cream correlates with temperature at –, drownings with temperature at –. Their product – almost equals the correlation of ice cream with drownings, –. So the temperature explains nearly the whole link, and the partial correlation is –. It is the same result as the "only days at 28–32°" button, without throwing any data away.

More on “Data and experiments”

  • Sampling bias

    A sample some people get into more easily than others, so it describes everyone badly.

    In 1936 a magazine polled millions of Americans and still picked the wrong election winner.

  • Law of large numbers

    The more repetitions, the closer the share of an outcome gets to its true probability.

    The share of heads creeps towards 50%, while the gap between heads and tails in coins usually grows.

  • Standard error

    How far an average or a rate computed from a sample usually misses the truth: σ divided by the square root of the sample size.

    Halving the error takes four times as much data.

  • Confidence interval

    A range around a measured number, built by a method that covers the true value in a chosen share of samples, such as 95%.

    Of twenty honest 95% intervals, on average one misses the truth, and none of them knows whether it is the one.

  • p-value

    How often chance alone would produce a gap at least as big as yours if there were really no difference.

    When there is no effect at all, p below 0.05 turns up as often as p between 0.50 and 0.55.

  • Chance to beat

    The number on Bayesian dashboards: how likely B is to be better than A, given the data and a starting assumption.

    "97% chance" tells the truth only when every idea of yours changes something; if four in five are useless, B is better in only a third of such tests.

  • Statistical power

    The probability that a test notices a real effect of a given size. A power of 80% means the test misses one working effect in five.

    A weak A/B test that does find an effect almost always exaggerates it: a real +10% shows up on the dashboard as +24%.

Glossary / Bootstrap

Glossary · bootstrap

Rerun the survey without asking anyone new

You have 50 orders and you have computed the average order value. How far can you trust it? The honest answer is to collect a thousand more such weeks and watch how the number jumps. The bootstrap does this without a single new order: a thousand times it draws 50 orders from your own 50, putting each one back. Every such copy loses about a third of your data and repeats some of the rest. And yet the spread of these "damaged" copies is almost the same as that of real new weeks.

How many orders get lost

A resample works like this: 50 times we pick one of your 50 orders at random, write it down and put it back. So the same order can come up twice or three times, and another one not at all.

What share of your 50 orders usually doesn't make it into a resample even once?

36.4% of the orders go missing, usually about 18 of 50. For large samples the share tends to 1/e ≈ 36.8%.

Notice that the dark pile sits around your week, not around the truth. The bootstrap doesn't tell you where the true average is. It tells you how far your number could have jumped, and that is exactly what the "±" in a report and a confidence interval need. For a median or a return rate there is no handy formula at all, so the bootstrap is often the only simple way to get the error.

Why damaged copies don't lie

An explanation without formulas, slightly simplified. A new week in the real shop means 50 new customers drawn at random from everyone. You can't visit everyone, but your 50 orders are the best portrait of the shop you have: small and large orders appear there in roughly the right proportion. A resample "visits" this portrait the way you would visit the shop. That some orders are missing and others repeat is not a flaw but the whole point: a new week would also miss some customers and bring several similar ones. That randomness is the very error we are after.

So the bootstrap is only as good as your portrait. If not a single big client happened to land among your 50 orders, the resamples won't learn about them either. And if the sample is biased, say you only surveyed people who answer the phone, the bootstrap will carefully measure the error of a biased number.

What the bootstrap is

The bootstrap is a way to estimate the error of any number computed from a sample, without a formula: you repeatedly draw a new sample of the same size from the sample itself, with replacement, and watch how the result changes. The steps:

  1. You have n values and a number computed from them: a mean, a median, a rate, the difference between two groups.
  2. Draw n values at random with replacement and compute the same number.
  3. Repeat 1,000–10,000 times.
  4. The standard deviation of these numbers is the bootstrap estimate of the standard error. The range between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles is a simple 95% confidence interval.

Share left out: (1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 0,368  ·  for the mean: SE* = σ̂ / √n = √((n − 1)/n) · s / √n

Each order is missed on a single draw with probability 1 − 1/n, and over n draws with probability (1 − 1/n)ⁿ. For the mean, the bootstrap reproduces the known formula almost exactly: the spread of the resamples equals σ̂ / √n, where σ̂ is the spread of your n values. It differs from the usual s / √n by a factor of √((n − 1)/n), which is 0.99 for 50 orders. In other words, the bootstrap "invented" the standard error formula on its own, knowing nothing about it.

Sample nLeft outDistinct valuesRecord kept

"Left out" is the share of values missing from a resample; "Distinct values" is how many different values remain in it; "Record kept" is the chance that the sample's largest value made it into the resample.

When to use what

  • A mean or a rate, plenty of dataThe formula σ / √n is enough: the bootstrap gives the same, only slower. It is useful as a check that you got the formula right.
  • Median, percentile, ratioThe median order, the 90th percentile of load time, revenue per user, the difference of medians in an A/B test. Either there is no formula or it is complicated and fragile. Here the bootstrap is the first choice.
  • Resample people, not sessionsIf one person contributes several rows of data, you draw the person with all their rows. Otherwise the resamples come out too calm and the error is understated. For time series, likewise, you draw whole weeks or blocks of days.
  • A small, skewed sampleWith 50 orders from this shop, the ±2 bootstrap-error interval covers the truth in about 92% of cases, not 95%. The smaller the sample and the longer the tail of big orders, the more optimistic the bootstrap. It is no substitute for more data.
  • How many resamplesFor the error itself 1,000 is enough: the error of the error estimate is then about 2%. For the edge of an interval, the 2.5th percentile, 10,000 is better.

Where the bootstrap goes blind: records

Switch the simulation to "Largest order". The dark pile turns into a single bar: in 63.6% of resamples the largest order is the same as in your week, because your record makes it into a resample with probability 1 − (49/50)⁵⁰. A resample can never go above it: it only knows the numbers you have already seen. Yet the real record of a new week beats yours in exactly half of cases: which of two weeks holds the bigger record is down to chance. In a typical week the spread of the resamples for the record is about three times smaller than the real one.

The same goes for anything that depends on the edges: the maximum, the minimum, the rarest event. The bootstrap works for numbers that rest on many values at once, like the mean or the median, and fails for those that rest on a single one.

Where the name comes from

  • Efron, 1979The method was described by the Stanford statistician Bradley Efron in "Bootstrap methods: another look at the jackknife" (The Annals of Statistics, 1979). The name comes from the idiom "to pull yourself up by your bootstraps": getting something that seemingly can't be had from anywhere.
  • The jackknifeThe bootstrap's forerunner is the jackknife of Quenouille (1949) and Tukey (1958): drop one value at a time from the sample and watch how the result changes. The bootstrap generalised the idea, but it needed computers, which were just becoming available in the 1970s.
  • The prizeIn November 2018 Efron was awarded the International Prize in Statistics for the bootstrap; it was presented at the 2019 World Statistics Congress.
  • The same 37% in machine learningLeo Breiman's bagging (1996) and random forests (2001) train each tree on a resample. The roughly 37% of the data left out of it becomes a ready-made test set for that tree.

More on “Data and experiments”

  • Sampling bias

    A sample some people get into more easily than others, so it describes everyone badly.

    In 1936 a magazine polled millions of Americans and still picked the wrong election winner.

  • Law of large numbers

    The more repetitions, the closer the share of an outcome gets to its true probability.

    The share of heads creeps towards 50%, while the gap between heads and tails in coins usually grows.

  • Correlation

    A number from −1 to +1 showing how closely two quantities move together.

    In summer both ice cream sales and drownings go up. Ice cream isn't to blame: the heat drives both.

  • Standard error

    How far an average or a rate computed from a sample usually misses the truth: σ divided by the square root of the sample size.

    Halving the error takes four times as much data.

  • Confidence interval

    A range around a measured number, built by a method that covers the true value in a chosen share of samples, such as 95%.

    Of twenty honest 95% intervals, on average one misses the truth, and none of them knows whether it is the one.

  • p-value

    How often chance alone would produce a gap at least as big as yours if there were really no difference.

    When there is no effect at all, p below 0.05 turns up as often as p between 0.50 and 0.55.

  • Chance to beat

    The number on Bayesian dashboards: how likely B is to be better than A, given the data and a starting assumption.

    "97% chance" tells the truth only when every idea of yours changes something; if four in five are useless, B is better in only a third of such tests.

  • Statistical power

    The probability that a test notices a real effect of a given size. A power of 80% means the test misses one working effect in five.

    A weak A/B test that does find an effect almost always exaggerates it: a real +10% shows up on the dashboard as +24%.

  • Minimum detectable effect (MDE)

    The smallest real effect a test of a given size will find with 80% probability (usually at a 5% significance level). Smaller effects the test will mostly miss.

    A shop with 1,000 visitors a day would need seven years of testing to reliably notice a +2% lift in conversion.

  • Holdout group

    A small random share of customers who for a long time see none of the shipped changes, so that comparing with them shows what all the changes gave together.

    Twenty changes a year, each one winning its A/B test, almost +50% together, yet customers who saw none of them buy only about 7% less.

  • Goodhart's law

    When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure: people lift the number itself, not the result it was meant to show.

    In 1902 Hanoi paid for rat tails: 20,114 came in on 12 June alone, and a health inspector found a rat farm.

Glossary / Goodhart's law

Glossary · Goodhart's law

We pay for rat tails. What could go wrong?

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. People optimise what is counted, not what the count was for. So a bonus on a number often grows the number itself, not the result it was meant to show.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

Hanoi, 1902

The French colonial authorities in Hanoi are fighting rats and announce a bounty for the city's residents: bring a rat's tail as proof and get paid. What happened next?

More and more tails came in: 7,442 on 19 May, and by 12 June 20,114 in a single day. But the rats did not go away, and a health inspector found a rat farm: animals were bred and their tails cut off to be handed in for the bounty. Historian Michael Vann, who studied the archives, calls the campaign an absolute disaster.

A bonus on a metric

The same at the office. A worker has 10 hours. An hour of real work gives one unit of result and one point in the report. An hour of "playing the metric" gives three points in the report and no result, but the worker dislikes it: the more such hours, the higher the risk and the guilt. You pay a bonus for every point in the report. Move the slider.

A toy model: the worker picks the hours of play g that maximise the bonus for points minus g² (the price of risk). Hence g equals the bonus per point, metric = 10 + 2g, result = 10 − g.

Without a bonus the report and the result agree: 10 and 10. The more generous the bonus on the number, the higher it climbs, up to double, and the less stands behind it: at a bonus of 5 the real result halves. The report has stopped showing what it was kept for.

What the law says

A metric is useful while nobody leans on it: then it honestly reflects what is going on. Once money, grades or a ranking depend on it, people find ways to lift the number itself, and the link between the number and the work weakens or vanishes. So Goodhart's law is not about dishonest people; it is about how incentives work.

Where it shows up

  • A bank and sales targetsIn 2016 the CFPB fined Wells Fargo 100 million dollars: staff, "spurred by sales targets and compensation incentives", opened more than 2 million deposit and card accounts that customers may not have authorised.
  • Votes on Product HuntOn launch day offers arrive: "100 upvotes and comments for 60 dollars". That is why Product Hunt counts points rather than votes, cleans out suspicious ones and, under its rules, removes products whose makers pay for upvotes.
  • A/B testsWhen a team is judged by the number of "winning" tests, there is a temptation to peek and stop at the first green number, or to slice the result into segments until something wins.
  • Customer supportA bonus for the number of closed tickets rewards closing them fast rather than solving the problem: the ticket is closed, and the customer writes again.

How to guard against it

  • Several metrics that pull different waysClosed tickets together with repeat tickets, sales together with returns. One number is easy to game; two that pull against each other are much harder.
  • Measure the outcome, not the actionNot "emails sent" but the sales they bring; not "votes" but sign-ups and feedback.
  • Spot-checkRegularly look at a few cases by hand: what really stands behind the number. In Hanoi it was an inspector who found the farm, not the report.
  • Don't tie money to one numberThe more a bonus depends on a metric, the stronger the pressure on it, as in the simulation above.

Where the wording comes from

Economist Charles Goodhart wrote in 1975 about UK monetary policy:

"Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."

The Bank of England was trying to steer the economy through money-supply figures, and the figures stopped behaving as they used to.

The well-known short form, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", is not Goodhart's. It was given by anthropologist Marilyn Strathern in 1997, in a paper on rating British universities, paraphrasing Keith Hoskin.

Where it shows up

Slice it until something wins

The new email is no better than the old one. The analyst checks the result in 20 countries. What is the chance at least one shows a "significant" difference?

Peeking at an A/B test

Two identical pages, a 30-day test, a check every evening and a stop at the first "95%". How often is one of the identical pages declared the winner?

Who gets the coupon?

A perfect model finds the 250 likeliest buyers, and they get discount coupons. How many purchases does that add?

Survivorship bias: Wald's planes

Bombers come back with holes in the fuselage and wings, and hardly any on the engines. Where should the armour go?

Axelrod's tournament

Programs play the prisoner's dilemma for 200 rounds, each against each. Who scores most: an eternal defector, a sneak, Tit for Tat or a coin toss?

More on “Data and experiments”

  • Sampling bias

    A sample some people get into more easily than others, so it describes everyone badly.

    In 1936 a magazine polled millions of Americans and still picked the wrong election winner.

  • Law of large numbers

    The more repetitions, the closer the share of an outcome gets to its true probability.

    The share of heads creeps towards 50%, while the gap between heads and tails in coins usually grows.

  • Correlation

    A number from −1 to +1 showing how closely two quantities move together.

    In summer both ice cream sales and drownings go up. Ice cream isn't to blame: the heat drives both.

  • Standard error

    How far an average or a rate computed from a sample usually misses the truth: σ divided by the square root of the sample size.

    Halving the error takes four times as much data.

  • Confidence interval

    A range around a measured number, built by a method that covers the true value in a chosen share of samples, such as 95%.

    Of twenty honest 95% intervals, on average one misses the truth, and none of them knows whether it is the one.

  • p-value

    How often chance alone would produce a gap at least as big as yours if there were really no difference.

    When there is no effect at all, p below 0.05 turns up as often as p between 0.50 and 0.55.

  • Chance to beat

    The number on Bayesian dashboards: how likely B is to be better than A, given the data and a starting assumption.

    "97% chance" tells the truth only when every idea of yours changes something; if four in five are useless, B is better in only a third of such tests.

  • Statistical power

    The probability that a test notices a real effect of a given size. A power of 80% means the test misses one working effect in five.

    A weak A/B test that does find an effect almost always exaggerates it: a real +10% shows up on the dashboard as +24%.

  • Minimum detectable effect (MDE)

    The smallest real effect a test of a given size will find with 80% probability (usually at a 5% significance level). Smaller effects the test will mostly miss.

    A shop with 1,000 visitors a day would need seven years of testing to reliably notice a +2% lift in conversion.

  • Bootstrap

    A way to get the error of any number without a formula: redraw a sample of the same size from your own data with replacement many times and watch how the result jumps.

    Each resample loses a third of your data and repeats the rest, yet the spread of such copies shows the real error.

  • Holdout group

    A small random share of customers who for a long time see none of the shipped changes, so that comparing with them shows what all the changes gave together.

    Twenty changes a year, each one winning its A/B test, almost +50% together, yet customers who saw none of them buy only about 7% less.

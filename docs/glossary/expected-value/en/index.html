What to expect from a die that has no 3.5?

Mathematicians say you should "expect" 3.5 from a roll of a die. There is no such face, and no roll will ever show it. Yet this number runs casinos, insurers and lotteries. Let's see what it really means and when to trust it.

How to compute it

Multiply each possible result by its chance and add them all up. You get the average your result will approach if you repeat the same thing very many times.

1 · ⅙ + 2 · ⅙ + 3 · ⅙ + 4 · ⅙ + 5 · ⅙ + 6 · ⅙ = 21/6 = 3.5

A lottery through the eyes of a player and of the organiser

A ticket costs 20 UAH. One ticket in 100,000 wins 1,000,000 UAH, the rest win nothing. The expected win: 1,000,000 · 1/100,000 = 10 UAH. So out of every 20 hryvnias the lottery pays back 10 on average. Buy some tickets and see how it looks from each side.

When to trust expected value, and when not

  • Trust it when there are very many repeatsA casino, an insurer, a lottery make millions of identical bets. Their total almost exactly equals the expected value, so they earn without any luck.
  • Don't rely on it for a single betYou won't play a million times. Insurance has a negative expected value for you, otherwise insurers would go bust. It is still sensible: it protects you from the one case that could ruin you.
  • Careful when you can be knocked outEven a game that pays on average is dangerous if you stake everything: you can hit zero before the "average" has time to work.

Expected values add up, even for dependent quantities

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

This rule doesn't need independence, which is exactly why it is so strong. Take n people drawing their hats at random from a pile. Let Iᵢ = 1 if person i gets their own hat, otherwise 0. Each has a 1/n chance, so E[Iᵢ] = 1/n. The number of lucky ones is the sum I₁ + … + Iₙ, and its expected value is n · 1/n = 1. The events are dependent (if the first nine got their own hats, the tenth surely does too), but that doesn't stop the expected values from adding up.

Why it takes 6 rolls on average to get a six

E = 1 + ⅚ · E ⇒ E = 6

The first roll always happens. With chance ⅙ it is a six, and we're done. With chance ⅚ it isn't, and we're back at the start: E more rolls on average lie ahead, since the die remembers nothing. That gives the equation above. In general, an event with chance p takes 1/p tries on average.

  • On average 6 rolls until the first sixA six comes with chance 1/6, so on average you wait 6 rolls. Yet the most likely single outcome is a six on the very first roll, even though that is only 1/6.

How fast the average approaches 3.5

σ² = E[X²] − (E[X])² = 91/6 − 3.5² ≈ 2.92 · σ ≈ 1.71 · spread of the average = σ / √n

A single roll misses 3.5 by about 1.71. The average of n rolls misses by √n times less: about 0.54 after 10 rolls, 0.17 after 100, 0.05 after 1,000. To become ten times more precise you need a hundred times more rolls. This is the law of large numbers, and it is why a casino stays calm and a single player doesn't.

Gambler's ruin

More on “Averages and spread”

  • Mean (average)

    The sum of all values divided by how many there are: what everyone would get if it were shared equally.

    Almost everyone has more legs than average: the average is a little under two.

  • Median

    The middle value: line everyone up in order, and half are below it, half above.

    A millionaire walks into a bar: the average income shoots up, the median barely moves.

  • Variance and standard deviation

    A measure of spread: how far values typically stray from the mean. The standard deviation is its square root, in the same units.

    Two towns with the same average temperature: one stays near +15 all year, the other swings from −20 to +50.

  • Normal distribution

    A bell-shaped distribution: most values sit near the mean, and the further away, the rarer.

    Every ball on a Galton board falls at random, yet thousands of balls always build the same bell.

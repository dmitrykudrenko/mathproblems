Glossary · median and mean

Who is the "typical" person in the bar?

Ten people with ordinary salaries sit in a bar. A millionaire walks in, and the "average person" in the bar suddenly earns hundreds of thousands a month. Nobody cheated: there are simply two ways to say "typical", and they answer different questions.

Two numbers "in the middle"

Mean (average)

Add up all the values and divide by how many there are. It is what everyone would get if everything were shared equally.

1, 2, 3, 4, 100 → (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 100) / 5 = 22

Median

Line everyone up in order and take the one in the middle. Half are below, half above.

1, 2, 3, 4, 100 → 3

A single 100 pulled the mean up to 22, though four out of five values are below 5. The median barely noticed it: to the median it makes no difference whether that value is 100 or a million.

When to use which

The rule is simple: the median answers "what is it like for a typical person?", the mean answers "how much is there in total, shared among everyone?".

  • What a typical person in town earnsMedian. A few millionaires shouldn't make the "typical" salary higher.
  • How much tax the town will collectThe mean times the number of people. Here every millionaire counts.
  • The price of an ordinary flatMedian. One penthouse doesn't make the area pricier for you.
  • How much pizza to order for 30 peopleAverage eaten times 30. The median "forgets" the ones who eat for three.
  • How long the commute usually takesMedian. But for planning a week, the mean: you still have to sit through the rare jams.
  • Class grades with no extreme valuesEither. When a distribution is symmetric, the mean and the median almost coincide.

Two more strange consequences

  • The mean can be impossible"1.5 children per family" is a perfectly fine mean, though no family has half a child. A median always equals a real value or sits between two real ones.
  • Most drivers can be better than averagePeople like to laugh at drivers who think they are better than average. But if a few very reckless drivers drag the average down, most drivers really will be above it. What is impossible is for most to be above the median.

Why they differ: each number minimises something

The mean minimises the sum of squares

Σ (xᵢ − a)² → min ⇔ a = x̄ = (x₁ + … + xₙ) / n

The derivative in a is −2 Σ (xᵢ − a). It is zero when a is the mean. A square grows fast, so a distant point pulls very hard: a millionaire 5,000 thousand away weighs, squared, as much as hundreds of thousands of ordinary deviations of 10 thousand hryvnias.

The median minimises the sum of distances

Σ |xᵢ − a| → min ⇔ a = median

Move a one step to the right: the distances to all points on the left grow by a step, to all on the right shrink by a step. The sum stops falling when there are as many points on each side. Here every point has one "vote" however far away it is, so the millionaire counts like anyone else.

How much data you have to spoil

Statisticians call this the breakdown point. To move the mean anywhere at all, changing one value is enough. To move the median the same way, you have to spoil half the data. That is why the median is called robust and the mean sensitive. Sensitivity is not a flaw: it is exactly what makes the mean right for totals, where every hryvnia counts.

Where it shows up

The friendship paradox

Who has more friends: you, or your friends on average?

The bus paradox

A bus comes every 10 minutes on average, but with no timetable. You walk up to the stop at an arbitrary moment. How long do you wait on average?

The St. Petersburg paradox

A coin is flipped until the first tails; the pot starts at $2 and doubles with every heads. What does the game pay on average?

The German tank problem

A factory numbers its tanks 1, 2, 3, … You come across four, numbered 4, 10, 15 and 60. How many tanks did the factory build?

Other terms

  • Expected value

    The average result you can expect if you repeat the same thing very many times.

    A die's expected value is 3.5, yet no face shows 3.5.

  • Correlation

    A number from −1 to +1 showing how closely two quantities move together.

    In summer both ice cream sales and drownings go up. Ice cream isn't to blame: the heat drives both.

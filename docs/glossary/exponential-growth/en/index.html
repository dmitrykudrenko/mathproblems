Glossary · exponential growth

How much rice lands on the last square of a chessboard?

According to an old legend, the inventor of chess asked the ruler for a modest reward: one grain on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, and so on, twice as many each time. The ruler laughed at such a trifle. It seems it would come to a few sacks. Exponential growth is when a quantity is multiplied by the same number at every step, and it is the kind of growth intuition underestimates most of all.

    The last square outweighs all of history

    The 64th square holds 263 ≈ 9.2 · 1018 grains, about 184 billion tonnes. Today the world harvests a little over half a billion tonnes of milled rice a year. So the last square is the whole planet's harvest for about 350 years. Humanity has not grown that much rice in all its history: since 1961, when the FAO started keeping records, about 33 billion tonnes of paddy rice, still in the husk, have been harvested, and harvests before that were much smaller.

    A constant rate, a constant doubling

    Exponential growth is when a quantity grows by the same factor, that is by the same percentage, over equal stretches of time. Ordinary, linear growth adds the same number each time. Exponential growth multiplies by the same number each time.

    Worked example: a deposit at 10% a year

    Put 10,000 dollars in at 10% a year and leave the interest in the account every year. Each year the amount is multiplied by 1.1. How long until it doubles?

    1.1T = 2 → T = ln 2 / ln 1.1 ≈ 7.27 years

    And it is the same for any amount: 10,000 becomes 20,000 in 7.27 years, 20,000 becomes 40,000 in the next 7.27 years, and so on. A constant percentage means a constant doubling time. After 30 years you get 10,000 · 1.130 ≈ 174,494 dollars. If the interest were taken out each year instead of added to the amount, there would be only 10,000 + 30 · 1,000 = 40,000.

    A thousand a day, or a doubling cent?

    You are offered a choice: get 1,000 dollars every day, or get 1 cent today, 2 tomorrow, 4 the day after and so on. Move the days slider and switch the scale. What a logarithmic scale is and why an exponential becomes a straight line on it is explained in the article on the logarithm.

    Exponentials around us

    • Compound interestInterest is earned on interest too, so the amount grows by a constant percentage. Debts work the same way: a loan at 30% a year doubles in about 2.6 years.
    • A viral postIf everyone who sees a post shows it to two new people within an hour, then after 33 such steps the new viewers number 233 ≈ 8.6 billion. That is more than there are people on Earth. So growth has to slow down long before that.
    • BacteriaE. coli in ideal conditions divides about once every 20 minutes. In a day that is 72 doublings: one cell would become 272 ≈ 4.7 · 1021 cells, about 4,700 tonnes. In reality, growth in a test tube stops at a few billion cells per millilitre.

    Why an exponential always stops

    Exponential growth lasts as long as each new piece grows as freely as the first ones. The bacteria have enough food, the post still finds people who haven't seen it. But every resource is finite. The more of it is taken, the slower the growth, and the curve turns from an explosion into the letter S: almost exponential at first, then a bend and a level ceiling. This curve is called logistic. The danger is that at the start the S-curve and the exponential look the same, and the early data doesn't show where the ceiling will be.

    How to recognise an exponential

    • Growth proportional to sizeIf the new comes from what is already there (money from money, cells from cells, views from views), expect an exponential.
    • Ask for the doubling time"Plus 3% a month" sounds modest, but it means doubling in about 23 months. A doubling time conveys the scale better than a percentage.
    • Don't extend the curve blindlyEvery exponential eventually hits a ceiling. A forecast of "it will carry on like this" for years ahead is almost always wrong.
    • But don't expect the ceiling tomorrowWhile only a small share of the resource is taken, the slowdown is invisible. Early data won't tell you how many doublings are left.

    N₀ · 2t/T = N₀ · ert

    N(t) = N₀ · 2t/T = N₀ · ert,   r = ln 2 / T,   T = ln 2 / r

    These are two ways of writing the same curve. The first counts doublings: in time t there are t / T of them. The second uses base e and a growth rate r. If the quantity grows by p per step (for example, 10% = 0.1), then r = ln(1 + p), and the doubling time is T = ln 2 / ln(1 + p). This is where the "rule of 70" from the article on the logarithm comes from: for small p, ln(1 + p) ≈ p, so T ≈ 0.693 / p ≈ 70 / p%.

    Why a square outweighs all the earlier ones

    S = 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 2n−1,   2S − S = 2n − 1

    Multiply the sum by 2: every term moves one square forward. Subtract the ordinary sum from the doubled one, and everything cancels except 2n and 1. So the first n squares hold 2n − 1 grains together, and the next, (n + 1)-th square holds 2n, exactly one more.

    The logistic curve

    exponential: dN/dt = rN · logistic: dN/dt = rN (1 − N/K)

    N(t) = K / (1 + (K/N₀ − 1) · e−rt)

    An exponential is growth whose speed is proportional to size. The logistic model adds a brake: the factor 1 − N/K, the share of the resource still free. While N is small compared with the ceiling K, the brake hardly acts. Compare the two curves for a post that doubles its viewers every hour, starting from one. The ceiling is the number of people who could ever come across it.

    The ratio of the two curves is 1 + N₀(ert − 1)/K. So the exponential runs 10% ahead of the logistic curve as soon as it passes a tenth of the ceiling, whatever the ceiling is. Before that moment the data can't tell them apart.

    Where it shows up

    The duckweed lake puzzle

    Duckweed doubles every day, and one plant covers a lake in 30 days. How long do two plants take?

    The St. Petersburg paradox

    A coin is flipped until the first tails; the pot starts at $2 and doubles with every heads. What does the game pay on average?

    Benford's law

    Take the powers of two from 2 to 2^1000. What share of them start with the digit 1?

    More on “Growth and chance over time”

    • Logarithm

      How many times you multiply the base by itself to get a number: log₂ 8 = 3, because 2 · 2 · 2 = 8.

      A sheet of paper folded in half 42 times would be thicker than the distance to the Moon.

    • Harmonic series

      The sum 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + …: it grows without limit, but incredibly slowly.

      Identical books can be stacked so the top one overhangs the table edge as far as you like.

    • Random walk

      A path where each next step is taken at random: forward or back, left or right.

      A drunk man will always find his way home, but a drunk bird may never return.

    • Poisson process

      A stream of independent random events at a constant average rate: calls, Geiger clicks, raindrops.

      In 1944 Londoners thought German flying bombs were aimed at certain districts; it was ordinary random clumping.

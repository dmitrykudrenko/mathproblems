How many folds would take a sheet of paper to the Moon?

An ordinary sheet of paper is 0.1 mm thick. Fold it in half, then again, and again. The Moon is 384,400 km away, so it seems you would need thousands or even millions of folds. Intuition is badly wrong here. A logarithm is exactly the answer to this kind of question: how many times do you have to double to get from one number to another.

    A logarithm is the number of folds

    Look at the steps under the chart. The things differ by factors of thousands and millions, yet the fold numbers go up calmly: 9, 15, 27, 32, 42. That is exactly what a logarithm does: it turns "how many times bigger" into "how many doubling steps". From a book to Everest the ratio is almost three hundred thousand, yet it takes only 18 folds.

    How many times to multiply

    The logarithm of x to base b is how many times you have to multiply b by itself to get x. It is written logb x. The most common bases are 2 (doubling) and 10 (counting zeros).

    Base 2

    2 · 2 · 2 = 8 → log2 8 = 3

    210 = 1024 → log2 1024 = 10

    For numbers between powers the logarithm is a fraction: log2 10 ≈ 3.32, because 10 lies between 8 and 16.

    Base 10

    10 · 10 · 10 = 1000 → log10 1000 = 3

    The base-10 logarithm is almost the number of digits: for a number with d digits it lies between d − 1 and d. A million has 7 digits, and log10 1,000,000 = 6.

    Worked example: the sheet and the Moon

    How many sheet thicknesses fit between us and the Moon? 384,400 km = 384,400,000,000 mm = 3,844,000,000,000 times 0.1 mm. Now ask: how many times do you double 1 to get this number?

    log2 3,844,000,000,000 ≈ 41.8 → it takes 42 folds

    After 41 folds the sheet still falls short; after 42 it is already thicker than the distance to the Moon: 439,805 km.

    Ten doublings are about a thousand

    The most useful fact about logarithms for mental arithmetic: 210 = 1024, almost 1000. So every ten doublings add roughly three zeros to a number. This is also why a kilobyte is 1024 bytes.

    The factor is a little bigger than the round number: 1024 instead of 1000, so the thickness always gets a bit ahead of the round figure.

    Log scales around us

    Many familiar units are really logarithms: one step on the scale means not "plus this much" but "this many times".

    • Decibels+10 dB is ten times the sound intensity, +20 dB a hundred times. +3 dB is about double. To the ear, +10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud.
    • Earthquake magnitudeOne more unit means ten times bigger swings on a seismograph and about 32 times more energy. A magnitude 7 quake releases a thousand times the energy of a magnitude 5.
    • pHOne unit down means ten times more hydrogen ions. Black coffee has a pH of about 5, pure water 7: coffee is a hundred times more acidic, not "a bit".
    • The musical octaveA note an octave higher has twice the frequency: concert A is 440 Hz, the A above it 880 Hz. The ear hears equal steps where the frequency multiplies.

    When to use a log scale

    • Values differ by factors of thousandsPopulations of villages and megacities, wealth, earthquake strength. On an ordinary scale everything but the largest merges into one line near zero.
    • Percentages matter, not incrementsPrices, savings, case counts. The same slope on a log scale means the same percentage growth over the same time.
    • A straight line is no comfortA straight line on a log scale is an exponential, an explosion. The chart looks calm while the numbers keep doubling.
    • There is no zero on itThe logarithm of zero or of negative numbers doesn't exist. If the data has zeros, a log scale simply can't show them.
    • Read the axis labelsMarks 1, 10, 100, 1000 instead of 1, 2, 3, 4 give away a log scale. Miss them and you will misjudge the difference many times over.

    Multiplying by adding: the slide rule

    bm · bn = bm + n ⇒ log (a · c) = log a + log c

    Multiplying powers means adding exponents. So the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms. John Napier published tables of logarithms in 1614, and astronomers began adding numbers from tables instead of doing long multiplication. Soon the slide rule appeared: if numbers are marked at distance log x from the start, laying one segment after another multiplies the numbers. Engineers used it for centuries, right up to pocket calculators.

    –

    The natural logarithm ln is used here, but the base doesn't matter: any base stretches all lengths by the same factor, and the two segments still add up to the product.

    Change of base

    logb x = loga x / loga b

    Logarithms to different bases differ only by a constant factor. For example, log2 x ≈ 3.32 · log10 x. A million has 6 zeros, so it takes 6 · 3.32 ≈ 19.9, that is 20 doublings to reach it. That is why a chart on a log scale looks the same for any base: only the labels on the marks change.

    The natural logarithm and the number e

    Put one dollar in at 100% a year. Compounded once a year, you get 2 dollars. Twice at 50%, 2.25. Monthly, 2.61; daily, 2.71. The more often, the closer to a limit that stops growing: e ≈ 2.71828. It is the natural base of continuous growth, and the logarithm to it, ln, is called natural.

    (1 + 1/n)n → e as n → ∞ · ln 2 ≈ 0,693

    From ln 2 ≈ 0.693 comes the "rule of 70": at r% growth a year, an amount doubles in about 70 / r years. Check how accurate it is.

    Why halving is so fast

    A logarithm also works the other way round. Think of a number from 1 to N. Each answer to "is it at most ...?", asked about the middle of the range, throws away half the options. After k questions N / 2k remain, so it takes about log2 N questions. This is how you find a word in a dictionary or a row in a sorted table.

    Where it shows up

    The duckweed lake puzzle

    Duckweed doubles every day, and one plant covers a lake in 30 days. How long do two plants take?

    Benford's law

    Take the powers of two from 2 to 2^1000. What share of them start with the digit 1?

    Twelve coins and a balance

    One of 12 coins is fake, lighter or heavier, nobody knows. How many weighings on a two-pan balance do you need to find it for sure and say which way it is off?

    The +50 / −40 coin

    Each of 10,000 players has $100 and stakes it all on a fair coin 100 times: heads +50%, tails −40%. What share of players finish richer than they started?

    More on “Growth and chance over time”

    • Exponential growth

      Growth where a quantity is multiplied by the same factor each time, so it doubles at equal intervals.

      One grain on the first square of a chessboard, doubling on each next one: the last square holds as much rice as the world grows in over 300 years.

    • Harmonic series

      The sum 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + …: it grows without limit, but incredibly slowly.

      Identical books can be stacked so the top one overhangs the table edge as far as you like.

    • Random walk

      A path where each next step is taken at random: forward or back, left or right.

      A drunk man will always find his way home, but a drunk bird may never return.

    • Poisson process

      A stream of independent random events at a constant average rate: calls, Geiger clicks, raindrops.

      In 1944 Londoners thought German flying bombs were aimed at certain districts; it was ordinary random clumping.

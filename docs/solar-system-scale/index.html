The scale of the Solar System Earth is a 5 cm circle. How much paper for the whole system?A strip 17.7 km long.

You draw Earth as a circle 5 cm across, the size of a tangerine. Now, at the same scale, you need the Sun, the other seven planets and the distances between them. How big a sheet will it take: a poster, a wall, a football pitch? Lock in your answer, then check it: zoom out a true-scale map on screen, work it out four ways, and read why intuition gets it wrong here.

If Earth is a 5 cm circle, the Sun is a 5.5 m ball, as tall as a two-storey house, Jupiter is 55 cm and Mercury 1.9 cm. The distances are more striking still: Earth sits 587 m from the Sun, Jupiter 3 km, Neptune 17.65 km. Even with the planets in a row, the sheet must be a strip 17.7 km by 5.5 m, as long as a city is wide. Showing the full orbits takes a square 35–36 km across.

The puzzle

One 5 cm Earth, one scale for everything

Division and multiplication are enough here. Everyone has seen the sizes and distances in a school textbook, on a poster or in a cartoon. Try to answer quickly, without working it out, the way you would answer a teacher who asks you to buy paper for a class project.

  1. 1

    Earth is 5 cm across

    The real Earth is 12,742 km across. On paper it is a 5 cm circle, like a tangerine or a ping-pong ball, roughly the size of the one on your screen.

  2. 2

    The Sun and eight planets at the same scale

    Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and the Sun, each shrunk by the same factor as Earth.

  3. 3

    And the distances too

    Each planet sits at its average distance from the Sun, shrunk in the same proportion. No squeezing, as posters do.

  4. 4

    The question

    How long must the sheet be? You may put the planets in a row on one side of the Sun, which makes the sheet as small as it can be.

About 5 cm on most screens. This is Earth in our puzzle.
Intuition says

"The Sun is big, so a poster or a classroom wall will do"

The Sun really is big: a 5.5 m ball. But the empty space between the planets is thousands of times bigger. The sheet must run for 17.7 km, and the Sun takes up a third of a thousandth of its length.

What we assume

  • Diameters are averages; distances are each planet's average distance from the Sun (the semi-major axis of its orbit).
  • There are eight planets. Since 2006 Pluto is a dwarf planet; it is in round 5.
  • The sheet is a rectangle, and what we want is its long side.

Way 1 · The scale

How many kilometres in a centimetre

12,742 km ÷ 5 cm = 2,548 km per 1 cm4,498,000,000 km ÷ 2,548 = 1,765,000 cm1,765,000 cm = 17.65 km

The most direct way, as with a map: one centimetre of paper is 2,548 km of space, a scale of 1 : 255 million. Divide the distance to Neptune, 4.5 billion km, by 2,548 and you get centimetres.

Way 2 · In Earths

Count everything in Earths, then multiply by 5 cm

Sun = 109 Earths → 109 × 5 cm = 5.5 m1 AU = 11,740 Earths → 587 mNeptune: 30 AU → 30 × 587 m = 17.6 km

The big numbers disappear and only ratios are left. About 11,740 Earths in a row fit between the Sun and Earth: that is the astronomical unit, AU. Neptune is 30 times farther. This way shows best where the emptiness comes from.

Way 3 · Orders of magnitude

An estimate in your head in 20 seconds

Earth ≈ 10⁴ km, Neptune ≈ 5 × 10⁹ kmratio ≈ 5 × 10⁵5 × 10⁵ × 5 cm = 2.5 × 10⁶ cm ≈ 25 km

Round everything to powers of ten and you get 25 km instead of 17.65. Off by half again, but the main thing shows: the answer is tens of kilometres, not metres. For decisions that is often accurate enough: intuition here is off by a factor of thousands.

In a row: a strip 17.7 km × 5.5 m. With orbits: a square 35–36 km across

Check it yourself in code

The same simulation, short and with no libraries. Run the JavaScript in your browser console (F12) or in Node; the Python needs only the standard library.

JavaScript
// Earth is 5 cm. Divide the real diameters and distances by one scale and see how long the sheet gets.
// Diameters in km (NASA), distances from the Sun in astronomical units (JPL).
const AU = 149597870.7, k = 12742 / 5;          // km per 1 cm of paper
const bodies = [['Sun', 1391400, 0], ['Mercury', 4879, 0.3871], ['Venus', 12104, 0.7233], ['Earth', 12742, 1.0000],
  ['Mars', 6779, 1.5237], ['Jupiter', 139820, 5.2029], ['Saturn', 116460, 9.5367], ['Uranus', 50724, 19.1892], ['Neptune', 49244, 30.0699]];
for (const [name, d, a] of bodies)
  console.log(name.padEnd(9), (d / k).toFixed(1).padStart(6), 'cm', (a * AU / k / 100).toFixed(0).padStart(6), 'm from the Sun');
const nep = bodies[8], row = (nep[2] * AU + bodies[0][1] / 2 + nep[1] / 2) / k / 1e5;
console.log('Sheet, planets in a row:', row.toFixed(2), 'km ×', (bodies[0][1] / k / 100).toFixed(2), 'm');

Round 4 · Explanations

The Solar System is almost entirely empty

Intuition keeps the textbook picture in mind, with the planets standing shoulder to shoulder. It shows sizes honestly and silently squeezes distances thousands of times. And big numbers like "150 million" and "4.5 billion" sound equally far.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

Walk the model

From the Sun to Neptune is three and a half hours on foot

  • 0 mthe Sun, a 5.5 m ball, as tall as a two-storey house
  • 227 mMercury, a 1.9 cm pea. Three minutes' walk
  • 587 mEarth, a 5 cm tangerine. Seven minutes
  • 3 kmJupiter, a 55 cm exercise ball. 37 minutes
  • 17.65 kmNeptune, a 19 cm melon. Three and a half hours

Imagine walking this model at 5 km/h. For almost the whole way you see nothing: between bodies the size of fruit lie hundreds of metres and kilometres of emptiness. That is why a sheet that holds the distances cannot hold the sizes.

This is the core of the puzzle, though not the whole story: why intuition is off by exactly thousands, and how to draw such numbers, comes next.

Where the wrong picture comes from

A poster is honest about sizes and lies about distances

At the top is how textbooks draw it: sizes roughly to scale, planets one after another. At the bottom are the same distances honestly, at the same width. On the poster the Sun to Neptune is a few Sun diameters; in reality it is 3,230. A squeeze of hundreds of times, and nobody mentions it.

A million and a billion

Big numbers all sound equally big

150 million kilometres to the Sun and 4.5 billion to Neptune are both "astronomically far". But the second is 30 times bigger. The same goes for "million" and "billion": a million seconds is 11.6 days, a billion seconds almost 32 years. The words sound alike; the gap is like a holiday against half a lifetime.

The puzzle is the same: we know "the Sun is big" and "the planets are far", but we don't feel how many times far compared with their size. And the answer depends on exactly that ratio: the distance to Neptune is 353,000 times the size of Earth.

One scale per question

Each dimension alone is easy to draw. All of them together are not

The planets' sizes in one picture: no problem. Distances in another: also fine. A third quantity, say each planet's year (88 days for Mercury, 165 years for Neptune): the same. Only "everything together" breaks: the quantities differ thousands of times, and on one canvas either the Sun doesn't fit or the planets vanish. You felt this in round 2.

That is why an atlas has many pages, each at its own scale. Data is the same: start from the decision to be made, zoom in on the number it depends on, and put its benchmark next to it. Give the other dimensions their own panels with their own scale. A powers-of-ten axis helps you see everything at once, a threshold helps you see the problem, a zoom helps you feel the scale.

Rigoroustables, charts, formulas

One axis for everything

On an ordinary axis everything sticks to zero. On a powers-of-ten axis each shows

sizes of bodiesdistances from the Sun

On an ordinary axis from 0 to 18 km every centimetre and every metre "costs" the same: Mercury's 1.9 cm and the Sun's 5.5 m sit at one point by zero, and even Earth at 587 m is almost there. A powers-of-ten axis gives each "×10" the same step: 1 cm, 10 cm, 1 m, 10 m… Then the sizes take the left half, the distances the right, and between them you see a gap of about a thousand times. Such an axis is also called logarithmic.

Deepergeneralisations, theory, history

“Deeper” is hidden on all puzzles.

The psychology of numbers

By nature we feel numbers on a powers scale

Robert Siegler and John Opfer (2003) asked children to place numbers on a line from 0 to 1,000. Younger ones stretched small numbers and squeezed large ones, as on a powers-of-ten scale, and only with age moved to an even one. Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues (2008) found the same in Mundurucu adults in the Amazon, who had no schooling in counting.

And David Landy and colleagues (2013) showed that many adults place a million near the middle of a line from a thousand to a billion, though it really sits right next to the thousand. With big numbers we feel "how many zeros", not "how many times".

History

The ratios were known a century before the kilometres

Back in 1619 Kepler linked a planet's period to its distance, so the planets' relative distances were known. What remained unknown was the scale itself: how many kilometres in an astronomical unit. In 1672 Giovanni Cassini in Paris and Jean Richer in Cayenne measured Mars's position at the same time and got a distance to the Sun less than a tenth short of the truth.

So 17th-century astronomers were solving our puzzle in reverse: they had the model in "Earths" and looked for one factor to turn it into kilometres. In 2012 the International Astronomical Union fixed the astronomical unit exactly: 149,597,870,700 m.

Round 5 · Going further

Light on foot, Pluto and a model in your own yard

The 5 cm Earth model keeps being useful: it gives a feel for the speed of light and the distance to the stars, and shows what model you can really build at a school or a stadium.

The maths

Divide the speed of light by the scale and you get 1.18 m/s, the pace of an easy stroll. Sunlight "walks" to Earth in 8.3 minutes, just as in reality, and to Neptune in 4 hours 10 minutes. The time doesn't depend on the scale, since both the distance and the speed shrank.

And the nearest star, Proxima Centauri 4.25 light years away, lies 157,600 km off on our model. If the Solar System is a city, the next star is four round-the-world trips away.

The logic

What if you add Pluto?

Pluto is 2,377 km across, 9 mm on the model, smaller than Mercury. Its average distance is 39.5 AU, 23.2 km on the model, so the sheet would get a third longer. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union wrote down that a planet must clear its orbit of other bodies. Pluto has not: its orbit crosses Neptune's and runs through the Kuiper belt, home to thousands of similar bodies. So since then it is a dwarf planet, and the puzzle has eight planets.

That change shows one more thing about scale: the word "planet" covers only eight bodies, while the Solar System has millions of smaller ones, almost all of them beyond Neptune. Our 17.7 km strip is only the inner part of the system.

In practice

Two models instead of one

If Neptune is at the far end of a football pitch, 105 m from the Sun, Earth is a 0.3 mm grain of sand and the Sun a 3.2 cm ball. That is why museums and schools build two models. One for sizes: a Sun as big as an umbrella and planets from a pea to a ball. Another for distances: a trail where the planets are signs with dots.

That is the same advice as for data charts. When values differ a thousandfold, don't try to show it all in one picture at one scale. Give two scales, a powers-of-ten axis, or show the small thing separately at its own scale.

Bonus · At work

Where this shows up at work

Real situations from marketing, analytics, product and business where the same maths applies. Only known data, research and plain calculations you can check.

Email marketing

0.4% spam complaints is a hairline on a chart and the red zone at Gmail

The situation
A campaign report puts the spam complaint rate on a pie chart next to "read" and "not opened". 0.4% is a 1.4-degree slice you can barely see. It looks like there is no problem.
The trap
The chart compares complaints with 100%, but what matters is the threshold. Gmail requires every sender to keep the complaint rate in Postmaster Tools below 0.3% and recommends below 0.1%. 0.4% is 133% of the limit beyond which mail is more likely to land in spam, and four times the recommended 0.1%. Just like the planets: a small number next to a big one disappears, though on its own it is critical.
How to plan for it
Draw a small number against its own threshold: an axis from 0 to 0.5% with lines at 0.1% and 0.3%, or "percent of the limit", where 100% is the limit. Keep pie charts for shares compared with each other, not with a rule. A powers-of-ten axis won't help here: complaints become visible, but you still can't see they are over the limit. What works is a separate panel zoomed in on complaints, with the threshold line, while the millions of sends and the money get panels of their own.

Source: Gmail Help: Email sender guidelines (requirements in force since February 2024; checked 17 Nov 2026)

Product analytics

An honest funnel in which the last step vanishes

The situation
A shop's funnel for a month: 100,000 visitors, 7,000 added to cart, 2,500 started checkout, 2,000 bought, 300 bought again within 90 days. On a chart with one shared scale the first bar is 600 pixels long.
The trap
Then purchases are 12 pixels and repeat purchases 1.8 pixels, a thin tick. The honest scale hides exactly the step profit depends on most. And the 80% step "checkout → purchase" and the 15% step "purchase → repeat" look equally invisible, though the first is fine and the second needs work.
How to plan for it
Show two scales side by side: absolute counts, and each step's conversion from the one before (7%, 36%, 80%, 15%). Or a powers-of-ten axis: 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000, where every funnel step shows. And label the bars with numbers: people read numbers, not bar lengths.
Monetisation

"Whales": 0.15% of players bring half the revenue

The situation
In 2014 the company Swrve looked at a month of purchases in free mobile games, tens of millions of players. Only 1.5% of players paid at all. Half of all revenue came from the top 10% of payers, that is 0.15% of all players.
The trap
On a linear "revenue per player" chart, 98.5% of players lie at zero, a few "whales" stand out as towers, and the other payers merge into a strip by the axis. Average revenue per user (ARPU) says nothing about a typical player: in these games the median is zero. A decision based on the average, like a picture at one scale, loses either the whales or everyone else.
How to plan for it
Split into segments and draw each at its own scale: non-payers, small payers, whales. Show the revenue share of the top 1% and top 10% of payers, and use a powers-of-ten axis for the distribution. Losing a single whale shows up in monthly revenue, though a chart of the average doesn't show it.

Source: Swrve, Mobile Games Monetization Report, 26 Feb 2014 (one month of data, January 2014)

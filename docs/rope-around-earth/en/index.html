A rope is tied tightly around the equator, then made 1 metre longer and lifted evenly off the ground. Most people expect a gap thinner than a hair, because one metre is nothing next to 40,000 kilometres. In fact a mouse could walk under the rope. And the answer is the same for an orange, the Earth or the Sun.

The puzzle

One rope, one equator, one extra metre

This is an old geometry riddle that often turns up in puzzle books. It tests how we handle very large and very small numbers when they meet in one problem.

  1. 1

    The rope

    A rope is tied tightly around the Earth along the equator. Its length equals the length of the equator, about 40,075 km. We treat the Earth as a perfect sphere with no mountains or oceans.

  2. 2

    One more metre

    The rope is cut and a 1 metre piece is spliced in. Now it is one metre longer than the equator.

  3. 3

    Evenly

    The rope is lifted off the surface so that it is at the same height everywhere. It forms a circle again, just a slightly larger one.

  4. 4

    The question

    How big is the gap between the rope and the ground? Could an ant get under it? A cat? A person?

Intuition says

"One metre over 40 thousand kilometres? The gap will be microscopic"

In fact the gap is about 15.9 cm. A mouse could walk under the rope without ducking. The mistake is comparing the metre with the whole equator, when all that matters is how a change in length relates to a change in radius.

Model assumptions

  • The Earth is a perfect sphere and the equator is a circle.
  • The rope does not stretch or sag.
  • After lifting, the rope forms a circle with the same centre.

The real equator has mountains and valleys, but the puzzle is about geometry, not terrain. Another way to lift the rope is in round 5.

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Don't compare one metre with 40,000 kilometres. What matters is how much the radius of a circle changes when its length grows by a metre.

  2. 2

    A circle's length is 2πR, so a change in length is tied to a change in radius the same way for any circle. The size of the Earth cancels out.

  3. 3

    How much longer must a circle get for its radius to grow by 1 cm? Work it out for a coin, since for the Earth the answer is the same. Now: how much does the radius grow if you add a whole metre?

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why the Earth's radius doesn't matter

A circle's circumference is proportional to its radius. So a change in length is proportional to a change in radius, and the size of the sphere cancels out. Here are five ways to see it.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

A square instead of a circle

All the extra goes into the corners

A running track

Why races start staggered

lane width1.22 mthe stadium standard
longer per lane≈ 7.67 m2π × 1.22 m per lap

A standard running track has two straights and two semicircular bends. The straights are the same for every lane, and the two bends together make a full circle. So each lane out is about 2π × 1.22 ≈ 7.67 m longer per lap, however big the arena. Without a staggered start, lane 8 would be about 54 m longer than lane 1.

The rules measure distance slightly inside each lane, so real start staggers differ a little from this simple estimate.

Rigoroustables, charts, formulas

“Rigorous” is hidden on all puzzles.

One formula

L = 2πR, so ΔR = ΔL / 2π

Each metre of radius adds 2π ≈ 6.28 m to the circle. And the other way round, each metre of circle adds 1/(2π) ≈ 0.159 m to the radius. The rule is the same for circles of any size. Move the slider: R changes by a factor of billions, but the gap doesn't.

Compare changes

A tiny fraction of a huge number

1 m relative to the equator2.5 × 10⁻⁸1 m / 40,075 km
the same fraction of the radius15.9 cm2.5 × 10⁻⁸ × 6,378 km

Intuition is right about one thing: the rope grew by a tiny proportion. But the radius grows by the same proportion, and the Earth's radius is 6,378 km. A tiny fraction of thousands of kilometres is centimetres. The mistake is carrying the "microscopic" proportion over to the gap itself.

For formula lovers

The even lift and the tent

Even lift: the circle of radius R + h must equal the old circle plus the added ΔL. R cancels.

2π(R+h)=2πR+ΔL h=ΔL2π=16.2832 m=15.92 cm

Square: each side a grows by 2h, so the perimeter grows by 8h.

4(a+2h)=4a+ΔL h=ΔL8=12.5 cm

Tent: the two tangent segments plus the arc still on the sphere must equal the old circle plus ΔL.

2R·tanθ+2R(π−θ)=2πR+ΔL tanθ−θ=ΔL2R

The height of the tent's peak above the sphere and the angle θ for small angles. For the Earth and one metre.

h=R(1cosθ−1)≈Rθ22 θ≈(3ΔL2R)1/3 Earth:θ≈0.354°,h≈121.5 m

With an even lift R cancels, so the gap does not depend on the size of the sphere. In the tent R stays in the formula: the height grows roughly as the cube root of R. So the tent over the Sun is taller than the one over the Earth, and the one over an orange is lower. For small angles tan θ − θ ≈ θ³/3, which gives the approximation.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

40,075 km

The length of the equator

The Earth's equator is about 40,075 km long. Because the Earth spins, it is slightly flattened, so a circle through the poles is shorter, about 40,008 km.

1 / 10⁷

Where the metre came from

In late 18th-century France the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. That is why a circle around the Earth through the poles comes out at about 40,000 km.

6.28 m

Lift it by a metre

To lift a rope around the Earth evenly by 1 metre, you only need to add 2π ≈ 6.28 m. For a person 1.8 m tall to walk under it, about 11.3 m is enough.

7.67 m

The running track stagger

A lane on a stadium track is 1.22 m wide. Over the full circle of the bends, each lane out is about 2π × 1.22 ≈ 7.67 m longer. That is why races with bends use staggered starts.

121.5 m

A tent over the equator

If the extra metre of rope is pulled up at a single point, the peak of the tent rises to 121.5 m. The rope leaves the ground about 39 km from the peak on each side.

×109

A rope around the Sun

The Sun's circumference is about 4.37 million kilometres, 109 times the Earth's equator. Yet one extra metre of rope still gives a 15.9 cm gap there, just as for an orange.

The puzzle

Two envelopes, one twice as fat

A host puts an amount x in one envelope and 2x in the other. You don't know which is which. The puzzle has been around since the mid-20th century, and papers on the best way to explain the flaw are still being written.

  1. 1

    The envelopes

    Two identical envelopes contain money. One holds twice as much as the other.

  2. 2

    The pick

    You take one envelope at random. You may open it and see the amount A, or leave it closed.

  3. 3

    The argument

    "The other envelope holds 2A or A/2, 50/50. So on average it holds ½·2A + ½·A/2 = 1.25A. Switching pays."

  4. 4

    The trouble

    The same argument works for a closed envelope, and for the other envelope after you switch. So you should switch forever. Which step of the argument is wrong?

Intuition says

"I can win A or lose only A/2. I should switch"

With a closed envelope, switching gains nothing: you picked at random, and the two envelopes are interchangeable. The flaw hides in what the letter A stands for. This puzzle is about a line of reasoning, not about a single number.

Assumptions

  • The host picks the smaller amount x by some rule (a distribution) that you don't know.
  • You pick an envelope at random, like a coin toss.
  • The goal is more money on average.

If you see the amount, the answer depends on the host's rule. Rounds 4 and 5 cover this.

Cover's threshold: before each round we draw a random number T (exponential with mean 100). If the amount in the envelope is below T, switch, otherwise keep. For any rule the host uses, the chance of ending with the larger envelope is above 50%, because sometimes T lands between x and 2x. How far above depends on whether T's scale matches the amounts. For amounts up to a million and the same T, the edge would be only a few thousandths of a percent.

Round 4 · Where's the flaw

Five ways to see what broke

The 1.25A argument has two weak links. First, the letter A stands for different amounts in different cases. Second, 50/50 odds cannot hold for every amount.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

One pair, two cases

The gain and the loss are equal: x

You hold x, switch to 2x+x
You hold 2x, switch to x−x

Say the envelopes hold x and 2x. Each case has chance ½, so the gain from switching is zero. The naive formula uses A = x in the first case and A = 2x in the second, then treats them as one amount. That is why the gain looks like A and the loss like A/2, when both are really x.

The argument proves too much

Each envelope is "bigger" than the other

Apply the same argument to the second envelope and call its amount B. It says the first envelope holds 1.25B on average. So each envelope is on average a quarter bigger than the other, which is impossible. When an argument proves a claim and its opposite, it contains an error, even before we can see where.

Without looking

A closed envelope: switching changes exactly nothing

If you don't open the envelope, you have no information at all. "Pick at random and switch" is the same as "pick the other one at random". Both give the larger envelope with chance ½. That is why the simulation shows equal totals for switching and keeping, whatever the host's rule.

Rigoroustables, charts, formulas

When you see the amount

The host's rule from round 2: switching pays for every amount but one

In the game the host picked the smaller amount x from 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320 with equal chances. For most amounts you can see, the odds really are 50/50 and switching averages 1.25A. But if you see 640, the other envelope surely holds 320. That one big loss balances all the gains, and the average gain from switching blind is zero.

For formula lovers

The naive sum, the correct sum, and the threshold

The naive sum: the other envelope is either double or half. The mistake is that A is not one amount here.

E[other]=12·2A+12·A2=1.25A

Correctly: the envelopes hold a pair (x, 2x), and switching gives +x or −x with equal chances.

E[gain]=12·(+x)+12·(−x)=0

Once you see the amount A, the chance that the other envelope is bigger depends on the distribution p of x.

P(other bigger|A) =p(A)p(A)+p(A2)

If that chance were ½ for every A, all these probabilities would be equal and their sum infinite. That is impossible.

½ for every A ⇒p(A)=p(A2)=p(A4)=… ⇒∑p=∞

Cover's threshold: a random threshold T adds a win when it falls between x and 2x.

P(larger)=12+12·P(x<T≤2x) >12

Here is how the threshold works. If T is below both amounts, you always keep; if it is above both, you always switch: either way your chance is ½. But if T falls between x and 2x, you switch away from the smaller envelope and keep the larger, so you win for sure. When T has positive density on every positive number, that chance is always above zero.

Deepergeneralisations, theory, history

“Deeper” is hidden on all puzzles.

The prior

"50/50 for every amount" is impossible

The chance that the other envelope is bigger depends on how the host chooses amounts. For it to be ½ for every A, the amount A/2 would have to be as likely as A, A as likely as 2A, and so on forever in both directions. Infinitely many equally likely amounts can't add up to a total probability of 1: the total is either infinity or zero. No such rule exists.

In 1995 the philosopher John Broome described this rule: the smaller amount is 2ᵏ with probability ⅓·(⅔)ᵏ, k = 0, 1, 2, … If you see A = 1, the other holds 2 for sure. The necktie paradox

An older relative of the puzzle comes from the Belgian mathematician Maurice Kraitchik. Two men argue over whose necktie cost more. They agree that the owner of the pricier tie gives it to the other. Each reasons: "If I lose, I lose my tie. If I win, I get a more expensive one. The bet is in my favour." A bet can't favour both sides.

What each one sees"I lose less than I can win"
What really happensOne's gain = the other's loss

The flaw is the same as with the envelopes: "my tie" is worth different amounts in the winning and the losing case. If the ties cost c and C, the winner gains C − c and the loser loses that same difference.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

Neckties

It started with neckties

The earliest known version comes from Maurice Kraitchik, author of a book on mathematical recreations. In it two men compare their neckties, and also their wallets.

1953

From Schrödinger

The mathematician John Littlewood gave a similar puzzle in his 1953 book and credited it to the physicist Erwin Schrödinger. In that version each card has a number on both sides. You see one side of a random card and decide whether to turn it over.

1958

Martin Gardner

Martin Gardner first wrote about the necktie paradox in his Mathematical Games column in January 1958. Later, in 1982, he popularised it as a wallet game in the book Aha! Gotcha.

1989

Nalebuff's envelopes

The economist Barry Nalebuff published two envelope problems, in 1988 and 1989. The second is the one most often seen today as "the two envelopes paradox".

1992

A paradox without probability

The logician Raymond Smullyan showed that the puzzle breaks intuition even without probability. "If the envelopes hold x and 2x, I win or lose x." "If I hold A, I win A or lose A/2." Both sentences sound right but lead to different conclusions.

> ½

Cover's trick

The random-threshold strategy is usually credited to the information theorist Thomas Cover. It wins more than half the time for any rule the host uses, even an unknown one. Dozens of papers have been written on the puzzle as a whole, and there is still no single explanation everyone agrees on.

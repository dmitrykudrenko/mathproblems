The puzzle

A circle, a triangle and one chord

The French mathematician Joseph Bertrand posed this problem in his 1889 book "Calcul des probabilités".

The French mathematician Joseph Bertrand posed this problem in his 1889 book "Calcul des probabilités". He wanted to show that the words "at random" do not, by themselves, define a probability.

  1. 1

    The circle

    Take a circle of radius R with an equilateral triangle inscribed in it. The triangle's side is √3 · R ≈ 1.73 R.

  2. 2

    The chord

    A chord is a segment joining two points of the circle. The longest chord is a diameter, 2R long. Short chords run close to the edge.

  3. 3

    A long chord

    Call a chord long if it is longer than the triangle's side. That is the same as its midpoint lying less than R/2 from the centre.

  4. 4

    The question

    A chord is drawn at random. What is the probability that it is long?

Intuition says

"There is one answer, you just have to compute it correctly"

In fact three correct calculations give three different numbers. None of them contains a mistake. Each one describes its own way of picking the chord.

Three ways to pick a chord

  • Method 1. Two random points on the circle are the chord's endpoints.
  • Method 2. A random radius and a random point on it. The chord passes through the point at right angles to the radius.
  • Method 3. A random point inside the disc is the chord's midpoint.

Each method looks natural. Rounds 2 and 3 show the answer each one gives.

Round 4 · Explanations

Three calculations, all of them right

Each answer has a short geometric argument. The calculations are all fine. They describe three different experiments.

Method 1 · a 120° arc

The answer is 1/3

Turn the circle so the first endpoint is at the top. The chord is long if the second endpoint lands on the lower arc between the triangle's other two vertices. That arc is 120° out of 360°, a third of the circle.

Method 2 · half the radius

The answer is 1/2

The chord is perpendicular to the radius and crosses it at the chosen point. The triangle's side crosses the radius exactly halfway, at distance R/2 from the centre. The chord is long if the point is closer to the centre, on the inner half of the radius. The chance is 1/2.

Method 3 · the inner circle

The answer is 1/4

The chord is long if its midpoint lies inside the circle of radius R/2. That circle is inscribed in the triangle. Its radius is half of R, so its area is a quarter: π(R/2)² = πR²/4. A random point of the big disc lands there with chance 1/4.

What "at random" means

Randomness needs a procedure

A probability is always computed for a specific experiment. "A random chord" names the outcome but not how it was produced. The three methods are three different experiments, so the answers differ.

The same thing shows up elsewhere. In the two-children problem the answer is 1/3 or 1/2 depending on how exactly you learned that one of the two children is a boy. In the Monty Hall problem switching pays because the host knows where the car is and always opens a door with a goat. If he opened a door at random and it happened to show a goat, the odds would be 1/2 and 1/2.

For formula lovers

It all comes down to the distance d from the centre to the chord

A chord at distance d from the centre has length ℓ. It is longer than the triangle's side √3 · R exactly when d is less than R/2.

ℓ=2R2−d2 ℓ>3·R⇔d<R2

Method 1: the central angle θ is uniform from 0° to 180°. The chord is long when θ is over 120°.

ℓ=2R·sin(θ2)>3·R⇔θ>120° P=60180=13

Method 2: the distance d is uniform from 0 to R.

d∈[0,R]P=R2R=12

Method 3: the chord's midpoint is uniform in the disc, so we compare the area of the circle of radius R/2 with the whole disc.

P=π(R2)2πR2=14

The density of the distance d for each method, with R = 1.

f1(d)=2π1−d2 f2(d)=1,f3(d)=2d

The three densities from round 3 differ, so the area under them from 0 to R/2 differs too. Method 1 often produces chords near the edge, which are short. Method 3 rarely puts the midpoint near the centre, because there is little area there.

Round 5 · Going further

Straws, other methods and a square

Is one of the three answers "the most natural"? The physicist Edwin Jaynes argued that it is. You can also invent as many new methods as you like, and the same problem appears even with a random point in a square.

Jaynes's argument, 1973

Why many people pick 1/2

Jaynes reasoned like this. The problem says nothing about where the circle is or how big it is. So the answer should not change if the circle is rotated, shifted or scaled. He called this a principle of maximum ignorance: if we do not know something, the solution should not depend on it.

Only one distribution of lines passes this test, and it gives the same answer as method 2: 1/2. Methods 1 and 3 fail when the circle is shifted. Physically, this distribution arises when long straws are thrown onto the circle from far away: a straw "does not know" where the circle is. The simulation here does exactly that. Change the circle's size: the share of long chords stays near 50%.

Straw centres are spread uniformly over the square, with random directions. Because the square is finite, the exact share is slightly above 1/2, by no more than half a percentage point.

This is an argument for 1/2. It does not prove the others wrong. If the experiment really works like method 1 or 3, the right answer is 1/3 or 1/4.

What does "a random point" mean?

The square contains a smaller square with the same centre and half the side. What is the chance that a random point lands in it? If you pick the coordinates x and y uniformly, the chance is the share of the area: 1/4. If instead you pick a random direction from the centre and go a random fraction of the way to the edge, the point lands in the small square when that fraction is under one half. The chance is 1/2.

The same 1/4 and 1/2 as in methods 3 and 2. The second procedure crowds points near the centre, just like the chord midpoints in method 2.

An even simpler example. A factory makes squares with a side from 1 to 2 cm. What is the chance the side is under 1.5 cm? If the side is uniform between 1 and 2 cm, the chance is 1/2. If the area is uniform between 1 and 4 cm², the area must be under 2.25 cm², and the chance is 1.25/3 ≈ 41.7%.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1889

Bertrand's book

Joseph Bertrand described the chord problem in his textbook "Calcul des probabilités", published in 1889. He gave three solutions to show that the question is badly posed, because it does not say how to pick the chord.

2/3

Another Bertrand paradox

The same book contains the "box paradox". There are three boxes: one with two gold coins, one with two silver coins and one with a gold and a silver coin. You pick a box at random, take out a coin at random, and it is gold. The chance that the other coin in that box is also gold is 2/3. Intuition usually says 1/2. It is a close relative of the Monty Hall problem.

1973

"The Well-Posed Problem"

That was the title of a 1973 paper by the physicist Edwin T. Jaynes. He argued that if the answer must not change under rotations, shifts and changes of scale, Bertrand's problem has a single solution, 1/2. Not everyone agrees that these requirements are compulsory.

1933

Kolmogorov's axioms

In 1933 Andrey Kolmogorov built probability theory on axioms. In it a probability is always defined on a specific space of outcomes. Bertrand's paradox is often cited as an example of why such a precise definition is needed.

0…1

Any answer at all

There are endless ways to pick a chord, and the answer can be any number from 0 to 1. You only need to choose the right distribution of the distance from the centre to the chord, as in round 5. Bertrand's three methods are just the best known.

dp · dθ

A relative of Buffon's needle

Jaynes's straw throwing uses the same distribution of lines as Buffon's needle. It obeys Crofton's formula: a random line hits a convex shape with a probability proportional to its perimeter. This branch of mathematics is called integral geometry.

Penney's game HHH against THH. What are THH's odds?7 in 8.

Each player picks a pattern of three coin flips, say heads, heads, heads. The coin is flipped until one of the patterns shows up in a row. You choose first, and your opponent sees your choice. If you pick HHH and they pick THH, what is their chance of winning? Lock in your answer, then test it against the computer and on a million simulated games.

Each player picks a pattern of three coin flips. The coin is flipped until one of the patterns shows up in a row. All patterns look equal, since each has a 1/8 chance. Yet THH beats HHH in 7 games out of 8, and whatever pattern you pick, your opponent has a reply that wins at least 2 times out of 3.

The puzzle

Two patterns, one coin

Walter Penney described this game in 1969 in the Journal of Recreational Mathematics. The rules fit in three lines, and you can play it with a friend in five minutes.

  1. 1

    The coin

    The coin is fair: heads (H) and tails (T) each come up with chance 1/2, and the flips are independent.

  2. 2

    Patterns

    A pattern is three results in a row, in a fixed order. There are eight of them: HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT. Any given three flips form each of them with chance 1/8.

  3. 3

    Choosing

    You pick a pattern first. Your opponent sees your choice and picks a different one.

  4. 4

    Playing

    The coin is flipped again and again. The player whose pattern first shows up as the last three flips wins. Say you picked HHH, your opponent picked THH, and the flips went like this:

    After the fifth flip the last three read THH, and your opponent won.

  5. 5

    The question

    You picked HHH, your opponent picked THH. What is the chance your opponent wins?

Intuition says

"Every pattern has a 1/8 chance, so it's 50/50"

In fact THH wins 7 games out of 8. An equal chance for a pattern in three fixed flips says nothing about which pattern appears first. What's more, this game has no best pattern: each one has a stronger rival.

Assumptions

  • The coin is fair and the flips are independent.
  • The players' patterns differ.
  • The window slides: a pattern can start at any flip, not only in blocks of three.
  • Flipping goes on until someone wins. The game ends with probability 1, and there are no draws.

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Each pattern having a 1/8 chance in three fixed flips says nothing about which one shows up first. Think about what has to come right before the first HHH.

  2. 2

    Suppose a tail has already come up somewhere. Can HHH still appear before THH after that? Look at the flip just before the first three heads in a row.

  3. 3

    Think about the only situation in which no tail comes before the first three heads in a row. How often does that happen?

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why the second player can always get the edge

The patterns are equal if you look at three fixed flips. What matters in the game is different: how patterns overlap with each other and with themselves. Here are five ways to see it.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

HHH against THH

HHH can only win right at the start

First three flips are HHH1/8HHH's only chance
Anything else7/8THH wins

If any of the first three flips is T, then the first HHH must come right after a T. That T plus the two heads after it already form THH, one flip earlier. So once the first T has appeared, HHH has lost for good.

Rock, paper, scissors

There is no best pattern

Each arrow points from the winner to the loser, and the number is the winner's chance. THH beats HHT, HHT beats HTT, HTT beats TTH, and TTH beats THH. The loop closes, as in rock, paper, scissors. Such a relation is called nontransitive: "A beats B" and "B beats C" do not imply "A beats C".

The second player's rule

Opposite of the middle, then the first two

The reply ends with the two flips that start the opponent's pattern. So it almost always lies in wait for that pattern: before the opponent can finish, their first two flips have to come up, and they are often preceded by the symbol you need. The first symbol is the opposite of the middle one, so the reply does not overlap itself and does not wait longer than it must.

Rigoroustables, charts, formulas

“Rigorous” is hidden on all puzzles.

Equal on average, not equal first

Patterns appear equally often, but differently

1,000 flips contain 998 windows of three, and each pattern appears 998/8 ≈ 124.75 times on average. But HHH comes in clumps: HHHH contains it twice, HHHHH three times. When appearances stick together, the gaps between clumps get longer, and the first appearance takes longer to come.

Waiting time

HHH takes 14 flips, HHT only 8

HHH: a miss sends you back14flips on average
HHT: an extra H does no harm8flips on average

Say HH has just come up. For HHT a third H spoils nothing: you still have HH and wait for a T. For HHH a T at that point wipes out your progress, and you start from scratch. Patterns that overlap with themselves take longer to arrive.

Yet waiting time does not settle a duel. HHH and HHT play 50/50, despite average waits of 14 and 8: both start with HH, and after the first HH the next flip decides everything.

For formula lovers

Conway's leading numbers

The leading number A·B is built like this:

  • for each k from n down to 1, compare the last k symbols of A with the first k symbols of B;
  • if they match, write 1, otherwise write 0;
  • read the resulting string of 0s and 1s as a binary number.
A=HHH,B=THH: A·A=1112=7,A·B=0002=0 B·B=1002=4,B·A=0112=3

Conway's formula: the odds come from the four leading numbers. The last line plugs in the numbers from the example above.

Odds that B comes before A =(A·A−A·B):(B·B−B·A) (7−0):(4−3)=7:1→P(Bfirst)=78

The mean waiting time for pattern A: twice A·A.

Mean waiting timeE[TA]=2·(A·A) HHH:2·7=14 HTH:2·1012=10 HHT:2·1002=8

John Conway found this formula and called it the leading-number algorithm, but he did not publish a proof. Proofs came later, among them a martingale argument by Shuo-Yen Robert Li (1980) and the work of Guibas and Odlyzko (1981). The formula works for patterns of any length, as long as neither contains the other. The page uses it for every exact number in the round 3 table, and the simulation checks them.

How long a pattern takes

Waiting time comes from self-overlaps

For every k at which the first k symbols of the pattern equal the last k, add 2k. The sum is the average number of flips until the first appearance. A length-n pattern with no self-overlap takes 2n flips on average, and n identical symbols in a row take the longest: 2n+1 − 2.

The Humble–Nishiyama game

Red and black instead of heads and tails

In 2010 Steve Humble and Yutaka Nishiyama suggested playing with a 52-card deck: 26 red (♥) and 26 black (♠). Cards are turned over one at a time. As soon as someone's pattern appears, that player takes all the turned cards, and the search starts again. Whoever collects more of these tricks wins.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1969

Problem 95, "Penney-Ante"

Walter Penney published the game as problem 95 in volume 2 of the Journal of Recreational Mathematics, on page 241.

1974

Martin Gardner's column

In the October 1974 issue of Scientific American, Martin Gardner devoted his "Mathematical Games" column to the paradoxes of nontransitive relations. That is where a wide audience learned about Penney's game and Conway's leading numbers.

7 : 1

A formula without a proof

John Conway came up with a way to get the odds for any pair of patterns from binary "leading numbers", but did not prove it. Rigorous proofs appeared in the early 1980s.

2/3

Efron's dice

The statistician Bradley Efron devised four dice with these faces: 4, 4, 4, 4, 0, 0; six 3s; 6, 6, 2, 2, 2, 2; 5, 5, 5, 1, 1, 1. Each die loses to the one before it with chance 2/3, and the first loses to the last. Same principle: whoever chooses second can always take a stronger die.

14 · 8

Waiting longer doesn't mean losing

HHH takes 14 flips on average, HHT only 8. Yet in a duel they are even, 50/50. Among fours it can even go the other way: HTTH, which takes 18 flips on average, beats TTHH (16 flips) with chance 7/12. Overlaps decide who comes first, not average waits.

99.49%

A near-certain win with cards

In Humble and Nishiyama's card version, the second player with ♥♠♠ against ♠♠♠ wins 99.49% of games and loses just 0.11%. With a coin the chance was 7/8. The deck turns an edge into a near-certain win.

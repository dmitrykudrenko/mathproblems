A report like this arrives every quarter: four lines and four decisions. Every number in it is true, and every one can lead you astray. The course teaches you to ask a report a few simple questions. The first part is about reading the numbers themselves: averages, rankings, comparing groups, charts. The second is about who got into the data and who is missing. The third is about conclusions: is it a cause or a coincidence, who gets credit for a sale, how many alarms are real, and are the numbers made up. At the end comes an exam of six reports and a verdict on this one.

≈ 1–1.5 hours, in 10-minute parts if you like; your progress is saved.

This course is about data that is simply collected: reports, surveys, cohorts. Experiments have a course of their own, "A/B testing paradoxes".

Check what you already know

Three short reports, one from each part. Choose what to do with each: the result suggests which part to start with. These answers don't count towards your course progress.

An HR report: "The average salary in the department is ₴63,800, 23% more than last year. Pay in the team is rising fast." Last year the department had 20 people; this year a CTO on ₴300,000 a month joined them.

Without the CTO the mean is the same ₴52,000 as last year: the whole "rise" is one salary. Chapter 1 is about this.

A training report: "Managers rated the course 4.8 out of 5. Let's renew it for next year." The form is handed out at the last session, and 35% of those enrolled got that far.

The rating came from those who stayed to the end, the ones who liked the course. The form knows nothing about the 65% who dropped out. Part 2 is about this.

A product report: "Customers who watched the welcome webinar renew twice as often." The webinar is optional. Proposal: make it compulsory for everyone.

Those who watch the webinar are keen anyway, and they renew more often anyway. Only a random half offered the webinar will show what the webinar itself gives. Part 3 is about this.

Part 1 · no formulas

How to read a report

Five situations where the number in a report is true and the conclusion drawn from it is wrong. Each chapter starts with a question: guess first, then check it on a live picture. There are no formulas here.

1

The average order is up 24%

"The average order is up 24%. Are customers buying more?"

A line like this in a quarterly report reads as good news. Before celebrating, let's see what those 24% are made of.

The average order is the sum of all orders divided by how many there are. In Q2 the shop had 1,000 orders with an average of ₴2,000.

In Q3 there were 1,000 orders again, and the average was ₴2,480: hence the +24%. Among these orders there is one unusual one. A corporate client bought ₴482,000 worth, while an ordinary order here is about two thousand.

Below, next to the mean, there will be one more number, the median. It is the order in the middle when all orders are lined up from smallest to largest: half are smaller, half larger.

What happened in Q3 to the ordinary customer's order?

Customers are not buying more. One order of ₴482,000 is ₴480,000 more than an ordinary order. Divided by 1,000 orders, that is +₴480 on the average, the whole rise from ₴2,000 to ₴2,480. The other 999 orders still average ₴2,000. Orders are almost never spread symmetrically: many small ones and a few large ones. So the average order is usually above the median even without corporate clients. Nearly all money and time behaves this way: revenue per customer, salaries, call durations, delivery times.

  • The ordinary customer's order did not grow: the median barely moved, and the mean without the largest order stayed at ₴2,000. The +24% is one deal. It deserves its own line, as a deal, not as a trend.
  • Ask for the median next to the mean. If they differ a lot, the data has a few very large values.
  • Ask what the mean is without the largest customers, for example without the top 1%.
  • Show big deals on a separate line: that way you see both the trend and the deal.
Story In a 1954 book, Darrell Huff describes a real-estate agent. To a buyer he says the neighbourhood's average income is $15,000: a mean pushed up by a few millionaires with weekend homes. A year later, arguing that the neighbours cannot afford a tax rise, he gives the same area an "average" income of $3,500. Now it is the median. Both numbers are true. Source: Darrell Huff, "How to Lie with Statistics" (1954), chapter 2, "The Well-Chosen Average".

  • Median

    The middle value: line everyone up in order, and half are below it, half above.

  • Mean (average)

    The sum of all values divided by how many there are: what everyone would get if it were shared equally.

2

The best stores are the smallest

"Here are the top 10 stores by conversion. Shall we roll their practices out across the chain?"

Before visiting the leaders to learn from them, it is worth checking what such a ranking can show at all.

The chain has 100 stores. Conversion is the share of visitors who bought: 5 purchases per 100 visitors is 5%. The ranking is built on one month's conversion.

The stores differ a lot in size. The smallest get about 200 visitors a month, the largest almost 20,000. Small ones, under 1,000 visitors, make up a third of the chain.

To test the ranking itself, take a case with no better or worse stores: all 100 work equally well, and in each one 5 visitors in 100 truly buy.

Which stores will end up in the month's top 10 by conversion?

Half right. Small stores do take the top, but the bottom too: their conversion jumps both ways. Large stores stay near 5% and are rarely at either end. Check below.A store with 200 visitors expects 10 purchases a month. Four extra or missing purchases, and its conversion is already 7% or 3%. A store with 20,000 visitors expects 1,000 purchases, and to move the same two points it would need 400 extra, so it is almost always near 5%. That is why both ends of the ranking go to small stores. The smaller the group, the more its rate wobbles around the true one. This is the law of large numbers, and the typical size of the wobble is called the standard error. Four times fewer people make the wobble twice as large.

  • Both the top 10 and the bottom 10 are mostly the smallest stores, though all the stores are identical: usually 7–8 out of 10, while only a third of the chain is small. Their place was set by the number of visitors, not by the quality of their work. Next month they would swap places.
  • Before learning from the leaders of a ranking, look at the size of each. If both the top and the bottom are small, the ranking shows noise.
  • Show the count next to the rate, and judge small units over a longer period or together.
Story In the late 1990s, people in the US noticed that many of the best schools in the rankings were small, and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation began funding the break-up of large schools into small ones. The statistician Howard Wainer looked at all 1,662 schools in Pennsylvania: among the 50 best on fifth-grade tests there were four times as many small schools as chance would give. But there were too many among the 50 worst as well. In 2005 the foundation moved away from the strategy. Source: Howard Wainer, "The Most Dangerous Equation", American Scientist 95(3), 2007.

  • Law of large numbers

    The more repetitions, the closer the share of an outcome gets to its true probability.

  • Standard error

    How far an average or a rate computed from a sample usually misses the truth: σ divided by the square root of the sample size.

3

Manager of the quarter

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 2, “The best stores are the smallest”

"The best managers of Q1 got a bonus and sold less in Q2. Did the bonus make them lazy?"

To blame the bonus, you need to know what would have happened without it. So picture the same department with no bonuses paid and nothing changed in the work.

The department has 40 managers. In Q1 the department as a whole hit 100% of plan on average, and the best five hit 124%.

What does a quarter's result depend on? Partly on skill: experience, a client base, a way with people. Partly on luck: a big client signed this very quarter, while another put the deal off to the next. As in chapter 2, luck here is ordinary noise. Suppose skill and luck matter about equally.

What will these five show in Q2, with no bonus at all?

Not all the way to the average: the skill is real, and it stays. Only the part that luck gave rolls back, roughly half the lead. Check below.Whoever reaches the very top usually had luck on top of skill. So in the five's 24-point lead, when skill and luck matter equally, on average half comes from skill and half from luck. Skill carries into Q2, luck does not: 12 points remain, about 112%. This is regression to the mean, and the bonus has nothing to do with it. Check below.About 112%. The worst five "improve" just the same, with no effort at all. If they were sent to a training course right then, the course would look brilliant. Anything done to people who just showed an extreme result (a bonus, a reprimand, coaching, a new system) will look stronger or weaker than it is. The only way to see this is to compare with a similar group that nothing was done to.

  • In Q2 the top five fall halfway back to the average on average, and the bottom five rise just as much. The bonus has nothing to do with it: when luck is half the story, this happens with no bonus at all. The more luck, the stronger the pull back.
  • Judge the effect of a bonus, a training or a programme for the "worst" against a similar group without it: regression acts on both.
  • Pick the best over several quarters, not one: over several quarters luck partly evens out.
Story Daniel Kahneman tells of an instructor of Israeli Air Force pilots. He was sure that praise does harm: a cadet praised for a superb manoeuvre flies worse the next time, while one who was shouted at does better. Kahneman explained that the instructor was seeing regression to the mean: after an exceptionally good attempt the next one is almost always closer to ordinary, whatever you say. Source: Daniel Kahneman, "Thinking, Fast and Slow" (2011), chapter 17.
When a measure becomes a target A bonus for hitting the plan has one more trap besides regression. The economist Charles Goodhart noticed that a figure people start to push on stops showing what it used to show. A manager paid on the quarter's plan moves deals from one quarter to the next, gives discounts at the end of the period or sells what the client does not need. The number grows, the business does not. The best-known example is Wells Fargo, which set staff targets for the number of products per customer. In 2016 US regulators found that employees may have opened up to 1.5 million deposit accounts and 565,000 credit cards without customers' consent, and fined the bank $185 million in total. What to do: put next to the target the measure it must not spoil (the plan and returns, the number of products and their use), and change from time to time what exactly you reward. How this looks with product metrics is covered in the course "Does the feature work?". Source: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, consent order against Wells Fargo Bank, 8 September 2016.

4

Better in every segment

"The partner channel converts at 4.8%, search ads at 8.5%. Shall we cut the partners?"

At first sight the comparison is fair. Each channel brought 5,000 visitors in a month. 240 of the partner visitors bought, and 425 of the ad visitors.

But visitors differ. Suppose new ones, on the site for the first time, mostly look around and rarely buy, while returning ones have bought before, know what they want and buy often.

So the analyst recalculates each channel's conversion separately: among new visitors and among returning ones.

Can the partner channel turn out better among both new and returning visitors?

Partners bring 4,000 new and 1,000 returning visitors, ads the other way round: 1,000 new and 4,000 returning. In each group partners are better: 3% vs 2.5% among new visitors and 12% vs 10% among returning ones. But the partners' 240 purchases are 120 from new and 120 from returning visitors, while the ads' 425 come mostly from returning visitors, of whom they have four times as many. The overall numbers compare the traffic mix, not the channels. This is Simpson's paradox. Move the traffic mix below.It can, in both groups at once. But new visitors rarely buy, and they are most of the partner traffic, while the ads have four times as many returning visitors. The overall numbers compare the traffic mix, not the channels. Two channels, 5,000 visitors each

  • The partner channel is better in both groups. Its overall conversion is lower only because it brings new people, and they buy less often. Cutting it means losing exactly the new customers.
  • When groups are compared by a single rate, ask whether their mix is the same: new and returning, regions, devices, large and small customers.
  • Compare within like groups, and then decide separately how much of each group you want.
Story In autumn 1973 the University of California, Berkeley received 12,763 applications to its graduate school. 44% of men and 35% of women were admitted, which looked like discrimination. But within departments women were not admitted less often; overall, slightly more often. Women more often applied to departments that were hard for anyone to get into, with long queues for each place. Source: P. J. Bickel, E. A. Hammel, J. W. The chart that lies

"Sales have soared this year, just look at the chart. Shall we raise next year's target?"

The height of a bar is the number, so bars must start at zero. A line chart shows change rather than size, and it may start above zero if that is plainly visible. The second trap is quieter: the author chooses which quarter to start from. Sales nearly always have seasons, and a window that starts at the weakest quarter will show growth almost every time.

  • Over the year, sales grew 5%, not six times. And the 12-quarter window shows the rest: Q4 is the highest quarter every year, and this one is 7% below last year's. Over all of 2025, sales fell 4%. This slide is no reason to raise the target.
  • Read the axis labels first, then look at the height. Redraw bars that don't start at zero from zero.
  • Ask why the chart starts exactly here, and compare with the same quarter last year.
  • Two lines on two axes: the author picks the scale of each axis, so "moving together" can be drawn for almost any two series.
Story In 2014 a news chart showed gun deaths in Florida before and after the "Stand Your Ground" law. Its vertical axis was flipped, with zero at the top. The line after the law went down, and readers saw a fall, though over the next two years deaths rose by about 50%. Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West discuss this chart among other examples of misleading axes. Source: C. Bergstrom, J. West, "Calling Bullshit", "Misleading axes on graphs", callingbullshit.org; the book "Calling Bullshit" (2020), the chapter on data visualisation.

So far we assumed everyone is in the data. The second part is about who got into the data and who is missing.

Two new situations on the ideas of part 1 · optional, nothing depends on it

A ranking of a chain's 30 coffee shops by app ratings. First place goes to a shop that opened a month ago: 9 reviews, an average of 4.9. What does this most likely mean?

Nine reviews wobble a lot: a few fives in a row put a shop at the top, a few threes at the bottom. See where it is once it has a hundred reviews (chapter 2).

Part 2 · a few numbers

Who got into the data

A report counts only those it sees: who answered, who stayed, who passed a filter, who came at the peak hour. Four chapters about the people missing from the data and how they change the conclusion. There are a few more numbers, but everything can be counted on your fingers.

6

92% satisfied

"92% of customers are satisfied. Can we report that?"

The number is real: a survey popped up in the app, and 92% of the answers say "satisfied". Before reporting it, it is worth asking whose answers these are.

Every customer saw the survey, and 7.6% of them answered, about one in thirteen. The rest closed the window.

Who answers more readily? In studies of satisfaction surveys, unhappy people more often stay silent: the higher the satisfaction, the more people answer. Suppose 10 in 100 satisfied customers answer, and only 2 in 100 unhappy ones, so the satisfied answer five times as readily.

How many of all customers are satisfied, not just of those who answered?

Let's count on 10,000 customers, 7,000 of them satisfied, that is 70%. 10% of them answer: 700 people. Of the 3,000 unhappy ones, 2% answer: 60. That is 760 answers (7.6%), and 700 of them say "satisfied": the same 92%. The survey hears the satisfied five times louder, so 70% turns into 92%. 7

Nobody minds the price

"We asked customers who have been with us a year: only 12% complain about the price. Can we raise prices?"

The survey asked people who have been with the company a year. To see what those 12% mean, let's go back a year, to the people who arrived then.

A year ago 10,000 people subscribed. Some found the price too high, others found it fine.

Since then, some people cancel every month. Those who find it expensive leave more often: 15% of them each month. Of the rest, only 3% leave each month.

After a year, everyone who stayed was asked, and 12% of them complain about the price.

What share of those 10,000 who arrived a year ago found the price too high?

Of those who find it expensive, only 14% remain after 12 months: 85% stay each month, and 0.85 to the 12th power is 0.14. Of the rest, 69% remain. If 4,000 of the 10,000 found it expensive at the start, 569 of them remain after a year, and 4,163 of the other 6,000. That is 4,732, and 569 of them are the same 12%. The price is the main reason people left, but the survey hears only those who stayed. This is survivorship bias. Check below.About 40%. 8

Pricier means better?

"Among our top sellers, the pricier the product, the higher its rating. Customers believe pricier is better. Shall we raise prices?"

The link in the report is real. But the report is built not on all products in the market, only on the top sellers, the ones customers actually buy.

How does a product get there? Suppose a buyer looks at both the price and the rating, and takes what is worth its money. Then the top sellers are the products with good value for money.

Does such a report say anything about buyers believing "pricier is better"? Let's test it on a market where price and rating are not linked at all: among all 400 products, the pricey ones are rated the same on average as the cheap ones.

What will a report built only on the top sellers show?

A pricey product passes the filter only if its rating is high, while a cheap one passes with an average rating too. So in the top, pricier products are better on average, though in the market there is no link. This is Berkson's paradox. Check below.It will show pricier products rated higher. The same goes for hiring. If you hire candidates whose interview and test-task scores add up to a high total, among those hired the two scores will look opposed: whoever shone at the interview is weaker at the test. Among all candidates there may be no such link at all.

  • In the top sellers, pricier products have higher ratings, though in the market price and rating are unrelated. The filter itself created the link, so this report is no ground for raising prices in the hope that "pricier is better". The stricter the filter, the stronger the false link.
  • A link in a selected group (top sellers, hired candidates, customers who reached checkout) says nothing about the link among everyone. Ask what the group was selected on.
  • If the selection depends on both things you compare, a link is almost guaranteed to appear.

9

A half-empty gym that feels packed

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “The average order is up 24%”

"The gym's average occupancy is 30%. Why do members complain it is crowded?"

The report isn't lying, and neither are the members. Let's see where each of the two numbers comes from.

The gym holds 100 people and is open 16 hours a day. The turnstiles know how many people are inside each hour. Add these numbers up over the day, divide by 16 hours, and you get 30 people in an average hour. That is the 30% occupancy.

But people don't come evenly. In the daytime there are 8–15 people in the gym, and in the evening, after work, 55–80.

And it is people who complain, not hours. Each visitor sees how many people are in the gym while they work out.

How many people does an average visitor see around them?

An hour with 80 people in the gym is 80 visitors who see a crowd. An hour with 8 people is only 8 who see an empty gym. Almost half of all visits (46%) fall in three evening hours with 65–80 people in the gym. So the average over visitors is weighted by the crowd, and it comes out at about 50, not 30. Both numbers are true: one is about the gym, the other about the people. Check below.About 50. An hour with 80 people is 80 visitors who see a crowd, and an hour with 8 people is only 8 who see an empty gym. The annual engagement survey: 71% engaged against 64% last year. Over the year, one in five people left the company. What do you find out first?

The app and big spenders

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 4, “Better in every segment”

"Customers with the app spend 2.3 times more. Shall we give a ₴500 bonus to everyone who installs it?"

The bonus costs ₴500 a person, so the whole decision rests on one question: how much extra money will a person bring who installs the app for the bonus?

The report splits customers into two groups. Those with the app spend ₴4,130 a quarter on average, the rest ₴1,810. Hence the "2.3 times".

When two numbers move together, like the app and spending, it is called correlation. And one more detail from the same report: the app is installed more often by regular customers, and rarely by occasional buyers.

How much will one new app user add per quarter?

The report can't tell that either. The app may help a little, and in our model it adds 5% to spending. How do you find the real effect? Offer the bonus to a random half of customers and compare the two halves. A random split makes the groups alike in their mix, so the difference between them is the effect of the bonus. This is an A/B test, and its own traps have a course of their own, "A/B testing paradoxes".

How to judge the impact of a new product feature when an experiment is impossible is the subject of a separate course for product teams, "Does the feature work?".

  • When you hear "those who do X have more Y", ask: who usually does X? Is there one cause behind both?
  • Split into like groups. If the gap melts within the groups, it came from the mix, as in chapter 4.
  • Only an experiment with a random split checks a cause reliably.
Story For decades, observational studies showed that women taking hormone replacement therapy after menopause had less heart disease: in a large study of nurses the risk was 44% lower. The randomised Women's Health Initiative trial with 16,608 women found the opposite: 29% more coronary heart disease with the hormones, and the trial was stopped early. The women who chose the therapy were healthier to begin with. Source: M. J. Stampfer et al., New England Journal of Medicine 325, 1991; Writing Group for the WHI Investigators, JAMA 288(3), 2002.

Who made the sale

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 10, “The app and big spenders”

"Retargeting brought 1,300 sales for ₴108,000, a ROAS of 12. Shall we double the budget?"

A ROAS of 12 means every hryvnia of ads brought back 12 hryvnias of revenue: 1,300 sales with an average order of ₴1,000 is ₴1.3M for ₴108,000 spent. It sounds like the best ads in the company.

Retargeting is advertising for people who have already been on the site: they looked at a product, maybe put it in the cart, and left. The ad follows them around other sites and reminds them of the product.

How does the report know these sales came from the ads? It hands sales out to channels by a rule called attribution. The simplest and still common rule is the last click: the sale goes to whatever the person clicked last before buying.

In a month the site sold 2,100 orders, and the last click credited 1,300 of them to retargeting.

What happens to sales if you switch retargeting off?

The last click shows who was next to the purchase, not who caused it. Retargeting is seen by people who have already been on the site and are close to buying anyway, so it often becomes the last click before a purchase that would have happened without it. In our model 2,000 people would have bought without the ads, 1,200 of them clicked a retargeting ad on the way, and the ads truly added only 100 purchases: 1,200 + 100 = 1,300 in the report.   • The channels that see people right before buying are the most overstated: retargeting, ads on your own brand name, coupons and cashback.

The fraud filter fired

"The filter is 99% accurate and it blocked a transaction. So it's a fraudster?"

"99%" sounds like "wrong once in a hundred". But what exactly do those 99% mean?

A filter's accuracy is described by two numbers, one for fraudsters and one for honest customers. It catches 99% of fraudulent transactions. And it wrongly blocks 1% of honest ones: one in 100 ordinary purchases.

Fraud is rare: 0.1% of transactions, one in a thousand.

The security team sees only the blocked transactions, and the question for it is simple: how many of them are really fraud?

What share of the blocked transactions are really fraud?

Out of 100,000 transactions, 100 are fraud, and the filter catches 99 of them. But 1% of the 99,900 honest ones is another 999 alarms. That makes 1,098 blocked, and only 99 of them are fraud, that is 9%. Check below.Fewer than one in ten. Out of 100,000 transactions, 100 are fraud, and the filter catches 99 of them. Each cell of the grid is 1% of the alarms. The share of real ones among the alarms is called a conditional probability: the probability of fraud given that the filter fired. Bayes' theorem calculates it, but picturing 100,000 transactions, as above, is enough.

  • Only 9% of blocked transactions are fraud: 91 out of every 100 alarms are honest customers. 99% accuracy does not save the day here: fraudsters are so rare that even 1% errors among honest customers outnumber everyone caught. A second independent check raises the share of real ones to 91%.
  • About any filter (anti-fraud, scoring, churn prediction, a spam detector) ask: how many alarms are real? It depends on how rare the event is.
  • An alarm needs a second, independent check, not an automatic block.
  • Count the cost of both kinds of error: a fraudster missed and an honest customer blocked.

Are these numbers made up?

"Can you tell from the amounts alone that an expense report is made up?"

Sometimes you can, without checking a single receipt. The clue hides in the first digit of each amount.

Suppose a person making amounts up tries to make them varied and plausible: 2,300, 5,800, 9,100. Real invoices seem to look the same: the first digit can be anything from 1 to 9.

Let's check on real data. You take 10,000 supplier invoices: small ones for ₴50, large ones for ₴500,000, and everything in between.

What share of these amounts start with the digit 1?

Almost 30%, while fewer than 5% start with 9. This is Benford's law. For an amount starting with 1 to start with 2, it has to double, while going from 9 to 10 takes only 11% more. So amounts that span several orders of magnitude "live" on the one for a long time and rush past the nine. Made-up "varied" amounts don't repeat this. Before you decide on a report

Thirteen questions, one from each chapter. Not every one applies to every report, but each will save you from a costly decision at least once.

What the number is

  1. The mean next to the median and without the largest customers.
  2. The size next to the rate: small groups wobble more, and in rankings they sit at both ends.
  3. Was the group chosen on the same measure? Expect regression, and compare with a similar group without the change.
  4. Is the mix of the groups the same? Compare within like segments.
  5. The axis labels, not the height: bars from zero, the same quarter last year, why the window starts just there.

Who is in the data

  1. How many answered and whether the opinion could affect who answers.
  2. Who left before the measurement: ask those who leave, and look at cohorts from the start.
  3. What the group was selected on: a link inside a selection says nothing about everyone.
  4. An average over what: over hours and groups, or over people.

What follows from it

  1. Is there one cause behind both numbers? A cause is checked by an experiment. And how many pairs were tried before the link was found?
  2. The increase, not the attribution: budget only after a test with a control group.
  3. How many alarms are real: the rarer the event, the more false ones. Changes in shares in points, not only in percent.
  4. Do the numbers look real: Benford and amounts just under a limit as a reason to look closer.

Anna is ready to spend $6,000 on the car and not a dollar more. What should she bid?

"Winning" an auction is easy: just bid more than everyone else. The hard part is not losing by winning: not paying more than the lot is worth to you. The course follows Anna. She buys a car at a damaged-car auction, sells things on eBay, advertises her shop on Google and Meta, flies on an overbooked flight and bids on a tender. Each format gets one rule: how much to bid. Game theory and the paradoxes of behaviour explain them, and simulations on the page check them.

≈ 60 minutes, in 5-minute parts if you like; your progress is saved. The maths is school fractions and simple probability.

Part 1 · no formulas

Before the first bid

Three things that hold at any auction: what the lot costs you, how not to lose that limit during the bidding, and why winning is bad news in itself. Each chapter starts with a question: guess first, then check.

1

What the lot costs you

"I'm ready to pay 6,000 dollars for the car. So I bid up to 6,000?"

First, by eye: how much would you bid in Anna's place to stay within her $6,000 with the auction's fees?

Bids of $5,000 and $4,500 fit: with all fees they cost $5,838 and $5,308. But $5,500 would cost $6,363, and $6,000 would cost $6,908. Exactly where the limit lies, we'll work out below.

Anna is buying a car at IAA's online damaged-car auction in Canada, so all amounts here are in Canadian dollars. $6,000 is everything she's ready to hand the auction. But the bid is only the hammer price. On top, the buyer pays fees, and the biggest of them, the buyer fee, rises in steps with the price. There's also a pull-out fee, an online bidding fee and a 1% digital fee ($5 to $50).

What is the highest bid Anna can afford?

$5,162. At that bid the buyer fee is $600, pull-out $79, online bidding $109 and the digital fee $50, exactly $6,000 in all. A $5,400 bid would cost $6,238, and $6,000 would cost $6,908. The hammer price and what Anna actually pays

IAA Canada's public buyer fee schedule effective 13 July 2026, an online purchase, before taxes and shipping. If the car isn't collected in time, storage of $40 a day is added.

The fees are no trifle: 16% of the bid. And they rise in steps: once the bid passes $5,500 the buyer fee jumps, and from $6,000 the online fee goes up too. So the limit is worked back from the full amount to the bid, before the start, not during the bidding. On other platforms the numbers differ, but the principle is the same: auction houses charge a buyer's premium, and eBay and ad systems have their own fees and taxes.

  • Bid limit = what you're ready to pay in all minus every fee, shipping and tax.
  • Work it out before the start, from the platform's fee table: there's no time during the bidding.

When the fight is no longer about the lot

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “What the lot costs you”

"I spent three days choosing this car and paid for an inspection. Am I really going to give it up over a mere 200 dollars?"

Anna set her limit in advance (chapter 1). Before the auction she paid $150 for an inspection and a vehicle history report. The live auction is on, and two bidders are left: her and one other buyer. The bid is already $200 above her limit, with a few seconds to go.

Do those $150 justify raising the limit?

The inspection is paid for whether she wins or loses, so it doesn't make the car worth more. Stop or raise once more

Money you can't get back is called a sunk cost. It weighs on anyone who has waited a long time for something, and at an auction it weighs double: the rival thinks the same way. Each next step seems small ("another $100 and it's mine"), but together the steps add up to a sum neither of the two meant to pay. Bidding where both are long past their limits and neither gives in is called escalation.

  • Write your limit down before the auction and don't revise it during: new information about the lot is a reason, excitement and "I've put so much in" are not.
  • Costs that stay whatever the outcome don't affect how much to bid.
  • Proxy bidding with a limit (chapter 4) protects you from yourself: in the last seconds the decision is already made.

The dollar auction

A one-dollar bill is up for auction. Both top bidders pay, but only the winner gets the dollar. How much does the seller usually collect?

In the dollar auction both leaders pay, so escalation has no natural stop. Martin Shubik, who described the game in 1971, wrote that total payments of 3 to 5 dollars for one dollar are not uncommon. Max Bazerman and Don Moore sold $20 bills this way for years, and the bidding usually ended between $20 and $70. At an ordinary auction the loser pays nothing, but the mechanics in your head are the same.

Two traps for those already caught up in it

The penny auction. Here every bid is paid for, win or lose. A bid costs, say, $0.50, raises the price by just 1 cent and adds a few seconds to the timer. A $100 item "went for $30": sounds like a bargain. But a $30 price means 3,000 bids, that is $1,500 of paid bids, and together the site took $1,530 for a $100 item. This is no longer an ordinary auction where the loser pays nothing; it's the mechanics of the dollar auction problem: each new step seems cheap, because the earlier bids are already spent.

The more bidders, the surer it is that one of them has badly overestimated the lot, and the surer it is that this one wins. So winning is news in itself: "everyone else valued the lot below me". Experienced buyers take that news into account before bidding and shade their estimate more, the more rivals there are. With 10 bidders in this example, breaking even would mean lowering the estimate by about $1,300.

  • The winner's curse works where the lot is worth roughly the same to everyone, but its value is known only roughly: damaged cars, land, licences, tenders (chapter 12).
  • The more bidders, the more you should shade your estimate before bidding.
  • If the lot's value is purely personal (you just like that colour), the curse weakens: then the limit from chapter 1 decides.

A game: six sealed bids against five bots

Each round brings a new flood car: it's worth the same to everyone, somewhere from $8,000 to $12,000. You see only your own estimate, which can be off by up to $2,000 either way. Five bots have their own estimates and are careful: they bid $1,000 below them. The highest bid wins and pays the next one, as with proxy bidding.

The session table is kept only in your browser. Over the long run, a million rounds, bidding your estimate wins 42% of rounds and loses $142 on average on each car won, that is $59 a round. Bidding $1,500 below your estimate wins only 7.5% of rounds but makes $38 a round on average.

Story The term was coined by engineers at the oil company Atlantic Richfield. Part 2 · fractions and simple probability

The rules change the best bid

The five formats you meet most often: proxy bidding, a fixed end, a sealed envelope, a reserve price and a falling price. Each gets one rule: how much to bid relative to your limit.

4

Automatic bidding on eBay: bid your limit

"I'll bid a bit less than I'm willing to pay. Maybe it'll come out cheaper?"

Anna is looking for a camera lens on eBay. It's worth $100 to her. Such lenses sell for anything from $50 to $150, so the maximum of her one serious rival can be anywhere in that range. eBay uses automatic bidding: you enter the highest price you're willing to pay, and eBay raises the bid for you in minimum steps, but never above your limit. So the winner pays roughly the rival's maximum plus one step.

What maximum should she enter?

The price Anna pays is set by the rival, not by her maximum. A lower maximum doesn't make winning cheaper, it only loses lots where the rival went to $90–100 and Anna would have gained. Proxy bidding turns open bidding into a second-price auction: the highest maximum wins and pays the next one. In such an auction an honest maximum is best whatever the rivals do. Your bid only decides whether you win; what you pay is decided by others. So "how much should I bid" comes down to "what is the lot worth to me", which chapter 1 answers.

  • An auction with proxy bidding (eBay, live car auctions with a preliminary bid): enter your true limit, no less and no more.
  • A maximum below your value saves nothing; it only loses profitable lots.

Sniping: why bid in the last seconds

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 4, “Automatic bidding on eBay: bid your limit”

"If proxy bidding bids for me anyway, why do people wait for the last seconds?"

The same lens, Anna's limit $100. This time the rival behaves differently: he'd pay up to $120, but doesn't enter it all at once. He started at $52 and adds $10 every time he's outbid. Let the bid step be $1: on eBay it depends on the lot's price.

When does it pay Anna to enter her $100 in the last seconds rather than right away?

An early bid gives the rival time to "grow" to his $120, and he'd win. A late bid leaves him no time to reply, and Anna wins for $53. But if every bid in the last minute extends the auction, everyone has time, and sniping gains nothing. Switch the options below.When the rival raises step by step and the end is hard. How the bidding ends

    Roth and Ockenfels compared eBay, where an auction ends at a time fixed in advance, with Amazon's auctions of those years, where every late bid pushed the end back. On eBay the last bid came in the final 5 minutes in 40% of computer auctions and 59% of antiques auctions, on Amazon in about 3%. And experienced eBay buyers bid later than novices, while on Amazon they bid earlier. Sniping isn't a trick of a few people; it's a response to the ending rule.

    The rules change. eBay has added "extended bidding": any bid in the last 60 seconds resets the clock to 60 seconds. In Australia all auctions have ended this way since 17 August 2026, and in the UK the rule is being tested in some categories. Before planning a late bid, check the ending rules on the lot page.

    • A hard end: a late bid can save money, because it gives no time to those who raise step by step. But still bid your limit, no more.
    • An extending end: sniping is pointless, enter your limit as a proxy bid (chapter 4).
    • Don't raise step by step yourself: that's how you set the price up for whoever waits for the last seconds.
    Sources A. E. Roth, A. Ockenfels, "Last-Minute Bidding and the Rules for Ending Second-Price Auctions: Evidence from eBay and Amazon Auctions on the Internet", American Economic Review 92(4), 2002; eBay UK help on extended bidding; eBay Australia's announcement of extended bidding, 2026.

    A sealed envelope: shade your bid at first price

    This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 4, “Automatic bidding on eBay: bid your limit”

    "Here everyone submits one bid in an envelope, and the winner pays their own. Should I write it honestly?"

    A company is selling off its vans by sealed envelopes: the highest bid wins and pays what it wrote. That's a first-price auction. To each buyer a van is worth between $0 and $10,000, any amount equally likely, and to Anna it's worth $8,000. There is one rival, and he's sensible too.

    What should Anna write in the envelope?

    Half her value. An honest $8,000 gives zero: win, and she pays exactly what the van is worth. Each thousand shaved off adds profit when Anna wins but makes winning rarer.     The seller's view: a reserve price

    This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 4, “Automatic bidding on eBay: bid your limit”chapter 6, “A sealed envelope: shade your bid at first price”

    "I'm selling an old camera. Should I set a hidden minimum price? What if then nobody buys?"

    Now Anna is selling. The camera just sits around, so it's worth nothing to her. There are two buyers, and to each it's worth between $0 and $100, any amount equally likely. It's an auction with proxy bidding, so the buyers bid honestly (chapter 4). On eBay a seller can set a reserve price: it's hidden, and buyers only see whether it has been met. If not, the lot isn't sold.

    Anna sets a reserve price of $50. What happens to her average revenue?

    It rises from about $33 to $42. True, in a quarter of cases both buyers are below $50 and there's no sale. But more often the reserve works differently: one buyer is high and the other low, and without a reserve the first would pay pennies after the second, while with it he pays $50.     A reserve is the seller's own bid: it becomes one more "bidder" and lifts the price when there's no real competition. It helps most when there are few buyers. With four buyers the same $50 reserve raises revenue only from $60 to $61.25: the competitors already hold the price up. And extra buyers beat any reserve: from two to four buyers revenue rises from $33 to $60.

    • Selling with few buyers: set a reserve, even if the lot sometimes goes unsold.
    • The seller's biggest lever is the number of buyers: photos, a description, a good ending time.
    • Buying: "reserve not met" means the seller values the lot above the current bid. Don't stretch your limit to meet it.

    Four auctions

    Four smart buyers, four auction formats: first price, second price, English and Dutch. Which brings the seller the most on average?

    Without a reserve the seller doesn't care which format to choose. First price, second price, English and Dutch auctions with sensible buyers give the same revenue on average: $33.3 with two buyers and $60 with four. The difference is only in the spread: with two buyers first-price revenue deviates from the average by about $11.8, and second-price by $23.6. So the seller picks the format for convenience and earns on the number of buyers and the reserve.

    • Variance and standard deviation

      A measure of spread: how far values typically stray from the mean. The standard deviation is its square root, in the same units.

    "The price drops every second. Press "Stop" as soon as it reaches my price, or wait?"

    In a Dutch auction the price doesn't rise, it falls: a clock lowers it step by step, and the first to press "Stop" buys the lot at the current price.     Search ads: first place isn't always the best deal

    This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 4, “Automatic bidding on eBay: bid your limit”

    "I want my shop to be first in search results. Should I raise my bid to the max?"

    When someone searches on Google, the ad slots above the results are auctioned. A slot isn't decided by the bid alone: Google computes an Ad Rank from six factors, among them the bid, the quality of the ad and the landing page, thresholds and the search context. And you pay, in Google's words, only what's minimally needed to clear the thresholds and beat the next advertiser. So it's an auction like the second price, but for several slots at once.

    A simplified example where all ads are equally good. There are two slots: the top one gets 200 clicks a day, the second 199. There are three advertisers: a click is worth $10 to Anna and $4 and $2 to the other two. Each pays per click the bid of the one just below. The rivals bid honestly, their value of a click.

    Does it pay Anna to lower her bid and drop to the second slot?

    Yes. At the top Anna pays $4 a click and earns (10 − 4) × 200 = $1,200 a day. In the second slot she pays $2 and earns (10 − 2) × 199 = $1,592.     So in such an auction (called the generalised second price) an honest bid isn't always best, unlike chapter 4. Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz showed in 2007 that honest bids aren't an equilibrium here, and advertisers really do bargain over the price of an extra click. And the 2024 court ruling in the US case against Google described the search ad auction as a "classic second-price auction, with modifications", among them "pricing knobs" with which Google tunes what advertisers pay.

    Ads on Meta: the bid is just one of three factors

    "A competitor bids twice as much as I do. Do I even stand a chance?"

    On Facebook and Instagram each impression is auctioned too. According to Meta, the ad with the highest "total value" wins, and that's made up of three factors: the bid, an estimate of how likely the person is to take the desired action, and the ad's quality.     Anna's. An impression of her ad is worth $4 × 3% = 12 cents on average, the competitor's $10 × 1% = 10 cents. The ad system would rather show what it earns on more often.     Let the algorithm bid

    This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 9, “Search ads: first place isn't always the best deal”chapter 10, “Ads on Meta: the bid is just one of three factors”

    "I don't want to think about the bid every time. I'll set a target of $20 per order, and the system will bid $20?"

    Anna no longer bids by hand. In Google Ads she picks a target cost per acquisition (tCPA): how much she's willing to pay on average for one order. From there the system bids in every auction itself. In Google's words, it uses this target to set a bid based on the likelihood of the ad to convert. So before every search the system predicts what share of those who click will buy. Anna advertises bags, and her target is $20 per order.

    For the search "leather bag" the system predicts that 2% of those who click will buy. Roughly what will it bid per click?

    The bid per click in different searches

    The simplest form of the rule: bid = target × prediction. Google doesn't publish the exact formula, and it also adjusts the bid for device, location and other signals.

    So the algorithm doesn't "bid the target"; it splits it across clicks: a lot for a search where people often buy, pennies for a search where they only read. Google warns plainly that some conversions will cost more than the target and some less, while on average the system aims at it. Hence two traps. If something extra counts as a "conversion", such as a view of the cart page, the prediction is inflated and the algorithm overpays for every click. And if the target is too low, the bids come out small and Anna simply loses most auctions.

    Target ROAS works the same way, except that instead of the price of an order it uses its value: bid ≈ expected order value × prediction / target ROAS. A $50 order, a 2% prediction, a 400% target: 50 × 0.02 / 4 = $0.25 a click. Then 50 clicks cost $12.50 and bring one $50 order, exactly 400%.

    A daily budget is an average

    The second thing the algorithm does is spread the budget. In Google a daily budget is an average: on a good day a campaign may spend up to twice the daily budget, but over a month no more than 30.4 daily budgets (30.4 is the average number of days in a month). So overspending on some days is evened out on others. Below is an example month with fluctuating demand; how exactly Google spreads money between days, it doesn't publish.

    At Meta the same question is settled by the bid strategy. The cost per result goal (cost cap) is the analogue of tCPA: Meta tries to keep the average cost per action near the one you set, but warns that this is not guaranteed. The bid cap is a hard ceiling in every auction, like your limit from chapter 4; in reports you'll see not the bid but a lower cost. And the minimum ROAS is the analogue of target ROAS.

    • Count only a real order as a conversion: extra "conversions" inflate the prediction and the bids.
    • Set the target from your limit (chapter 1): what an order brings you, not what you'd like to pay. Too low a target means lost auctions.

    Reverse auctions: whoever asks less wins

    This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 3, “The winner's curse”chapter 6, “A sealed envelope: shade your bid at first price”

    "The airline asks how much it would take for me to fly later. How much should I ask?"

    Airlines sell a few more tickets than seats, because some people don't show up. When everyone does, someone has to stay behind. In 1968 the economist Julian Simon proposed not to bump people at random but to hold an auction: volunteers write how much they want for a later flight, and those asking least get the money. The US regulator allowed this in 1978. According to press reports, Delta collects such bids already at online check-in and at kiosks, and accepts the lower ones first.

    Anna doesn't mind flying later if she gets $200 (a voucher for future flights). One volunteer is needed, and there are five candidates including her. Every other one's minimum is any amount from $100 to $600, and they also ask sensibly.

    How much should Anna ask?

    Moderately more. It's the first price turned inside out (chapter 6): asking your minimum is like bidding your value, zero gain. Asking a lot means losing to other passengers more often.     The airline's ceiling is the compensation it would have to pay a passenger bumped involuntarily. In the US since 22 January 2025 that's 200% of the one-way fare, capped at $1,075, if a domestic flight arrives 1–2 hours later, and 400%, capped at $2,150, if later than that. And in 2017, after the scandal of a passenger dragged off a United flight, Delta, according to press reports, let supervisors offer volunteers up to $9,950 instead of $1,350.

    A tender: the winner's curse in reverse

    Anna, as a supplier, bids on a public tender: the buyer takes the cheapest offer. That's a reverse auction too, and the winner's curse from chapter 3 lives in it, only inside out. The cost of the job is the same for everyone, but each estimates it with an error, and the lowest price comes from whoever underestimated it most. So a won tender is a reason to recount your costs.

    In Ukraine this is how Prozorro, the fully open public procurement system where every tender can be seen online, works. Its auction runs in three real-time rounds, bidders see each other only as "Bidder 1, 2…", and whoever offered the lowest price moves last in a round. There are guards against the winner's curse: a price far below the others' must be explained within one working day, and a winner who refuses to sign the contract loses the tender security. So a price that's too low costs money even if you spot the mistake in time.

    • A reverse auction where you're paid your own ask: ask more than your minimum, but the closer to it, the more competitors there are.
    • Know the buyer's ceiling: on an overbooked flight it's the compensation for involuntary bumping.
    • A tender: build in a margin for cost errors, the larger the more bidders there are.

    Auction or "Buy It Now": when to stop

    "Camera bags show up every day, and each is gone within a day. Take the first decent one or wait for a better one?"

    On eBay an auction lot can have a "Buy It Now" button, but it disappears after the first bid, and the lot carries on as an ordinary auction. Anna wants to buy a bag as cheaply as possible. Over 10 days 10 "Buy It Now" offers will appear, one a day, with prices in random order. She must decide on each at once: the next day it's gone. All she has are the prices she's already seen.

    Which strategy buys the cheapest of the ten more often?

    A checklist before you bid

    One or two questions from each chapter. Go through them before you press "Place bid".

    Before any auction

    1. My limit with all fees? The full amount minus fees, shipping and taxes.
    2. Is the limit written down before the start? Money already spent is no reason to raise it.
    3. Is every bid paid for, or a rival suspiciously persistent? A penny auction takes money from the losers too; a written limit guards against shill bidding.
    4. Is the lot worth the same to all, with an unknown value? Shade your estimate, more so with more bidders.

    The format

    1. Proxy bidding or second price? Bid your limit, no less and no more.
    2. A hard end or an extending one? A late bid helps only with a hard end.
    3. Do you pay your own bid? Write less than your value: (n − 1)/n in the simple case.
    4. Selling with few buyers? A reserve raises revenue; more buyers raise it more.
    5. Is the price falling? It's a first price: press "Stop" at a share chosen in advance.

    Platforms

    1. Search ads? Count the price of an extra click, not the slot.
    2. Ads on Meta? The action rate weighs as much as the bid.
    3. Does an algorithm bid? The target comes from your limit, a conversion is only a real order, the budget is a daily average.
    4. A reverse auction or a tender? Ask a bit above your minimum, keep a margin for errors.
    5. Do offers come one at a time? Look at some first, then take the first better one.

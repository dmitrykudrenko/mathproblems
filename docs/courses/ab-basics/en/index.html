A/B testing in plain words

The boss asks: "Do we ship B to everyone?"

An A/B test is a way to compare two versions fairly: half the people see A, half see B, and we count who bought. The idea is simple, but it comes with many questions. Why test when you can compare "before" and "after"? How many people do you need? What does "95% confidence" mean? When may you stop? The course answers eight such questions, one per chapter. There are no formulas: only counting ("6 purchases in 100"), a coin and a "Run it again" button. At the end comes an exam of six dashboards and an answer for the boss on this one.

≈ 30–40 minutes, in 5-minute parts if you like; your progress is saved.

This is the first step. When you want more, there is a course for those who run tests every week: "A/B testing paradoxes".

Part 1 · no formulas

How a test works

Four questions about why a test is built the way it is: two groups at the same time, a coin, and many people. Each chapter starts with a question: guess first, then check it on a live picture.

1

Why test if there is "before and after"?

"We redesigned the site on 1 November, and there were 30% more orders in November than in October. Isn't that proof?"

The simplest way to check a change is to compare the month before and the month after. In October an online shop got 4,500 orders. On 1 November a new design went live, and November brought 5,850 orders, 30% more.

How many orders did the new design add?

November is Black Friday and the start of gift shopping. Last year, with no redesign at all, orders rose 29% from October to November. So the season gave almost the whole +30%, and the design may have added a little, nothing, or even hurt. Switch on last year below.These numbers can't tell. From one month to the next everything changes at once: the season, the weather, ads, competitors' prices, the news. "Before and after" adds them all into one number and credits it to the new design. An A/B test does it differently: on the same days, half the people see the old site and half see the new one. The season, the weather and the ads are the same for both halves, so the difference between them comes from the design. The half with the old version is called the control group.

  • Last year, with no changes, November gave +29% on October; this year, with the new design, +30%. The season gave almost all of the increase. How much the design gave only a comparison with a control group in the same weeks can show.
  • "Before and after" shows everything that changed in that time, not just your change.
  • An A/B test compares two versions on the same days, so everything else is the same for both.

How to judge a change when a test is impossible is the subject of the course "Does the feature work?".

2

Why does a coin split the groups?

"The new cart is seen by those who clicked "Try the new version" themselves. They buy 2.5 times as often. Shall we switch it on for everyone?"

A shop gets 1,000 visitors a week. Among them are 200 regular customers who buy often and 800 newcomers who mostly just look. A "Try the new cart" button appeared on the site, and 100 people clicked it.

Of those who clicked, 13 in 100 bought. Among the other 900, only 5 in 100.

Is the new cart better than the old one?

Regular customers are keener to try new things. They are 60 of the 100 who clicked, and only 140 of the other 900. Regulars buy more often with any cart. In this example the new cart changes nothing at all, and the 13 vs 5 gap appears anyway. See below who is in which group.Unknown. Regular customers are keener to try new things. When people choose for themselves, different people end up in the groups, and the difference in purchases shows the people, not the versions. A coin knows nothing about people: regulars and newcomers land in A and B equally, with a little wobble. That is why in an A/B test each person's group is picked by chance, not by them and not by a manager.

  • When people chose their group, 13 vs 5 appeared with no effect of the cart at all: 60 of the 100 who clicked are regulars, against 140 of the other 900. When a coin splits them, both groups get about 100 regulars, and both buy about 6 in 100.
  • If people chose the version themselves, the difference shows who chose, not which version is better.
  • Groups must be split by chance: a coin, a lottery, the random generator in the testing tool.
Story In 1948 Britain's Medical Research Council tested streptomycin against tuberculosis. Which of the 107 patients got the drug was decided not by doctors but by random numbers in sealed envelopes. That way doctors could not, even unintentionally, give the drug to those who were more likely to recover anyway. It is counted among the first randomised trials in medicine. Source: Medical Research Council, "Streptomycin treatment of pulmonary tuberculosis", BMJ, 30 October 1948.

  • Sampling bias

    A sample some people get into more easily than others, so it describes everyone badly.

3

Can a difference come from nowhere?

"Pages A and B are identical. So where does a difference come from?"

The best way to understand a test is to run it on two identical pages. Such a check is called an A/A test. A coin splits the visitors, and both halves see the same page. On it, an average of 5 people in 100 buy.

Each group got 100 visitors.

How often will one group have at least 3 more purchases than the other, say 4 against 7?

In about 41 tests out of 100, one of the identical groups beats the other by 3 purchases or more. And 4 vs 7 on a dashboard looks like "B is 75% better". Purchases are separate people, and in a small group each extra buyer moves the number noticeably. Even when the versions are the same, the numbers in the groups differ: chance decides exactly who came and who bought. The fewer the people, the stronger this wobble. So a difference on a dashboard does not yet mean one version is better. The question is always the same: is this difference bigger than what chance alone gives?

  • Between identical groups of 100 people, a gap of 3 purchases or more happens in about 41 tests out of 100, and equal purchase counts in only 13. Such a gap says nothing about the versions.
  • There is a difference even between identical versions. Before celebrating, ask whether A against A could have shown such a gap.
  • Percentages on small groups mislead: 4 against 7 purchases is "+75%".

The A/A test: two identical pages

The same page is shown as "A" and as "B", converting at 5%, with 1,000 visitors each. How often will one of two identical pages "win" by 20% or more?

  • Law of large numbers

    The more repetitions, the closer the share of an outcome gets to its true probability.

4

How many people do you need?

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 3, “Can a difference come from nowhere?”

"We have a thousand visitors per version. Isn't that enough?"

Now let B really be better. On the old page A, 5 people in 100 buy; on the new page B, 6 in 100. For a business that is a lot: one extra purchase for every five.

The test runs until each version has been seen by 1,000 people. Then we compare where there are more purchases.

How often will the worse version A come out ahead?

A thousand people means on average 50 purchases on A and 60 on B. That is a gap of 10 purchases, while chance easily moves each number by 7–10 purchases either way. So in about 15 tests out of 100, the worse A is ahead. Change the number of people below.About 1 time in 7. A thousand people means on average 50 purchases on A and 60 on B. A test sees a difference only when it is bigger than the wobble of chance. And the wobble fades slowly: to make it half as big you need four times as many people. Hence a simple rule: the smaller the difference you want to notice, the more people you need. Half the difference means four times the people.

Exactly how many is what sample-size calculators work out, and there are free ones. They need two things: the current conversion rate and the smallest difference you want to notice. For 5 vs 6 in 100 it comes to about 6,500 people per version. Then the test will notice such a difference about 8 times in 10. What "notice" means is the next chapter.

  • When B truly gets 6 purchases in 100 and A gets 5, with 1,000 people the worse A is ahead in about 15 tests out of 100. With 100 people it is 32, with 10,000 almost never.
  • Work out how many people you need before the test starts, not after.
  • A small difference needs many people. If you don't have that many, test bolder changes or run longer.

The most dangerous equation

Disease is rarest in quiet rural counties. Clean air? Or something else?

  • Statistical power

    The probability that a test notices a real effect of a given size. A power of 80% means the test misses one working effect in five.

What you know now "Before and after" mixes the change with the season (1), so two versions are compared at the same time and a coin splits the groups (2). Even identical versions give different numbers (3), and to notice a real difference you need many people (4). The second part is about how a dashboard tells a real difference from a chance one, and where that method can be broken.

How to read the result

5

What does "95% confidence" mean?

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 3, “Can a difference come from nowhere?”

"The dashboard says: B is ahead, confidence 95%. Is that a guarantee?"

The testing tool knows how the numbers wobble (chapter 3) and compares B's lead with that wobble. The bigger the lead and the more people, the higher the "confidence". A winner is usually declared once it reaches 95%.

To see what this threshold promises, take identical pages again, 1,000 people on each.

A and B are identical. How often will the test still show "B ahead, confidence 95%" or more?

That is exactly what 95% means: if the versions were the same, chance would give such a lead for B less than 1 time in 20 tests. It is not a guarantee but an agreement about risk: about one "winner" in twenty among identical versions will be fake. Run 20 tests below.About 1 time in 20. The 95% threshold protects against chance, but only up to one in twenty. And the promise holds only if you play fair: the number of people is set in advance, the result is read once at the end, and one version is checked against one. The next three chapters are three ways to break that promise without noticing.

One more thing: 95% says nothing about the size of the gain. "B +27%, confidence 95%" does not promise +27% after launch (chapter 8). Some tools show a "chance to beat" instead of confidence; it is computed differently, and a chapter of the course for marketers covers it.

  • Among identical versions, "B ahead with 95% confidence" comes up in about 1 test in 20. That is the price of the threshold: one winner in twenty among identical versions is fake.
  • "95% confidence" means chance gives such a lead less than 1 time in 20. It is neither a guarantee nor the size of the gain.
  • The promise holds only if the number of people was set in advance and the result is read once, at the end.
Story Why 95% in particular? In 1926 the statistician Ronald Fisher, who worked with field trials at the Rothamsted agricultural station, wrote that he personally considered a result worth attention when chance would give it less than once in 20 times. It was his personal rule, not a law of nature. It stuck and now sits on most dashboards. Source: R. A. Fisher, "The Arrangement of Field Experiments", Journal of the Ministry of Agriculture 33 (1926).

  • p-value

    How often chance alone would produce a gap at least as big as yours if there were really no difference.

6

When may you stop a test?

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 5, “What does "95% confidence" mean?”

"The test is planned for 14 days, but on day three it is already at 95%. Why wait?"

The test is planned for 14 days, with 300 people seeing each version every day. The dashboard updates every morning, and every morning someone opens it. The agreement: as soon as confidence reaches 95%, the test stops and the winner is announced.

Take identical versions again, where there can be no winner.

How often will a fake winner appear within the 14 days?

In about 19 tests out of 100, four times as often as the threshold promises. Confidence wanders up and down every day, and each morning is a new chance to touch 95% by luck. Had the test not been stopped, most of these "winners" would have dropped back below by day 14. The 95% threshold is designed for one look at the end. Every daily check with the right to stop is one more try, and fake winners pile up. You may look at the dashboard: to spot a bug or a disaster. You may not stop the test because it has "already gone green".

  • With a check every morning, identical versions touch 95% at least once in about 19 tests out of 100, but on the planned day 14 in only 5. The fake winner most often appears in the first days, when there are still few people.
  • Write down in advance how many people and days the test will take, and read the result only then.
  • Test in whole weeks: different people buy on Monday and on Saturday.
  • If you really need to stop early, there are special methods that correct for peeking. The ordinary "95%" is not one of them.
Story In 2010 the developer Evan Miller published a short article, "How Not To Run an A/B Test". He showed that the habit of stopping at the first "significant" result turns the promised 5% of false wins into far more, and proposed a simple rule: fix the sample size in advance and don't stop earlier. Since then many testing tools have added protection against peeking. Source: Evan Miller, "How Not To Run an A/B Test", evanmiller.org, 2010.

Why not test 10 variants at once?

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 5, “What does "95% confidence" mean?”

"AI wrote 10 headlines. We test them all against the old one, and one won with 95% confidence. Do we take it?"

The new headlines are no better than the old one: on each, the same 5 people in 100 buy. Each headline is seen by 2,000 people, the old one by 2,000 as well, and each new one is compared with the old.

How often will at least one of the 10 equally good headlines "win" with 95% confidence?

In about 24 tests out of 100. Each headline has its own 1-in-20 chance to beat the old one by luck, and there are ten headlines. The chances add up, and a "winner" turns up in every fourth test, though no headline is better. Run it below.About 1 time in 4: in 24 tests out of 100. The same happens when one test's result is cut into pieces: mobile and desktop, new and returning, cities, days of the week. Each piece is one more try, and among a dozen pieces "B wins on Android in Kharkiv" is easy to find. The more tries, the surer one of them wins by luck.

  • Among 10 headlines that are no better than the old one, a "winner" appears in about 24 tests out of 100, not 5. One of many variants winning is not yet a win.
  • The more variants or segments, the more fake winners. Count how many tries there were.
  • Check a winner picked from many in a separate test: it against the old one, one on one.
  • Write down the segments you care about before the start, rather than searching after.
Story In the xkcd comic "Significant" (2011), scientists check whether jelly beans cause acne. Overall, no. Then they check 20 colours one by one, and green ones are "linked to acne" with 95% confidence. A newspaper runs a headline about green jelly beans, though one hit in twenty is exactly what chance should give. Source: Randall Munroe, xkcd #882, "Significant", 2011.

Why is the effect smaller after launch?

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 5, “What does "95% confidence" mean?”

"In the test B gave +20%, and after launch only +10%. Did someone make a mistake?"

This time B really is 10% better: 5 people in 100 buy on A, 5.5 on B. Each version is seen by 5,000 people, and the test is fair: the number of people was set in advance and the result is read once at the end. The team runs many such tests and ships those where B won with 95% confidence.

What gain, on average, do the tests show where B won?

About +21% on average, twice the truth. Not every test produces a winner, only one where chance pushed B upward: without that push, +10% on 5,000 people rarely reaches 95%. This is called the winner's curse: when you pick the best, you also pick the lucky ones. The fewer people in a test, the bigger the push needed to win, and the more the winner exaggerates. There is a second reason too: the novelty effect. Something new and bright draws attention for the first weeks, then people get used to it and the difference fades.

  • B wins in only about 30 tests out of 100, and these winners show about +21% on average instead of the true +10%. A smaller effect after launch is not a mistake here, just the usual price of picking winners.
  • A winner's gain in the test is usually inflated. Plan budgets and forecasts on a smaller number.
  • The more people in the test, the smaller the exaggeration.
  • To see the real effect, keep a small group without the change for a few weeks after launch.
Story At Microsoft, which runs thousands of online experiments a year, analysts go by "Twyman's law": any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong. Ron Kohavi, who led experimentation there for years, advises checking a surprisingly big win as carefully as a bug. Source: Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang, Ya Xu, "Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments" (2020), chapter 3.

✓

Before you believe an A/B test

Eight questions, one from each chapter. You can ask them of any dashboard without calculating anything yourself.

How the test is built

  1. Is there a control group on the same days? "Before and after" is not a test.
  2. Who split the groups? It should be chance, not the people and not a manager.
  3. Could A against A have shown such a gap? On small numbers, easily.
  4. Was the number of people worked out in advance? A sample-size calculator, before the start.

How to read the result

  1. 95% is a risk of 1 in 20, not a guarantee and not the size of the gain.
  2. Did the test reach its planned end? Don't stop as soon as it goes green.
  3. How many variants and segments were there? Check a winner picked from many separately.
  4. Expect a smaller effect after launch and keep a small group without the change.

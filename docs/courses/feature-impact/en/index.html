The report's conclusion: the feature doubles retention. Proposal: show it to everyone in a pop-up.

Reports like this get written after every launch. Does the feature really double retention? The course answers in three steps. First, with no formulas, we see why the usual feature numbers mislead: who uses the feature, what the share of people who tried it shows, what else changed that month. Then we learn to estimate impact without an A/B test: a staggered rollout, difference in differences, a threshold, matched groups, a long holdout. The third part covers cohorts, cannibalisation, totals and when to insist on an experiment. At the end comes an exam of six product reports and a verdict on this very "Saved searches" report.

≈ 1.5 hours, in 10–15 minute parts if you like; your progress is saved.

Already have a report on your feature? Can you trust this impact? A few questions about the launch give a list of risks and an estimation method.

The share of people still using the product after some time: day-30 retention of 40% means 40 of 100 new users are still there a month later.
Cohort
Everyone who arrived in one period, say signed up in March. Cohorts are compared with each other at the same age (chapter 11).

A percentage point, the difference between two percentages: from 30% to 32% is +2 pp, or +7% of 30%.
DAU/MAU
How many of the month's users come on an ordinary day: 20% means a monthly user visits about 6 days out of 30 on average (chapter 3).
Adoption
How many users used the feature at least once. It shows how many people saw it, not whether it helped (chapter 2).

One metric of value that decides, and a few guardrails that must not get worse: uninstalls, complaints, speed (chapter 3).
Holdout
A share of users deliberately left without the feature, so there is someone to compare with (chapter 10).
Natural experiment
A case where circumstances split people into groups almost as a test would: one country got the feature before another, a status starts at a threshold (chapters 6–8).
Part 1 · no formulas

Why feature numbers mislead

Five usual ways to show that a feature works, and why each of them can show success where there is none. Each chapter starts with a question: guess first, then check it on a live picture.

1

Those who use the feature stay twice as often

"Feature users retain twice as well. So the feature doubles retention?"

That "twice" comes up after many launches, and it sounds like a ready-made argument. But before trusting it, recall how people end up among the "feature users" at all.

Nobody hands a feature out at random. Users find it and turn it on themselves. And not everyone finds it equally: those who come in every day see more buttons and try more functions.

To check whether that "twice" says anything about the feature itself, take a feature that surely does nothing. The "Save search" button broke and saved nothing for a month, though it could be pressed as usual. Like any search feature, it was pressed mostly by people who search a lot.

A month later, how do those who pressed the broken button retain compared with the rest?

The button did nothing, so the gap is not from it. It was pressed mostly by those who search a lot, and they rarely leave anyway. 2

Tried ≠ helped

"41% of users tried the feature. Is that a success?"

41% is a nice number for a slide, but whether it is a success depends on where it came from and what happened next.

The share of people who tried a feature is counted first: it grows fast and is easy to show. But one can try it out of curiosity, because of a pop-up, or by accident. So the team cares about something else: how many people use the feature a month later.

At first the feature was quietly added to the menu. Within a week 9% of users tried it, and a month later 3 in 100 were using it. It is really needed by about 6 people in 100.

Then a pop-up at login was added that shows the feature to everyone. Now 41% tried it.

How many people will be using the feature a month after the pop-up?

A month later the feature is used by those who need it, and there are only 6 such people in 100: no pop-up makes it more than 6. From the menu about 60% of them found it, and a month later 3 in 100 remained. The pop-up shows the feature to everyone, so now almost all of those 6 find it, and about 5 remain a month later. Four and a half times as many people tried it, but only 1.6 times as many use it: the rest of the 41 who tried it did so out of curiosity and left.   • The pop-up lifts the share who tried from 9% to 41%, and the share still using it a month later from 3.2% to 5.1%. The first number grows fourfold, the second 1.6 times, and only the second speaks of value.
  • The share who tried measures how the feature was shown, not what it is. Compare it only across the same way of showing.
  • Look further down the funnel: how many came back in week two and how many use it a month later. Whether the feature changed the product's retention as a whole, these numbers don't tell either: that is part 2.
3

A metric that is easy to move

"DAU/MAU went from 20% to 30%. Do people get more from the product?"

To answer, you need to know what exactly moved this number. DAU/MAU is the share of monthly users who come in on an ordinary day. 20% means one in five comes in daily, 30% almost one in three.

One change moved it: a classifieds app added daily push reminders, and within a month DAU/MAU went from 20% to 30%.

The economist Charles Goodhart noticed that a figure people start to push on stops showing what it used to show. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern put it shorter: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

What does this rise in DAU/MAU say about the product's value to people?

A push brings a person into the app, and DAU grows even if they did nothing there. In a product the easiest things to move are counts of visits and minutes: DAU, sessions, time in the app. In the model below the pushes alone give those +10 pp of DAU/MAU, while contacts with sellers don't change at all, and uninstalls go up. To talk about value you need a metric of what people get: here, contacts with sellers. Levers that move metrics

Which metric should be primary for "Saved searches"?

  • Pushes lift DAU/MAU from 20% to 30% while contacts with sellers stay the same, and uninstalls grow. A real search improvement barely moves DAU and even cuts the minutes: people find what they need faster.
  • Before the launch, pick one primary metric that counts value for a person: a deal, a purchase, a task done per user. Keep the share who tried and the minutes as secondary.
  • Add two or three guardrails that must not get worse: uninstalls, push opt-outs, complaints, speed. A win in the primary metric with a failed guardrail is not a win.

4

Before and after the launch

"We launched the feature on 1 September, and September's conversion is 12% above August's. There's the effect."

That is how most launches get reported: compare the month before with the month after. It is simple, which is why it is the most common. Let's see what else happened in that time.

Conversion here is the share of visitors who bought. "12% above" is a relative difference: in August 3.7 in 100 bought, in September 4.2.

But September is not an ordinary month. Last year conversion also grew from August to September, by 6%, though there was no feature then. And this September an ad campaign ran too.

How much of these 12% did the feature itself give?

The season takes about 6 of these 12%, and the campaign gave part of the rest. How much is left for the feature, a plain "before and after" can't tell: it sees only the sum of everything that changed in the month.   • In this example the 12% are made of the season (+6%), the campaign (+3%), a new checkout from another team (+2%) and the feature itself (+1%). A "before and after" report merges them into one number.
  • Compare with the same period last year, not with the previous month: that shows the season.
  • Keep a shared calendar of changes: every team's releases, campaigns, prices, outages. Before crediting a metric's move to the feature, check what else happened that week.
  • Best of all is a group that lived without the feature in the same month: then the season and the campaign act on both groups alike (chapters 6–7).
5

Launched after the worst week

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 4, “Before and after launch”

"Retention fell to a one-year low, we rushed out a fix, and it recovered. The fix worked!"

It sounds like a perfect story: it fell, we fixed it, it recovered. But first let's see how a weekly metric behaves when nobody fixes anything.

Weekly retention here is the share of users who came back the next week. Usually it is about 40%. But even with no changes at all it wanders: each week brings slightly different people, weather, holidays. An ordinary week easily drifts 2 pp up or down.

One week retention fell to 36%, the lowest in a year. Alarm is greatest exactly when the metric is at its worst, and the team rushed out a fix. The next week it was 40% again.

What follows from this story about the fix?

The worst week of the year is mostly unlucky noise: out of 52 ordinary weeks one is bound to be the lowest. The next week is a new throw, and it is almost always closer to the usual 40%. In the model below a fix that does nothing "raises" retention by 4 pp on average: from 36% to 40%. To see the fix's share you need to compare with what would have happened without it.   • A fix that does nothing "adds" about 4 pp in a week on average: the worst week of a half year sits about 4 pp below normal, and the next week simply returns to it. The same happens with people taken into a programme after their worst month.

What you can do now You know that the feature's users differed before it (1), that the share who tried measures exposure, not value (2), that DAU and minutes are easy to move without value (3), that "before and after" mixes the season, campaigns and others' releases (4), and that after the worst week a metric comes back by itself (5). All five traps share one cure: a group of people who lived without the feature at the same time. The second part is about where to find such a group when there was no A/B test.
Part 2 · a few numbers

How to estimate impact without an A/B test

An A/B test is the cleanest way, but often there wasn't one: the feature has already gone to everyone, or it can't be shown to only some people. Then you look for a group that lived without the feature at the same time and resembled those who had it. Five ways, from the most reliable to the weakest, made of subtraction and averages.

A staggered rollout as a natural experiment

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 4, “Before and after launch”

"Android got the feature in March, iOS only in May. Is that bad for the analysis?"

On the contrary, for the analysis it is a gift, though it doesn't look like one at first.

Count in weeks of the year: the feature reached Android in week 9 and iOS only in week 18. For nine weeks in a row one platform has the feature and the other doesn't. Yet both live in the same season, under the same campaigns and holidays.

The platforms are not the same: even without the feature, weekly retention on iOS is higher, about 44% against 40% on Android.

What is the best way to estimate the feature's effect in the first weeks on Android?

iOS over the same weeks shows what would have happened to Android without the feature: holidays, campaigns, the season. In the model below the holidays fall in weeks 10–12, and "before and after" on Android gives about +4 pp. iOS without the feature grew by about 2 over the same weeks. The difference of the changes, about +2 pp, is the effect. Difference in differences

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 6, “A staggered rollout”

"In the country with the feature retention grew by 6 pp. But it also grew where there is no feature. So what is the effect?"

The answer hides in the second sentence: how much the country without the feature grew. Chapter 6 already counted this way; now let's take the method apart, since most estimates without an experiment rest on it.

You take the change in the group with the feature and subtract the change in the group without it over the same time. The second change is the season, campaigns and everything else that acted on both groups.

The feature launched in Poland, while Czechia stayed without it for now. Over the half year after the launch, retention in Poland grew from 30% to 36%, and in Czechia from 28% to 32%.

What is the feature's effect?

Poland grew by 6 pp, Czechia without the feature by 4. Those 4 pp would have happened in Poland too, so the feature gets 6 − 4 = 2 pp. But only if the countries moved in parallel before the launch. A threshold

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “The feature's users”

"Gold customers spend twice as much. Does gold status pay off?"

The "twice" is true here: gold customers spend $132 a month on average, the rest $64. The question is how much of it the status itself did.

Gold status starts at 1,000 points, and points pile up for those who buy a lot. So a comparison of "with it against without" again compares different people, as in chapter 1.

But right at the threshold a natural experiment appears: a customer with 995 points and one with 1,005 are almost the same, yet only the second has the status. Customers with 990–999 points spend $95 a month on average, those with 1,000–1,009 spend $99.

How much a month does gold status itself give?

Near the threshold the customers are almost the same, and the jump there is the status's effect: $99 − $95 = $4. That is less than a tenth of the $68 big gap.   • A narrow window gives an effect close to the true one, though noisy, as it holds few customers. The wider the window, the closer the estimate gets to the naive gap: ever more different customers enter the comparison.
  • A threshold works only if customers can't place themselves precisely around it. If points can be bought to jump over it, the keenest end up above it, and the comparison is unfair again. A check: the number of customers near the threshold should be about the same on both sides.
  • The effect at the threshold applies to customers near the threshold. For those with 300 points, the status may act differently.

Matched groups that still mislead

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “The feature's users”

"We matched each feature user with a non-user: the same plan, the same account age. Is the comparison fair now?"

Matching is the most common answer to chapter 1. If the feature's users differ from the rest, let's find each of them a similar twin without the feature and compare the pairs.

Matching uses what is in the data: plan, country, account age. Then a paying long-standing user is compared with a paying long-standing one, not with a newcomer on the free plan.

For this team, the naive comparison gave the feature's users +20 pp of retention. After matching on plan and account age the gap shrank to +8 pp.

What is the feature's true effect?

Matching removed the visible part of self-selection: about 12 of the 20 pp. But motivation, need and habits don't show in the data, and it is the motivated who turn the feature on more often and leave less often. That part stayed in the 8 pp, and how much of it is there is unknown, so +8 is only an upper bound. Matching: what the data can see and what it can't

  • In this model the feature adds +2 pp. Matching on plan and age gives +8, and only matching on motivation too, which the data doesn't have, brings it back to +2. Matching shrinks the error but doesn't tell how much of it is left.
  • A matched comparison is a hypothesis with an upper bound, not an effect. If the gap vanished after matching, that is good news; if it remains, it may still be self-selection.
  • The best variable to match on is the metric itself before the launch: the user's retention or activity in the month before the feature. It carries part of the invisible motivation.

10

A long holdout

"We show the feature to everyone, but want to know its effect over three months. How many people should we leave without it?"

This question has a calculation behind it, and it is simpler than it seems. A holdout is the simplest honest way to measure a feature you have already decided to ship: a small random share of users stays without it for a few months.

The smaller the share, the fewer people miss out on the feature. But the noisier the comparison, and a small effect gets lost in it.

Here there are 200,000 active users, and retention is 40%. The team wants to reliably detect an effect of 1 pp or more, that is 41% with the feature instead of 40%. "Reliably" means the comparison spots an effect of that size at least 8 times out of 10.

What share of users should stay without the feature?

With 10% the holdout has 20,000 people, and the comparison reliably sees effects from 1 pp. With 1% (2,000 people) it sees only effects from 3 pp, with 5% from 1.4 pp. Half sees effects from 0.6 pp, but then 100,000 people miss the feature.   • A holdout's precision hinges on its own size: doubling the share from 10% to 20% narrows the margin by only a quarter, and going from 20% to 50% by about another fifth. That is why people usually keep 5–10%, not half.
  • The holdout is drawn by lot before the launch, not from those who "didn't update yet": they differ on their own, as in chapter 1.
  • Sizing is the same decision as sizing an A/B test: first the effect worth seeing, then the number of people. The calculator is in chapter 9 of the course "A/B testing paradoxes", and a holdout for a whole year of changes is in chapter 17.

Deeper: why the noise between identical groups is so large.

The A/A test: two identical pages

The same page is shown as "A" and as "B", converting at 5%, with 1,000 visitors each. How often will one of two identical pages "win" by 20% or more?

  • Standard error

    How far an average or a rate computed from a sample usually misses the truth: σ divided by the square root of the sample size.

  • Confidence interval

    A range around a measured number, built by a method that covers the true value in a chosen share of samples, such as 95%.

  • Statistical power

    The probability that a test notices a real effect of a given size. A power of 80% means the test misses one working effect in five.

What you can do now You see a natural experiment in a staggered rollout (6), compute a difference in differences and check the parallel lines before the launch (7), look for a jump at a threshold (8), understand why matching is only an upper bound (9), and know how many people to leave without the feature (10). The third part is about what hides inside the metrics themselves: old and new users, actions that just moved from one feature to another, and totals that move the wrong way. And at the end, when none of this replaces an experiment.
Part 3 · a little more maths

Cohorts, cannibalisation and when you need an experiment

The first two parts asked whom to compare with. The third asks what the metric actually counts. The retention of "all active users" depends on who is among them, actions can move from one feature to another, and total conversion can fall while it grew in every group. At the end: when all this is not enough and you should insist on an experiment.

Retention and "old users are better"

"Over the year, the retention of active users grew from 60% to 80%. Has the product got better?"

That is the joy when the retention chart creeps up without any big release. Before thanking the product, let's look at who the "active users" are.

Retention here is the share of those active this month who are still active the next month.

The active users include newcomers and long-standing users, and they behave very differently. Newcomers leave most often: many take a look and never come back, so after the first month only 40% of them stay. Those who have stayed a few months almost all stay on, over 90%.

A year ago 60% of active users stayed the next month, now 80% do. And nobody changed the product in a way that would raise retention.

How could retention grow like that without improvements?

Long-standing users who are past their first months almost all stay. When signups slow down, the share of newcomers among active users falls, and overall retention rises by itself, though every cohort retains the same.   • Count retention by cohort: "of those who signed up in March, on day 30 there remained…". Compare cohorts of the same age before and after the launch.
  • Judge a feature for long-standing users on cohorts that got it at the same age, not on all active users together.

Cannibalisation

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 2, “Tried ≠ helped”

"'Quick reply' was used 50,000 times in a week. So it gave 50,000 replies?"

The feature's counter tells how many times it was used. Whether it added replies depends on what the sellers would have done without it.

A new feature often does what an old one already did, only differently. "Quick reply" sends a ready-made text with one tap, while an ordinary reply the seller types out.

Before the launch sellers wrote 200,000 replies a week. After the launch "quick reply" was used 50,000 times a week.

By how much did the number of replies a week grow?

Most quick replies the sellers would have written anyway, just more slowly: the action simply moved from the old way to the new one. The gain is the total number of replies after minus before, not the feature's counter. If, as in the model below, 80% of quick replies replaced ordinary ones, ordinary replies fell to 160,000, 210,000 in all: a gain of 10,000, not 50,000. Where the 50,000 quick replies came from

  • If 80% of quick replies replaced ordinary ones, the feature gave +10,000 replies, not +50,000. A report based on the feature's counter would overstate the effect fivefold.
  • Measure the whole: all replies, all orders, all revenue, not the actions through the new button.
  • If the new feature only speeds up an old action, its value lies in time or quality, not quantity. Then pick the metric to match (chapter 3).

An effect hidden in the totals

"The new checkout raised conversion on both phones and computers. But total conversion fell. Is the feature harmful?"

It happens, and there is no need to rush to call the feature harmful. First let's see what total conversion is made of.

Before the launch half the visitors came from phones and half from computers. On phones 2 in 100 bought (2.0%), on computers 5 in 100 (5.0%). Together that made 3.5%.

After the new checkout launched, phones show 2.2% and computers 5.3%: both groups improved. Yet total conversion fell from 3.5% to 3.1%.

What happened?

Total conversion is the average of the groups, weighted by their shares. Say a social media campaign brought many people on phones: their share grew from 50% to 70%. Then together it is 0.7 × 2.2% + 0.3 × 5.3% ≈ 3.1%, while with the old 50/50 shares it would be 3.75%. Every group buys better, but there are more people in the low-conversion group. This is Simpson's paradox. Check below.Computer conversion actually grew, from 5.0% to 5.3%. What changed is the share: there are more people on phones, from 50% to 70%, and they convert less.   • When the total and the groups disagree, first check whether the mix changed: devices, traffic sources, new and old users, countries.
  • Compare groups with groups, and recompute the total with the old shares. But a slice chosen after seeing the data is only a hypothesis (chapter 3 of the course "A/B testing paradoxes").

When to insist on an experiment

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 7, “Difference in differences”chapter 9, “Matched groups”chapter 10, “A long holdout”

"There's no time for an A/B test. We'll launch and look at the numbers later."

Sometimes that is reasonable: not every change is worth a test. But after a launch without a test only the methods of part 2 remain, and each of them rests on an assumption: parallel lines, an honest threshold, similar groups. An experiment needs none of them.

Before you say the feature worked

The same questions as a questionnaire that gives the risks and an estimation method for your launch are on the page "Can you trust this impact?". Handy to open before every report and to send to colleagues.

What we compare with

  1. Not "with the feature against without": these people differed before the launch.
  2. Not just "before and after": last year's period and the calendar of changes are checked.
  3. Not from the worst week, but from the normal level before the drop.
  4. There is a group without the feature at the same time: a wave, a country, a platform, a holdout.
  5. The lines before the launch are parallel, and the effect is computed as a difference in differences.

What we count

  1. The share who tried is not success: we look at who came back and stayed.
  2. One primary metric of value and guardrails chosen before the launch; DAU and minutes are secondary.
  3. The whole, not the feature's counter: all orders, all replies.
  4. Retention by cohort, not of all active users together.
  5. The mix didn't change: devices, sources, new and old; otherwise we compare groups with groups.

Before reporting

  1. The threshold is honest: about as many people on both sides of it, and the effect applies only to them.
  2. Matching is an upper bound, not an effect; the best variable is the metric itself before the launch.
  3. The margin is stated: the holdout is big enough to see the effect we care about.
  4. A big decision goes through an experiment: if money or the roadmap depends on the effect, at least a cheap test.

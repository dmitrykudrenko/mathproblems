How to pass interviews with puzzles

Interview · a puzzle10 minutes

You have two ropes and a lighter. Each rope burns for exactly one hour, but unevenly: half a rope does not necessarily burn in half an hour. How do you measure 45 minutes?

"Take your time. Think out loud, I'm interested in how you reason."

You might get a puzzle like this in an interview for a quant firm (an investment firm that trades by mathematical models), consulting, analytics or software. This course is about what to do in those ten minutes. First we learn to reason out loud: read the question, try small cases, work backwards, estimate and shape an answer. Then we build a toolkit: what one test tells you, the worst case, invariants, a smart schedule and logic without numbers, "who tells the truth". The third part is about probability, needed mostly for quant and analytics, and about what to do when you're stuck. At the end comes a mock interview with a timer and hints, and the rope puzzle worked through.

"Why this? I'm not applying to university" An interview puzzle is not a test of school maths. You won't need its formulas at work, and the interviewer knows that. What they want to see is how you handle a problem you have never seen before: what you clarify, where you start, how you notice your own mistake and how you explain an idea to someone else. Working on a new bug, a metric that suddenly dropped or a project with no instructions looks the same, and ten minutes of conversation show it better than a CV. So the right answer is not the main thing: think out loud, ask, make mistakes and fix them. And popular puzzles are easy to find, so you may well know yours. Then the conversation won't end with the answer: you'll be asked to change the conditions, defend the solution against an objection or explain it to someone who disagrees. So prepare not just to solve but to discuss. That is what this course teaches.

≈ 1.5–2 hours, in 10–15 minute parts if you like; your progress is saved.

Part 1 · for everyone

Reasoning out loud

Six habits an interviewer notices in the first minutes. Each chapter starts with a small puzzle: guess first, then check it on a live picture. Arithmetic is enough here.

1

What is really rated

"They'll give me a puzzle. What if I don't know the answer?"

To answer that fear, first let's see what is actually rated. An interview puzzle is not an exam with the answer at the back of the book. The interviewer has little time, and in it they want to see how you think. The answer matters, but it is only part of what they will note down.

Here is how it looks. Two candidates get the same puzzle: how many squares in total can you see on an 8×8 chessboard. A thinks silently for a minute and names the right number; asked "How did you get it?", A says they knew the puzzle. B thinks out loud, makes a mistake, notices it, fixes it and checks the answer on a small example.

Which of the two did the interviewer learn more about?

From A the interviewer learned only that A knew the puzzle. From B they learned how B clarifies, looks for a pattern, notices and fixes a mistake, that is, how B will handle a problem they don't know yet. That is what puzzles are given for. See both conversations below.From A the interviewer learned only that A knew the puzzle, and has nothing else to write down. B showed how they clarify, look for a pattern and fix their own mistake. See both conversations below.

Two conversations about one puzzle

The puzzle: "How many squares in total can you see on an 8×8 chessboard?"

Candidate A

(a minute of silence)

— 204.

— How did you get that?

— I knew this one.

Candidate B

clarifying— We count squares of any size made of cells? So the whole board too?

simple case— 1×1 squares: 64. A 2×2 square fits in 8 positions along a row... no, 7, since it takes two cells. So 7×7 = 49.

pattern— Looks like there are (9 − k)² squares of size k×k. Then the total is 64 + 49 + … + 1, the sum of squares from 1 to 8, which is 204.

check— Let me check on a 2×2 board: the formula gives 4 + 1 = 5. Counting by hand: four small ones and one big. It matches.

Both got 204. But A's notes will say one word, "knew", while B's will show four signals, and those are what B is scored on.

You know the puzzle: what next

Popular puzzles are in every collection, so the interviewer often knows you may have seen them. The answer is then only the start, and the conversation moves on to the nuances. Here is what usually comes next:

  • "What if the conditions change?" What happens in the Monty Hall problem if the host doesn't know where the car is and opened a door with a goat by chance? Knowing the answer is not enough: you need to know which detail of the conditions it rests on.
  • "How does it generalise?" Not 10 floors but 100; not two eggs but three. Does your solution still work, or was it a trick for one number?
  • "I disagree" The interviewer may deliberately dispute the right answer. Repeating it won't help: find exactly where your reasoning parts ways and show it on a small example.
  • "Explain it more simply" So that a colleague without maths would follow, or with an example from work: where does the same trap show up in a product?
  • "Which assumption is weakest here?" Honestly naming the limits of your solution is a stronger signal than a flawless answer.

So when you prepare, don't memorise answers. For each puzzle you know, ask yourself: why exactly this, what changes with one detail, and how to convince someone who thinks otherwise.

Which puzzles get asked? It depends on the company and the role. Outside quant firms (investment firms that trade by mathematical models) and analytics they are mostly logic brainteasers with no formulas: who is lying, how to time something with ropes, how to find a fake coin with a balance, how to get across a river. They need no special knowledge, so they turn up in software, product and management interviews alike. Consulting and product interviews often ask for a rough estimate. Probability and average puzzles are mostly for quant firms, trading, analytics and data science. And some big tech companies have dropped brainteasers altogether: in 2013 Laszlo Bock, then Google's head of people operations, told the New York Times that their own data showed brainteasers did nothing for hiring. The course covers all three kinds: parts 1 and 2 are for everyone, part 3 is mostly for quant and analytics.

What the firms themselves say Bock called brainteasers "a complete waste of time": they predict nothing and mostly make the interviewer feel smart. Jane Street's page for trading candidates says the final round may include problem solving, probability and statistics, and that the interview feels more like a conversation than a quiz; its tips include communicating clearly and correcting your mistakes. Bain's practice coffee-shop case says there are no "right" assumptions for a market size, only reasonable ones, and that structure matters. Sources: ABC News on Bock's New York Times interview, 20 June 2013; janestreet.com/trading-interviews; bain.com, the Coffee Shop Co. case.

Whether the candidate gets it right has never mattered much to me. What matters is whether they can question a wrong assumption in conversation, and, when they are right, how well they can explain their idea or take apart someone else's."   • Think out loud from the first minute: silence is something the interviewer can't write down.
  • If you know the puzzle, say so, explain the solution as if you were deriving it for the first time, and be ready for what follows: other conditions, a generalisation, an objection.
  • Find out in advance what kind of puzzles your target employer asks: quant, consulting and software ask very different things.

Read the question again

"The answer is obvious. Why is the interviewer smiling?"

Often because the "obvious" answer answers a different question than the one asked. It's a typical interview mistake, and hard maths has nothing to do with it: the brain slips in a familiar template, and out comes a confident but wrong number.

Try it on a classic that takes a few seconds.

Try small cases

"A hundred people, a hundred steps. How do you even hold that in your head?"

You don't have to hold it all in your head. Big numbers in a question are often only there to scare you. If you can't see the puzzle at once, solve it for 1, 2, 3, 4 and look at the answers. A pattern often shows after a few steps, and explaining it is easier than guessing it.

Here is exactly such a puzzle, with a hundred people and a hundred steps. Guess the answer for a hundred first, then check it on small corridors.

A corridor has 100 closed lockers. The first student opens them all. The second toggles every second one (2, 4, 6, …): closes an open one, opens a closed one. The third toggles every third, and so on up to the hundredth, who toggles only the hundredth. How many lockers are open at the end?

The lockers left open are 1, 4, 9, 16, …, 100. See how it shows up in small corridors below.Ten lockers stay open: 1, 4, 9, 16, …, 100. See how it shows up in small corridors below.

A corridor of lockers

Why squares? Locker 12 is touched by students 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12, its divisors. Divisors come in pairs: 1 and 12, 2 and 6, 3 and 4, so the locker is toggled an even number of times and ends closed. Only a square has an unpaired divisor: for 16, the divisor 4 pairs with itself. Small cases gave the pattern, and the explanation turned it into a proof.

  • Say out loud: "Let me try two, three, four first." Interviewers are waiting for exactly this step.
  • A pattern you found is not yet an answer. Explain why it holds, and only then carry it to 100.

Work backwards

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 3, “Small cases”

"This is a game of many moves. Where do I start?"

From the end, odd as it sounds. When a puzzle is a sequence of steps, starting from the first is often hopeless: there are too many options. The last step, though, is usually simple. Solve it, then the one before, and so on back to the start. It's the same "small cases" idea, except that what's small here is the end of the game.

There are 21 matches on the table. Two players take turns taking 1, 2 or 3 matches, and whoever takes the last one wins. You choose whether to go first or second. What do you do?

  • If the puzzle is a game or a multi-step process, ask yourself: "What does the last step look like?"
  • A table of positions built from the end is a ready answer for the interviewer: easy to show and to check.

A rough estimate

"How would I know how many there are? I don't remember numbers like that."

That's the usual reaction to a question like "How many piano tuners are there in Chicago?" But you don't need to remember such a number. Estimation questions, also called Fermi questions, don't test your memory. They are asked to see how you break an unknown number into a few known ones, state your assumptions and check that the result is plausible. Being within a factor of a few is usually enough.

Here is one such question. Just guess first.

How many babies are born in the world in one day?

According to the UN, 132 million babies were born in 2023, about 362 thousand a day. With the real numbers the estimate gives about 300 thousand a day, about a sixth short of the truth. That gap is interesting too, and worth saying aloud: the population is not steady but growing, so there are more young people than in a steady world, and they have more babies. Saying which way your estimate may be off and why is the best way to finish an answer.

  • First the formula in words ("population divided by life expectancy"), then the numbers. The formula is what the interviewer is rating.
  • Round boldly: 8 billion and 80 years are easy to do in your head, and accuracy hardly suffers.
  • Check the result another way or against a number you know, and say which way it might be off.

Deeper: a puzzle where the sense of scale fails almost everyone.

The rope around the Earth

A rope around the equator is made 1 metre longer and lifted evenly. How big is the gap between it and the ground?

How to shape an answer

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “What is really rated”

"Five minutes in and I still don't see a solution. What do I say?"

A dead end in an interview is a normal part of the puzzle, not a failure. Interviewers often deliberately pick a puzzle few people finish in ten minutes, to see what you do when you don't know.

You're stuck five minutes in and have been silent for a minute. What's the best move?

This gets the conversation back: the interviewer sees your train of thought, can give a hint, and the simpler case often leads to the solution. Below is a skeleton answer for any puzzle.The best move is to say what you've tried and where you're stuck, and take on a simpler case. Then the interviewer sees your thinking and can give a hint. Below is a skeleton answer for any puzzle.

The skeleton of an answer

  1. Restate the question"So we need to find… and the constraints are…"
  2. State assumptions, ask"I'm assuming that… Is that fine?" One sharp question beats five vague ones.
  3. Give a first rough answerAn obvious, even suboptimal, solution or a bound: "certainly no more than…". It gives you something to improve.
  4. SimplifySmall cases, working backwards, extreme values. Say out loud what you are trying.
  5. State the idea and the answerOne or two sentences: why it works, not just what came out.
  6. CheckPlug it into the question, compare with a small case or with your first rough bound.

If you're stuck, go back to step 4 and say so: "I'm stuck here, let me try it for two."

  • Break any silence longer than half a minute, even with no idea yet: say what you're checking right now.
  • "I don't know" is not the end of an answer but its start: "I don't know, but I can say it's certainly no more than…".
What we can do so far Reading the question and checking an answer by substitution, trying small cases, working backwards, estimating, and talking it through with a skeleton. Those are habits. Next come tools: a few ideas that unlock whole families of interview puzzles. Each is again about how to reason, not what to memorise.
Part 2 · school maths

The toolkit

Five ideas behind most interview logic puzzles: how much one test tells you, the worst case, invariants, a smart schedule, and "assume and check" for puzzles about truth and lies. You'll need powers of two and a little patience, and the last chapter needs no numbers at all.

7

What one test tells you

"Hundreds of thousands of options and only a handful of tests. Is that even possible?"

That's the surprise when you hear a puzzle like this one. The interviewer has picked one word from a dictionary of 100,000 words. You ask questions, and they answer only "yes" or "no". Each question is a test with two possible outcomes.

To see how many tests you need, don't try out strategies. Count how many different sets of answers all the tests can give together. If there are fewer sets than possibilities, no strategy will help. If there are as many or more, it can usually be done.

How many questions do you need to find the word for sure?

Each question can halve the list: 100,000, 50,000, 25,000… 16 questions give 216 = 65,536 different answer patterns, too few; 17 give 131,072, enough. What if a test has three outcomes instead of two? A balance shows "left heavier", "right heavier" or "equal", so n weighings give 3n different outcomes. It gets more interesting when several tests run at once: then you count not questions but combinations of all the tests' answers together. Both puzzles below come down to exactly this count.

  • First count the bound: how many distinct outcomes all the tests give together, and how many possibilities you must tell apart. That is a lower bound for any strategy.
  • A good test splits the possibilities into equal parts. A question that is almost always answered "no" tells you almost nothing.

Twelve coins and a balance

One of 12 coins is fake, lighter or heavier, nobody knows. How many weighings on a two-pan balance do you need to find it for sure and say which way it is off?

The worst case and an evil opponent

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 7, “What one test tells you”

"Binary search is the best, surely. Why doesn't it work here?"

Halving, as in the game from chapter 7, is the first idea in any search puzzle. But the two-egg puzzle this question comes from has a constraint that game didn't have.

Besides, "for sure" in an interview means "in the worst case". It helps to imagine an evil opponent who knows your strategy and always answers so as to hurt you most. A strategy is good if even they can't force you into more steps.

The puzzle itself. A 10-floor building and two identical eggs. From some floor up, eggs break; below it they don't. A broken egg is gone; an intact one can be dropped again. You need to find the highest safe floor for sure.

How many drops does that take in the worst case?

Drop the first egg from floor 4, then 7, then 9: each jump one floor shorter. Once it breaks, the second climbs one floor at a time.   • When the question says "for sure" or "guaranteed", count the worst case and imagine an opponent who will arrange it.
  • A limited resource (eggs, races, mistakes) changes the best strategy. Run the familiar algorithm through the worst case before proposing it.

Invariants and parity

"I keep trying and there's always a gap left. Am I doing something wrong?"

That happens in "is it possible" puzzles: it seems only a lucky arrangement is missing, and you keep searching for it. Before the tenth try, it's worth asking what in the puzzle never changes.

An invariant is a quantity that doesn't change whatever you do. If it would have to be different at the end than at the start, the goal is unreachable, and you don't need to try every option to prove it. In an interview it is the shortest path from "I can't make it work" to "it's impossible, and here's why".

The classic example is a chessboard and dominoes.

Two opposite corner squares are cut out of an 8×8 chessboard, leaving 62 squares. Can you cover them with 31 dominoes, each covering exactly two adjacent squares?

Opposite corners have the same colour, so 32 squares of one colour and 30 of the other remain. And every domino covers one white and one black. Try the same on a smaller board below: 4×4 without two opposite corners.No. Opposite corners have the same colour, so 32 squares of one colour and 30 of the other remain, and every domino covers one of each. Try the same on a smaller board below: 4×4 without two opposite corners.

A 4×4 board without two corners

The same puzzle, only smaller: 14 squares and 7 dominoes. Place a domino: tap two adjacent squares. Tapping a placed domino removes it.

  • If the question asks "is it possible" and you keep failing, look for what doesn't change: colour, parity, a sum, a remainder.
  • Parity is the most common interview invariant: it works for dominoes, hats and toggles alike.

Greedy or smart

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 6, “Shaping an answer”

"I'll just do the best thing at each step. What could go wrong?"

A greedy strategy, always doing what looks best right now, is a natural first step and a good "first rough answer" from chapter 6. But in scheduling puzzles it often loses, and a lower bound helps you check: the least time it could possibly take, however you arrange things.

Here is a popular scheduling puzzle that also comes up in interviews.

A toaster fits two slices of bread and toasts one side of each per minute. You need three slices toasted on both sides. Flipping and swapping slices takes no time. What is the shortest time?

There are six sides and two slots, so less than 3 minutes is impossible, and 3 is reachable if you don't finish the first pair first.   • Give the greedy answer first, then compute a lower bound. If they differ, the better schedule lies in that gap.

The bridge and the torch

11

Who tells the truth

"There isn't a single number here. Where do I even start?"

Many interview puzzles have no numbers at all: who is lying, who said what, which question to ask. The tool here is simple: assume and check. Take one statement, assume it is true and follow the consequences until you hit a contradiction or a picture where everything fits. And if there are only three people, there are just eight options, and you can go through them all, like the small cases of chapter 3.

Let's start with the most famous puzzle of this kind. There are only two people here, but what you're looking for is not an answer but a question.

Two doors: one leads to freedom, the other to a trap. Two guards stand by them: one always tells the truth, the other always lies, and you don't know which is which. You may ask one question to one guard. Which question lets you walk out free for sure?

The answer passes through both guards, the truthful one and the liar, so it is always false. The truthful guard honestly reports his partner's lie, the liar twists his partner's truth. Both point at the trap, so take the other door. You never learn who is who, and you don't need to. The island of knights and knaves

Knights always tell the truth, knaves always lie. Three islanders each said one sentence. Who is who? Tap the people to switch between knight and knave: under each you can see whether that person could say their words.

The guards puzzle is a favourite in interviews precisely because brute force doesn't work: there are endless possible questions. What helps is another question to yourself: "What is unknown here, and how do I get rid of it?" The unknown is whether the guard lies. If the answer passes through both guards, the lie works exactly once whoever you ask, and the unknown is gone.

  • Assume and check: one assumption and a chain of consequences, said out loud, is the shortest path in truth-and-lies puzzles.
  • When there are few options, go through them all. Eight options for three people take a minute, and that is a complete answer.
  • If something unknown is in the way, build a move after which it no longer matters, like the question through both guards.

Part 3 · quant and analytics

Probability and dead ends

Three intuition traps loved in quant, analytics and data science interviews: conditional probability, the average, and when to stop. You'll need fractions and the idea of an average. Other interviews rarely ask these, so chapters 12–14 can be skipped. The last chapter of the part, on hints, is for everyone.

Conditional probability

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 2, “Read the question again”

"Two options are left, so it's fifty-fifty?"

"Two options, so it's half and half" is a typical mistake in probability puzzles. Options aren't always equally likely: what matters is not how many are left but how many ways you could have arrived at what you see.

  • Ask how exactly you learned what you know: who chose, what was shown and why. The answer depends on it, as in the Monty Hall problem from chapter 2.

The two-child paradox

A family has two children, and at least one of them is a boy. What is the chance both are boys? And what if at least one of them is a boy born on a Tuesday?

Symmetry and the average

"There are so many cases. Do I really have to go through them all?"

Often not. In average puzzles you don't need the full distribution. It's enough to split the quantity into a sum of simple parts: the average of a sum is the sum of the averages, even when the parts depend on each other. And symmetry gives each part's average with no calculation at all.

Here is a puzzle with truly countless cases: ten hats can be handed back to ten people in 3,628,800 ways.

Ten people check their hats, and the attendant hands them back completely at random. How many people get their own hat back, on average?

Each of the ten gets their own hat with chance 1/10, so on average 10 × 1/10 = 1. And it doesn't depend on the number of people. Check below.Exactly 1. Each person gets their own hat with chance 1/10, and averages add up: 10 × 1/10 = 1, whether there are 10 people or 1000. Check below.

Look at the last number: in about 37% of evenings nobody gets their own hat, so at least one person does in 63%. That's the second trick of this chapter: when asked "at least one", compute the opposite, "none", and subtract it from one. That is how the birthday problem is solved too.

  • The average of "how many" is the sum of each one's chance. Dependence between them doesn't get in the way.
  • Compute "at least one" through "none". It's almost always easier.

Deeper: a sticker album whose average is a sum of simple parts, and a room where "at least one pair" is computed through "none".

How many stickers to fill an album

An album has 50 slots, and the stickers in the packs are random. On average, how many stickers do you have to buy to fill it?

The birthday paradox

How many people must be in a room before two of them probably share a birthday?

When to stop

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 3, “Small cases”

"Take the first offer or wait for a better one?"

That's a question about your own job search, and in quant or analytics interviews it is asked from the hiring side: you're hiring, and candidates arrive one at a time. The maths is the same.

Stopping puzzles are like life: options arrive one at a time, a rejection can't be undone, and nothing is known about those still to come. The big case isn't visible at once, so we start small (chapter 3): three candidates have only six possible orders, few enough to list by hand.

Three candidates arrive in random order. You must decide on each at once: hire, or reject forever. You only see whether they're better than those before. Which strategy hires the best one more often?

This strategy wins in three orders out of six, a 1/2 chance, while "take the first" or "take the third" wins only 1/3 of the time. All six orders are below.Skipping the first and taking the next better one wins in three orders out of six, a 1/2 chance; the other two simple strategies win 1/3 of the time.   • Listing all cases for a small size is a full-fledged interview answer, if you then say what you see in the table and how it carries over to larger numbers.
  • The first options are useful even when you reject them: they set the bar for the rest.

When you're stuck: hints

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 2, “Read the question again”chapter 3, “Small cases”chapter 9, “Invariants and parity”

"I was given a hint. Does that count against me?"

A hint is an ordinary part of the conversation: the interviewer gives it to keep the puzzle moving and to see more. What's rated is not that you needed it but what you did with it: whether you saw where it points and connected it to what you had already tried.

You've been turning over a puzzle about a hundred items for a few minutes, and the interviewer asks: "What if there were only two?" What does that most likely mean?

It's the hint from chapter 3: solve a small case and carry the idea to a hundred. Say aloud that you heard it, and start with two. Try the hint ladder below.Most likely it's the hint from chapter 3: solve a small case and carry the idea to a hundred. Say aloud that you heard it, and start with two. Try the hint ladder below.

A hint ladder

There are 100 coins on the table, 10 of them heads up. You're blindfolded: you can't see the coins or feel which side is up, but you can move and flip them. Split the coins into two piles with the same number of heads.

Hint 1What if there were two coins, one of them heads up?

Hint 2The piles needn't be the same size. How many coins should go in the smaller one?

Hint 3If a pile of 10 coins has k heads, how many heads will it have once you flip all ten?

Count off any 10 coins into a separate pile and flip them all. If that pile had k heads, the rest has 10 − k. After flipping, the pile has 10 − k heads, the same as the rest.

Notice what each hint did: the first was a small case (chapter 3), the second removed an unnoticed constraint (chapter 2), the third was an invariant: the difference in heads between the piles stays under your control (chapter 9). Interview hints almost always point to one of this course's tools. So when you hear one, say which technique it points to and how it changes your plan.

  • When you hear a hint, restate it and say what you'll try: "Right, so for two coins…"
  • Don't throw away what you've done: explain how the hint fits with your attempts.
  • If a hint is unclear, ask directly: "Are you pointing me to a small case?" That's a signal too, not a weakness.

Puzzle 3 · a simpler view

Two trains 100 km apart head towards each other, each at 50 km/h. A fly flies from one train to the other at 75 km/h, turns around, flies back and so on until the trains meet. How many kilometres does the fly cover?

0:00

Don't add up the flight legs one by one: there are infinitely many.

Which is easier to find: the fly's path, or how long it flies?

The trains close in at 100 km/h. How long until they meet?

The trains meet in an hour, and the fly flies at 75 km/h for that whole hour (chapter 2: ask what exactly you need to find).75 km. The trains meet in an hour, and the fly flies at 75 km/h for that whole hour. Infinitely many legs add up to a finite distance.

How did you reason?

A model answer"I could compute the first leg, but then I'd get an infinite series. Easier through time: the trains close in at 100 km/h, so they meet in an hour. The fly flies the whole hour, so 75 km. Check: the first leg takes 100/125 = 0.8 h, that's 60 km, less than 75, consistent."

Puzzle 4 · logic

There are three switches in a corridor, and behind a closed door a room with an ordinary incandescent bulb. Only one switch controls it. 0:00

There are three switches and one visit. How many different states of the bulb must that one visit tell apart?

Your eyes see only two states: on or off. What else can you learn about an incandescent bulb when you walk up to it?

A bulb that has been on for a while stays hot even after it is switched off.

A model answer"Let me check: it's an ordinary incandescent bulb, and I may touch it? Good. Three switches, one visit, so I need a check with three outcomes. Light gives two: on or off. And now the puzzle from the start of the course

Interview · a puzzle10 minutes

Two ropes, each burns for exactly an hour, but unevenly. How do you measure 45 minutes?

Here's how a strong answer might sound. Each line is a habit from the course.

  1. Clarifying (chapter 2)"Can I light a rope at both ends at once? And light it at any moment?" — "Yes."
  2. A simple case (chapter 3)"First, one rope. If I light it at both ends, the flames meet after exactly 30 minutes, however unevenly it burns: together they burn an hour of rope twice as fast."
  3. Working backwards (chapter 4)"45 = 30 + 15. So I need another 15. If a rope has 30 minutes of burning left, lighting its other end gives exactly 15."
  4. The idea and the answer (chapter 6)"At the same time I light the first rope at both ends and the second at one end. When the first burns out, 30 minutes have passed and the second has 30 left. I light its other end: it burns out in 15. Total 45."
  5. Checking (chapter 6)"Nowhere did I assume the rope burns evenly: only that the whole rope burns for an hour. The same method also measures 15, 30 and 60 minutes."

A puzzle interview checklist

Practising on catalogue puzzles is easy in the "Interview trainer": a random puzzle, a timer, three hints and a self-review of your reasoning.

Before the interview

  1. Found out what this employer asks: quant, consulting and software ask different things.
  2. Practised the answer skeleton out loud with a timer, on at least five puzzles.
  3. Thought through follow-ups for popular puzzles: what changes with one detail of the conditions, and how to explain the solution to someone who disagrees.
  4. Went through the tools: tests, the worst case, invariants, scheduling, "assume and check".
  5. Know a few anchor numbers: the population of your country and the world, minutes in a year, life expectancy.
  6. Probability: equally likely outcomes and "at least one", if you're going into quant or analytics.

During

  1. Restated the question and named assumptions before calculating.
  2. Think out loud: break any silence longer than half a minute.
  3. A rough answer or bound first, then improvements.
  4. Stuck — a small case, or work backwards, and say so out loud.
  5. Restate a hint and connect it to your attempts.
  6. Checked the answer by substitution or a small case, and said which way it might be off.

After

  1. Wrote down the puzzle and where you got stuck, while you still remember.
  2. Solved it fully at home and compared it with what you said in the interview.
  3. Found a similar puzzle in the trainer and solved it out loud with a timer.

The share of visitors who did the thing you want: 250 purchases out of 5,000 visitors is 5%.

How many percent B's conversion is above A's: 5.86% against 5.00% is +17%.
Confidence
If B were no different from A, a gap this big or bigger would happen in about 6 tests out of 100; 94% is 100 minus 6. It is not the chance that B is better (chapter 10).
Significant result
A gap that noise rarely produces: by convention, confidence of 95% or more.
A/A test
A test where both groups see the same page, so any difference in it is noise.
Chance to beat
The number on Bayesian dashboards: how likely B is to be better than A, given the data. It is not the same as confidence (chapter 11).
MDE
The minimum detectable effect, the smallest lift worth catching: the test may miss smaller ones, and that is a deliberate choice (chapter 9).
SRM
Sample ratio mismatch: you planned an even split, but the groups came out noticeably different (chapter 13).

How to read a dashboard

Eight situations where the dashboard says one thing and the truth is another. Each chapter starts with a question: guess first, then check it on a live picture. There are no formulas here; looking carefully is enough.

1

Noise or effect

"B +17%. Is that a real effect, or just luck?"

That gets asked after every test, and the answer starts with another question: what lift does noise alone give when there is no effect at all? To see it, let's put the old "Buy" button against exactly the same one. Such a test is called an A/A test.

Each visitor either buys or not, a bit like a coin toss. So even two identical pages never give identical numbers.

Each page gets 1,000 people, and both truly convert at 5%: one person in twenty buys. The dashboard shows the lift: by how many percent B's conversion is higher than A's. That "+17%" is such a relative lift, not 17 percentage points.

What lift can the dashboard show in such an A/A test?

With 1,000 visitors a page has only about 50 purchases, and each extra or missing purchase shifts the rate by 0.1 points, that is 2% of 5%. A random swing of 7 purchases either way is normal. Say, 48 purchases in A and 56 in B is already +17%. Noise alone gives a lift of ±20% or more in about 30 A/A tests out of 100. The typical size of this wobble is called the standard error: how far a number from a sample usually lands from the true one. Run hundreds of A/A tests and the lifts pile up into a bell: the normal distribution. The more visitors, the narrower the bell.

  • Before celebrating a lift, ask what lift noise alone gives at this number of visitors.
  • Before serious tests, run an A/A test: it checks both your intuition and the tool itself.
  • With 1,000 visitors per page, a lift of tens of percent is ordinary noise. So the +17% on the dashboard at the top proves nothing yet: exactly how little, we work out in chapter 10.

May I stop once it shows 95%?

"The test is planned for five weeks, but on day nine the dashboard already shows 95%. Why wait?"

Almost everyone who opens the dashboard daily reasons like this: if it already shows 95%, why wait almost four more weeks? Let's check whether an early "95%" can be trusted on a test where there can be no win: two identical "Buy" buttons again, as in chapter 1.

The 95% threshold promises that if the pages are identical, a false "win" happens in at most 5 tests out of 100. But confidence on the dashboard does not stand still: new people arrive every evening, and the number wanders up and down.

The same shop: 2,000 visitors a day, 1,000 for each button, 5% buy. The marketer opens the dashboard every evening for 30 days and stops the test as soon as confidence touches 95%.

How often will a test run by these rules end in a "win"?

No, not in most. There really are far more false wins than the promised 5 in 100: over 30 evenings with 1,000 people per button there are about 28 in 100. But it won in Poland!

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 2, “Stopping at 95%”

"Overall B did nothing. But in Poland it is significantly better. Ship it there?"

A team tested a new newsletter email (B) against the old one (A) in 20 countries. Overall there is no difference. After such a test it is tempting to slice the result: countries, devices, traffic sources, new and returning customers. And in Poland something "significant" turned up.

Each slice is a separate check with the same 95% threshold as in chapter 2. A country lights up when its confidence is 95% or more, either way: B better or worse.

In each country 1,000 people got the old email and another 1,000 the new one, and 5% buy. One such send to 20 countries is one campaign.

Suppose the new email is in truth no better than the old one. How often will at least one of the 20 countries show a "significant" result?

Not almost always: in about a third of campaigns none of the 20 countries lights up. But in the rest, about 64 campaigns in 100, at least one does. 4

10 headlines from AI

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 3, “A win in a segment”

"AI wrote ten headlines in a minute. We test them all at once against the current one: whichever wins is ours."

That's how many teams think now: variants cost next to nothing, and a dozen new ones against the current one has become routine. Such a test is called A/B/n.

Here is how it works: the current headline and ten new ones, each seen by 5,000 visitors. The test compares each new headline with the current one separately, at the same 95% level.

Suppose the AI wrote smoothly but to no effect: in truth no headline is better or worse than the current one, all convert at 5%.

How often will at least one headline in such a test differ "significantly" from the current one?

Ten comparisons, ten chances, as with the countries in chapter 3. At least one significant difference happens in 29 tests out of 100, and a "winner" better than the current headline in 14. That is fewer than 40 in 100 for ten independent checks, because all variants are measured against the same control: if the current headline got lucky or unlucky, that shifts all ten comparisons together. A careful rollout

"We started B on 10% of traffic and added more every day. B is better on every day of the week. So it is better for the week too?"

That's the question from a team that did everything carefully. Let's go through their week day by day: the answer hides in details that are easy to skip.

Why ramp up? B is a new page, and if it has a bug, it's better that few people see it. So on Monday 10% of visitors go to B, the share grows a little every day, and by Sunday half of them see B.

The shop gets 10,000 visitors a day. On weekdays 6 in 100 buy, but on weekends only 3 in 100.

B really is better every day, and by the same 5%: on weekdays 6.3 in 100 buy with it instead of 6, on weekends 3.15 instead of 3. That "+5%" is a relative difference, not 5 percentage points.

On the next Monday the analyst writes the report: adds up all of B's visitors over the seven days (21,000), all of A's (49,000), counts the purchases and divides.

What will the weekly report show once all visitors are added together?

The report will say A won: 5.34% against 4.90%, so B is 8% worse. The reason is when the visitors came. B's share grew towards the end of the week, so 44% of its visitors fell on the weekend, when everyone buys half as often. For A it was only 22%.   • The same happens when a newsletter or ad campaign with a different audience arrives mid-test while the groups' shares are unequal.

Fewer buy from the cart

"A new product card: more people add to cart, but fewer buy from the cart. Is B worse?"

A team redesigned the product card and now doesn't know whether to be pleased. The test report shows two things: with the new card people put the item in the cart more often, but a smaller share of carts ends in a purchase.

That share is called cart conversion: how many of those who put the item in the cart then bought. In A it is 40%, so 4 carts in 10 end in a purchase. In B it is about 33%, and the gap is significant, so it is not noise.

And visitors differ: some came to buy, some are comparing, some are just browsing.

Cart conversion fell from 40% in A to 33% in B, and it is not noise. Is B worse?

Cart conversion counts only those who reached the cart, and B brought different people there. In the model below, of A's 10,000 visitors 2,150 reach the cart and 860 buy, that is 40%. With B, 2,750 reach it: 600 more people who were just browsing, and only one in ten of them buys. So the share of carts falls to 33.5%, while purchases rise to 920 from 860, 7% more per visitor. The verdict comes from the per-visitor metric. People are split at random only at the entrance: each visitor has an equal chance of seeing A or B. So A and B are fairly comparable only at the level of all visitors. Those who reached the cart have already been selected by the page itself, and in B they are different people than in A.

  • Compute the main metric per visitor who was randomly assigned to A or B: purchases or revenue per visitor. Rates at later funnel steps help with diagnosis, not with the verdict.
  • The same goes for the average order value: if B brings more small purchases, the average order falls while revenue per visitor rises. Revenue, where one big order flips the result, is chapter 12.

The reverse happens too, and it is sneakier: an intermediate metric rises, it is closely tied to revenue, and yet revenue falls. This is called the surrogate paradox. An example: 20% off everything. Without it 4 in 100 visitors buy, with an average order of $100. With the discount 4.6 in 100 buy, so there are 15% more orders, but the order is $80. Revenue per visitor: $4.00 in A and $3.68 in B, 8% less. Last year the weeks with more orders also had more revenue, and still a change that adds orders can take revenue away: the discount pulls it down along another path, through the price.

  • An intermediate metric (clicks, carts, orders) is not the verdict, even when it is closely tied to revenue. Keep revenue per visitor next to it as a guardrail metric.

+15% in the first week, then nothing

"After a week of testing the new banner gave +15%. We shipped it, and a month later revenue is the same as before. Where did the effect go?"

This question comes after the launch, but the clue was in the data during the test. Let's go back to that week.

The banner sits on the home page, and it is mostly seen by returning customers: they visit often and know the old page well.

The dashboard shows the cumulative lift: B's conversion over all days since the start against A's. At the end of the week it reads +15%, and the team ships the banner.

But split the same week into single days and something else shows: the lift shrank every day, biggest on Monday and smallest on Sunday.

Had the team looked at those days on decision day, what effect should it have expected a month after the launch?

Returning customers notice something new and click out of curiosity, and after a few days they get used to it. That part of the lift fades, and a daily lift that melts is its trace. Even a month does not fully save you: the cumulative lift averages all days, and the first week pulls it up. It is more honest to look at the lift in the last weeks, or at the lift by days since a person's first visit: if it is large in the first days of acquaintance and then melts, that is novelty.

The reverse happens too, and it is more dangerous. Regular customers dislike change: the button is not where they are used to it, the menu is different. For the first days they stumble, and once they get used to it the effect grows. This is change aversion. A mirror model: the same true effect of +2%, but on the first day habit takes away 20%, and that loss halves every two days. The first week on average shows −6.9%, and a good change gets rejected. Weeks two, three and four give +1.2%, +1.9% and +2.0%, and the cumulative lift after 28 days is still −0.4%. The cure is the same: the lift week by week, and separately for new and returning visitors. New visitors have no habits, so there is neither novelty nor aversion, and their lift is closer to the true one from day one.

  • Run the test in whole weeks and look at the lift week by week: the first week apart from the rest, new visitors apart from returning ones.
  • After the launch, check the effect again, for example with a small holdout group that stays on the old version (chapter 17).
  • Besides novelty, noise is at work too: a test stopped when the lift looked its best records a lucky streak, and the following days will most likely be more ordinary.

A subject line: the winner in 2 hours

"The email platform picks the better subject line by itself: it sends two subjects to 10% of the list each, and two hours later sends the winner to the rest. What could go wrong?"

That's the question from a marketer who left the choice of subject line to the platform. The scheme sounds sensible: two subjects are tried on small groups, and the rest of the list gets the one opened more often.

Here is how it looks in one send. The list has 50,000 addresses. Subject A goes to 5,000 of them, subject B to another 5,000, and 40,000 addresses wait. Two hours later 49% of recipients have opened subject A and 47% subject B, so the platform sends A to the rest of the list.

Who manages to open an email within two hours? Mostly habitual readers: they open almost every email you send, and quickly. The rest open later, over the day.

And one more detail. Since September 2021, with iOS 15, Apple Mail has had Mail Privacy Protection. If a person turns it on, the mail app downloads the email's images in the background, even when nobody opened the email. The tracking pixel fires, and the platform records an "open" whatever subject the person got. According to figures Litmus cites in its email client market share report, this protection affects roughly 55–60% of all recorded opens.

How often does the rule "winner by opens after 2 hours" pick the subject that really brings more clicks?

No more often than a coin flip: in the model below this rule picks the subject with more clicks in only 43 sends out of 100. Look at who is behind the opens of the first two hours. If 40% of addresses have Apple Mail protection, then of every 100 recorded "opens" 82 were recorded by Apple Mail itself, 16 came from habitual readers whom the subject does not affect, and only 2 from people who actually decided whether to open the email. The difference between the subjects drowns in noise. 9

How many people you need

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “Noise or effect”

"Fine, noise happens. How many visitors do I need not to be fooled by it?"

This is where planning starts. Noise does not go away, but enough people can outweigh it. How many you need depends on three things.

The first is the usual conversion rate. With the "Buy" button from the dashboard, 5 visitors in 100 buy.

The second is the smallest effect that matters to you (the MDE). A +10% lift means 5.5 in 100 buy with the new button instead of 5: one extra purchase per 200 visitors.

The third is how strictly you judge. The industry standard: at most 5 false winners in 100 between identical pages and 80% power, meaning the test spots a real effect of the chosen size 4 times out of 5.

Under these conditions, detecting +10% takes 31,234 visitors per variant.

And how many to detect a lift half as big, +5%, that is 5.25 purchases in 100 instead of 5?

How to choose the MDE? It is a business decision, not a statistical one: the smallest lift that makes the change worth doing. The shop in the calculator: 2,000 visitors a day, 5% conversion, that is 100 purchases, average order $40. Hence one more rule: significant does not mean important. With millions of visitors even a +0.5% lift comes out significant, and it may not cover the cost of building it. Significance only says the effect is most likely not zero. Whether it is worth the money is your call.

One more thing: traffic is a budget. While a test runs, you can't try another idea on the same page, and a test runs in whole weeks. In a year the shop from the calculator can fit about ten tests that detect +10% on one page (5 weeks each), or only two that detect +5% (18 weeks each). The smaller the lift you want to see, the fewer ideas you get to check.

Two numbers, two questions

Confidence 94% asks: "If B is really no better, how often would noise give a gap this big or bigger?" The answer: in about 6 tests out of 100, and 94% is 100 minus 6. The number says how odd your data are for a world with no effect.

Chance to beat 97% asks the opposite: "Here are my data. How likely is B to be better?" That is usually what you want to know. How it is computed is in chapter 11.

Two sentences about a medical test differ the same way: "in a healthy person it is falsely positive in 6 cases out of 100" and "with a positive test I am ill with a 94% probability". The first does not give the second: that also depends on how rare the illness is. This is the false positive paradox.

Most significance calculators show only the first number, whatever they call it: confidence, significance, statistical significance. If you are not sure which one you see, check the tool's help: a mention of the p-value means the first.

Revenue per visitor and one big customer

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 6, “Cart and purchases”

"The main metric is revenue per visitor. B lost. But what if it is because of one customer who spent $3,000?"

The suspicion is natural: big orders are rare, but they weigh a lot. To answer it, let's first look at how noisy this metric is.

Revenue per visitor is an honest metric: it is computed over everyone randomly assigned to A or B (chapter 6). But it is noisier than conversion. On top of the chance of "bought or not" comes the chance of the order size, and orders are spread out: many small ones, a few big ones and, rarely, very big ones.

Here is group A of an ordinary test: 10,000 visitors, 5 in 100 buy, that is 500 orders, with an average order of $40. Revenue per visitor is the group's whole revenue divided by 10,000 people.

Now a wholesale buyer happens to land in group A and places one order for $3,000.

By how much does this one order raise group A's revenue per visitor?

By about 15%. Without the wholesaler the group made 500 × $40 = $20,000, that is $2.00 per visitor. One $3,000 order adds another $0.30 for each of the 10,000, making $2.30 instead of $2.00: +15%. "We split evenly, but the groups have slightly different numbers of people. That's a trifle, isn't it?"

A team split traffic evenly and at the end of the month sees 50,000 visitors in A and 48,500 in B. A gap of 1,500 people, about 3%.

Nobody expects a perfectly even split: each visitor is sent to A or B as if by a coin toss, and chance rocks the shares a little. The question is how much.

Is a gap of 1,500 people ordinary noise?

No. With a fair split each group would have 49,250 people, and noise usually moves that by no more than ±314. With 10,000 people per variant the test notices +5% only rarely: the calculator in chapter 9 would ask for 122,124. So a real idea gets through only when noise happened to help it, and its recorded lift is several times its true effect. Third: effects fade after the launch, like the novelty in chapter 7. The bigger the sample, the weaker the first two mechanisms: move the second slider to the right.

It can be worse: a small test not only exaggerates but gets the sign wrong. If the shop has only 1,000 people per variant, the test spots a real +5% 6 times in 100. Among these significant results the lift is exaggerated nine times on average, and almost a quarter of them say B is worse, although it is really better. At 10,000 per variant the exaggeration is threefold, and 2–3 significant results in 100 get the sign wrong. Specialists call these two errors Type M (for magnitude) and Type S (for sign).

  • Don't add up lifts from dashboards. Keep a long-term holdout: a small share of people who do not see the shipped changes. It alone shows what all the changes gave together.
  • Discount the recorded lift of a small test by half or more: the lower the power, the bigger the exaggeration.
  • A big effect from an important change deserves a repeat test.
Story Microsoft summed up its experiments: only about a third of ideas improved the metric they were designed to improve; about a third changed nothing, and another third did harm. So the model's assumption above, that one idea in five works, is not overly gloomy. Source: Ron Kohavi, Thomas Crook, Roger Longbotham, "Online Experimentation at Microsoft", 2009.

Deeper: why the winner of an auction almost always overpays.

The winner's curse

Ten bidders honestly estimate a jar of coins worth 100 dollars and bid their estimates. On average, what does the auction winner make?
  • An auction is won by the bidder who overestimated the jar the most, and a test by the variant noise helped the most. So the winner's recorded lift is inflated just like the winning bid.

Deeper: the same mechanism outside marketing.

The false positive paradox

1% of people have a disease. The test is 99% accurate, and you test positive. What is the chance you're really ill?
  • The share of false winners among the winners depends on how rare good ideas are. It is the same mechanism as in the false positive paradox: when the real thing is rare, even an accurate test gives many false "yes" answers.

Not everyone sees the popup

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 9, “How many people you need”

"A discount popup appears when a person is about to leave. Among those who see it, purchases are up by a quarter. What is that for the whole shop?"

The popup test result is in. To turn that quarter into a number for the whole shop, you first need to know who sees the popup.

The popup appears when a person is already reaching to close the tab. One visitor in five does that, and without the popup such people rarely buy: 2 in 100.

The rest never see it and buy as usual: 6 in 100. Together the shop converts at 5.2%.

The test compared those who saw the popup in B with the same kind of people in the control group. Their purchases rose by 25%: 2.5 in 100 instead of 2.

By how much did the whole shop's conversion rise?

About +1.9%. Count per 100 visitors: 20 see the popup and buy 0.5 instead of 0.4, the other 80 buy 4.8 as before. Together 5.3 purchases instead of 5.2, that is +1.9%. The effect is scaled not by the share of people but by the share of purchases: those who see the popup are 20% of visitors but only 7.7% of purchases, since they buy three times less often than the rest.That is the most common way to count, and it inflates the effect 2.6 times. Two dilutions are at work here. The first dilutes the effect: the popup changes only part of the purchases, while the shop's lift is counted over all of them. The second dilutes the signal with noise: people the popup never touched buy or don't by chance, and that noise stays in the test. So in the example a test analysing everyone needs 780,865 visitors per variant, while analysing only those who saw the popup needs 69,045 visitors, of whom 13,809 see it. That is 11 times less traffic.

But the control group has no popup. Whom do you analyse there? Those who would have been shown it: in the control you log the event "the popup would appear here" by the same rule as in B. Then both groups hold the same kind of people, and the gap between them is the popup's effect. Specialists call this triggered analysis.

But is narrowing a test to a segment always worth it? The concentration rule: if the segment holds a share f of visitors, a test on it alone is faster only when the effect there is at least 1/√f times the average over everyone. For 10% of visitors that is 3.16 times, for 1% it is 10 times. Why the root: the test collects segment people 1/f times more slowly, while the sample it needs shrinks as the square of the effect. In the popup example the effect lives in the segment only: among those who see the popup, every 100 people make 0.5 more purchases, while over all visitors it is 0.1, five times less. The threshold for 20% is 2.24 times, so narrowing pays with room to spare: with equal noise it would save traffic 5 times, and here 11 times, because these people rarely buy and their outcome is less noisy. Now another case: a new checkout form gives everyone +2%, and mobile users, who are 10% of visitors, twice as much, +4%. Twice falls short of the 3.16 threshold, and a test on mobile users alone needs 2.5 times more traffic than a test on everyone.

The most treacherous trap is when the variant itself changes who sees the popup. Say in B it appears not on exit but after 10 seconds, and it is also seen by those who hesitate: they are 20% and buy 3 in 100. Let the popup do nothing at all. Then among those who saw it in B conversion is 2.5% against 2% in the control group, that is +25%. Among the rest it is 7% against 6%, growth too. And the shop as a whole still has 5.2%: people simply moved from one group to the other.

  • In both groups log who would see the change, by one and the same rule, and analyse them.
  • Scale the effect to the whole site by the share of purchases, not the share of people.
  • Narrow a test to a segment with share f only if the effect there is at least 1/√f times the average: for 10% of traffic, more than three times.
  • If the variant changes who counts as "those who saw it", compare the whole site only.

Deeper: how one person changing teams makes both teams stronger.

The Will Rogers phenomenon

One person moves from a strong team to a weak one. Can the average score rise in both teams?
  • This is the Will Rogers phenomenon: move people who are below their group's average but above the other group's, and the average rises in both groups, though nobody started buying more. That is why segments whose borders the change shifts cannot be compared.

Deeper: who should see a discount popup at all.

Who gets the coupon?

A perfect model finds the 250 likeliest buyers, and they get discount coupons. How many purchases does that add?
  • A discount should go not to the likeliest buyers but to those it persuades: the likeliest buyers will buy anyway, and the popup scares some people off. Finding the persuadable takes the same control group.

A win B took from A

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 17, “A year of wins”

"The promo in group B gave +12% sales. Do we roll it out to everyone and expect +12%?"

The test was run honestly: four weeks, an even split, a significant gap. But there is a detail the dashboard doesn't show: the stock.

The shop sells a popular product. 1,000 units arrive each week, but more people want it, 1,100, so it sells out every week.

During the test group B sees a promo on this product, group A doesn't. Both groups buy from the same stock.

Over the four weeks B bought 12% more units than A.

How many units a week will the shop sell if the promo goes to everyone?

There are only 1,000 units, and they sold out without the promo too. A shared stock

The promo brought no new product to the stock: it only helped group B get there first. As many units as B won, A lost, so the gap between the groups doubles: B is 28 units above the usual 500, A 28 below, 56 units in all, the same +12%. Specialists call this interference: one group's result depends on what the other group sees. If the promo is a 10% discount, revenue in the test shows +0.8% for B, while rollout gives −10%: the same 1,000 units, only cheaper.

This happens wherever the groups share something. An ad budget and an auction: if B bids higher, A loses impressions or pays more per click, and the test overstates B's edge. A marketplace: a discount in B takes free couriers or sellers' stock away from A. Referrals and social networks: a person in A sees an invite from a friend in B and gets part of the effect too, so here the test understates it. The same mechanism inside a product is in chapter 12 of the course "Does the feature work?": a new feature takes actions away from an old one.

The "split the stock" box shows the cure: give each group its share of the common resource, the same as its share of people. Then B can't take what isn't its own, and the test shows what rollout will give. That is how ad tests split the budget. Where the resource can't be split, you split not people but whole worlds: cities or regions (cluster, or geo, randomisation), or time, with the whole shop running the promo on some days and not on others (a switchback). The price is fewer independent units, so such tests run longer.

  • Before the test, ask what the groups share: stock, budget, an auction, couriers, friends. If they share something, the gap between the groups is not yet the rollout's effect.
  • Split the common resource in the same proportion as the people; if you can't, randomise cities, regions or days instead of people.
  • After the rollout, check the effect on the whole shop with a long holdout (chapter 17): new sales show only there.

Deeper: can a new road make everyone's trip longer?

Braess's paradox

4,000 drivers take 65 minutes to get to work. The city opens a free, instant shortcut between the routes. Will the trip get shorter or longer?
  • This is Braess's paradox: a new shortcut cuts the trip for everyone who takes it while the others drive as before, but once everyone takes it, every driver needs 80 minutes instead of 65. What helps part of the people can stop working, or hurt, once everyone does it: the test compares a part with the rest, while rollout changes the whole system.

Before the test

  1. The plan is written before the start and shown to colleagues.
  2. One main metric per visitor — not a rate at a later step; intermediate metrics only for diagnosis.
  3. MDE in money, sample and duration worked out in advance, in whole weeks.
  4. No time — an honest compromise is chosen and written down before the start.
  5. Segments named in advance; the rest only as hypotheses.
  6. A few bold variants; if there are many, the 5% threshold is divided by their number and the winner is confirmed in a separate test.
  7. Revenue: the order cap is declared in the plan; conversion and average order are looked at separately.
  8. Email: metric, decision time and group size are written down in advance; the winner is chosen by clicks or purchases, not opens.
  9. Split and count the same unit: people, not sessions.
  10. Not everyone sees the change — in both groups we log who would see it; we analyse them, and scale to the whole site by the share of purchases.
  11. The groups share nothing: stock, budget, an auction, couriers; if they do, split the resource or test by cities or days.

During the test

  1. Don't stop at the first "95%": decisions only on the planned day.
  2. "95% chance to beat" is no reason to stop either: it is peeking with a different number.
  3. B's share stays constant: after a short start, 50/50 to the end; if not, compare within days.
  4. Traffic split as planned: the split's p-value is not below 0.001; losses from cookie consent and blockers are the same in both groups.
  5. Lift week by week: does it melt or grow after the first week, separately for new and returning visitors?

After the test

  1. p-value and confidence interval, not just the lift; "not significant" ≠ "nothing".
  2. Significant is not yet important: does the lift pay for the change?
  3. The report is written: the interval in plain words, the decision with its reason, what we learned, the next test.
  4. Discount the recorded lift of a small test, repeat important wins, and let a long-term holdout show what all the changes gave together.

Noise or effect

"B +17%. Is that a real effect, or just luck?"

Each visitor either buys or not, a bit like a coin. So even two identical pages never give identical numbers. Below that is exactly the case: A and B are the same, both truly convert at 5%, and each gets 1,000 people. Run a few such tests and watch where the lift jumps.

The typical size of this wobble is called the standard error: how far a number from a sample usually lands from the true one. Run hundreds of A/A tests and the lifts pile up into a bell: the normal distribution. The more visitors, the narrower the bell. Notice that lifts of tens of percent are ordinary here, even though the pages do not differ at all.

The A/A test: two identical pages

The same page is shown as "A" and as "B", converting at 5%, with 1,000 visitors each. How often will one of two identical pages "win" by 20% or more?
  • Before celebrating a lift, ask what lift noise alone gives at this number of visitors.
  • Before serious tests, run an A/A test: it checks both your intuition and the tool itself.
  • With 1,000 visitors per page, one of two identical pages "wins" by 20% or more in about 36% of tests. A +17% lift at this sample size proves nothing.

  • Standard error

    How far an average or a rate computed from a sample usually misses the truth: σ divided by the square root of the sample size.

  • Normal distribution

    A bell-shaped distribution: most values sit near the mean, and the further away, the rarer.

2

How many people you need, and how to check

"Fine, noise happens. How many visitors do I need not to be fooled by it? And how do I check my result?"

The size of a test depends on three things: the usual conversion rate, the smallest effect that matters to you, and how strictly you judge. The industry standard: at most 5% false winners between identical pages and 80% power, meaning the test spots a real effect of the chosen size 4 times out of 5. A small effect hides in the noise, so it needs many people: a lift half as big needs about four times the sample.

Once a test has its sample, the result has to be checked. The check answers one question: if the pages were identical, how often would noise produce a gap at least as big as yours? That share is the p-value. If it is below 5%, the result is called significant. The confidence interval shows something else: all the effects your data are still compatible with. "Confidence 94%" in many tools means 1 minus the p-value. It is not the probability that B is better. It only measures how rarely noise produces a gap like this. Distributions, power and the winner's curse are covered in more depth in round 4 of the A/A test puzzle and round 5, with a calculator and a traffic split check.

  • Work out the sample before the start and run the test in whole weeks.
  • Look at the confidence interval, not just the lift: "from −1% to +35%" is more honest than "+17%".
  • "Not significant" does not mean "no effect". The test may simply be too small to see it.

  • p-value

    How often chance alone would produce a gap at least as big as yours if there were really no difference.

  • Confidence interval

    A range around a measured number, built by a method that covers the true value in a chosen share of samples, such as 95%.

  • Statistical power

    The probability that a test notices a real effect of a given size. A power of 80% means the test misses one working effect in five.

May I stop once it shows 95%?

"The test is planned for a month, but on day nine the dashboard already shows 95%. Why wait?"

The dashboard updates every evening, and every evening you want a look. The 95% threshold is designed for one look at the end of the test. What happens when you look every day and stop at the first "95%" is shown in the puzzle below: the two pages are identical, and the marketer checks every evening for 30 days.

Peeking at an A/B test

Two identical pages, a 30-day test, a check every evening and a stop at the first "95%". How often is one of the identical pages declared the winner?
  • Fix the sample and the decision day before the start. In between you may look at the dashboard, but not act on it.
  • Daily looks over 30 days turn one of two identical pages into a "winner" in about 28% of tests instead of the promised 5%. Every look gives noise one more chance.
  • If you really need to be able to stop early, plan the looks in advance with stricter bounds (next chapter).

I can't wait a month

5

But it won in Poland!

"Overall B did nothing. But in Poland, on mobile, among new customers, it is significantly better. Ship it there?"

After a test that showed nothing, it is tempting to slice the result: countries, devices, traffic sources, new and returning customers. Each slice is a separate check, and each has its own chance of a false winner. The puzzle below works out what happens when a new email is no better than the old one and the result is split into 20 countries.

Slice it until something wins

The new email is no better than the old one. The analyst checks the result in 20 countries. What is the chance at least one shows a "significant" difference?
  • Name the segments that matter before the start. A segment win found after the test is a hypothesis for a new test, not a conclusion.
  • With 20 independent slices and no real effect at all, the chance of at least one "significant" one is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. With many slices, each one needs a stricter threshold (the Bonferroni or Holm corrections).

Case: a careful rollout

"We started B on 10% of traffic and added more every day. B is better on every day of the week. So it is better for the week too?"

A gradual rollout sounds sensible: if B is broken, few people suffer. But it quietly changes which days end up in each group. A shop gets 10,000 visitors a day. On Monday 10% of them go to B, the share grows every day, and by Sunday it is half. People here buy half as often on weekends as on weekdays. And every day, B converts 5% better than A.

The classic version of this trap is in the catalogue. If you want to solve it yourself, do it before the case.

Simpson's paradox

Treatment A beats treatment B for small kidney stones and for large ones. Can B be better for all the patients together?

  • Compare variants within the same day (week, channel), and then average those comparisons. Pooling all visitors mixes B's effect with changes in traffic.
  • The simplest cure: keep B's share constant for the whole test. If you did ramp up, analyse only the period with a constant share, or weight by day.
  • The same happens when a newsletter or ad campaign with a different audience arrives mid-test while the groups' shares are unequal.

Case: fewer buy from the cart

"A new product card: more people add to cart, but fewer buy from the cart. Cart conversion fell from 40% to 33%. Is B worse?"

Visitors differ. Some came to buy, some are comparing, some are just browsing. The new card does not change those who were ready anyway. It persuades some of the lukewarm to put the item in the cart. These new people buy less often than those who added to cart before, so the share of carts that end in a purchase falls. Each dot below stands for 10 visitors.

People are split at random only at the entrance: each visitor has an equal chance of seeing A or B. So A and B are fairly comparable only at the level of all visitors. Those who reached the cart have already been selected by the page itself, and in B they are different people than in A.

Berkson's paradox

In town, looks and kindness are unrelated. You only date people who are good-looking and kind enough together. Are the two linked among your dates?

Survivorship bias: Wald's planes

Bombers come back with holes in the fuselage and wings, and hardly any on the engines. Where should the armour go?
  • Compute the main metric per randomised visitor: purchases or revenue per visitor. Rates at later funnel steps help with diagnosis, not with the verdict.
  • The same goes for the average order value: if B brings more small purchases, the average order falls while revenue per visitor rises.
  • This is a relative of Berkson's paradox: selecting by a later step creates a picture that is not there in the whole crowd, just as "good-looking" and "kind" become opposites among the people you date.

Case: +15% in the first week, then nothing

"After a week of testing the new banner gave +15%. We shipped it, and a month later revenue is the same as before. Where did the effect go?"

Returning customers notice something new and click out of curiosity. After a few days they get used to it, and the effect fades. Below is a model of such a test: the banner's true long-run effect is +2%, but on the first day novelty adds another +30%, and that extra halves every two days. Each variant gets 5,000 people a day, A converts at 5%. Choose the day you stop the test.

Even a month does not fully save you: the cumulative lift averages all days, and the first week pulls it up. It is more honest to look at the lift in the last weeks, or at the lift by days since a person's first visit: if it is large in the first days of acquaintance and then melts, that is novelty. The reverse happens too: people get used to an unfamiliar interface, and the effect grows over time.

  • Run the test in whole weeks and look at the lift week by week: the first week apart from the rest.
  • After the launch, check the effect again, for example with a small holdout group (chapter 9).

Regression to the mean

After praise players do worse, and after criticism they do better. Does that mean criticism works?
  • Besides novelty, regression to the mean is at work: a test stopped when the lift looked its best records a lucky streak of noise, and the following days will most likely be more ordinary.

Case: the wins add up, revenue doesn't

"Over the year we shipped every significant winner. In the report they add up to a big gain. Why has revenue barely moved?"

A team runs 40 tests a year. Only some of the ideas really work, and their effect is modest: +5% on a 5% conversion rate. The other ideas change nothing. Every significant winner goes into the product, and its lift from the dashboard is written into the report. Assume also that half a year later a third of a real effect has faded, as in chapter 8. Compare three numbers: what was written in the report, what was real, and what a holdout group that saw the old site all year will show.

Three mechanisms are at work here. First: false winners, empty ideas that won thanks to noise. Second: the winner's curse. A low-power test lets a real idea through only when noise happened to help it this time, so the recorded lift of a real winner is usually several times its true effect. Third: effects fade after the launch. The bigger the sample, the weaker the first two mechanisms: move the second slider to the right.

The false positive paradox

1% of people have a disease. The test is 99% accurate, and you test positive. What is the chance you're really ill?
  • Don't add up lifts from dashboards. Keep a long-term holdout: a small share of people who do not see the shipped changes. It alone shows what all the changes gave together.
  • Discount the recorded lift of a small test by half or more: the lower the power, the bigger the exaggeration.
  • A big effect from an important change deserves a repeat test.
  • The share of false winners among the winners depends on how rare good ideas are. It is the same mechanism as in the false positive paradox: when the real thing is rare, even an accurate test gives many false "yes" answers.

Case: we counted sessions, not people

"The tool splits people into A and B, and the report shows session conversion. What's the difference?"

One loyal customer comes twenty times a month, and those sessions are alike: if they like to buy, they buy often. For the test they are not 20 independent observations but roughly one. A check that counts every session separately believes it has far more data than it does, and so sees "significant" differences where there are none. How wrong the check is can be estimated with the simple design-effect formula: the noise is really 1 + (m − 1)·ρ times bigger, where m is the number of sessions per person (for unequal visits, the average weighted by sessions) and ρ is how alike one person's sessions are. When everyone visits once, or one person's sessions are independent, the formula gives 1 and there is no error.

  • Analyse at the level you randomise: if you split people, count people.
  • If the metric is still per session (clicks per session, session conversion), compute its error by people: with the delta method or a bootstrap that resamples people, not sessions.
  • An A/A test with noticeably more than 5% "significant" results often catches exactly this mistake.

  • Variance and standard deviation

    A measure of spread: how far values typically stray from the mean. The standard deviation is its square root, in the same units.

  • Independence

    Events are independent if knowing about one doesn't change the chances of the other.

A/B test checklist

Before the test

  1. One main metric per visitor — purchases or revenue per randomised person, not a rate at a later step.
  2. Sample and duration worked out in advance, in whole weeks.
  3. No time — an honest compromise is chosen and written down before the start.
  4. Segments named in advance; the rest only as hypotheses.
  5. Split and count the same unit: people, not sessions.

During the test

  1. Don't stop at the first "95%": decisions only on the planned day.
  2. B's share stays constant; if not, compare within days.
  3. Traffic split as planned, and an A/A test gives about 5% "significant" results.
  4. Lift week by week: does it melt after the first week?

After the test

  1. p-value and confidence interval, not just the lift; "not significant" ≠ "nothing".
  2. Discount the recorded lift of a small test, and repeat important wins.
  3. A long-term holdout shows what all the changes gave together.

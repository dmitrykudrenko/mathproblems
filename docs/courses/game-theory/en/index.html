Game theory for business

Coffee beans, 250 gthis morning
Marta14 $ The shop next door13 $ a dollar lower

A pack costs both of them $8, and customers buy wherever it's cheaper. What should Marta do?

In most business decisions the outcome depends on more than you. You cut the price, and the rival answers. You trust a supplier, and he decides whether keeping his word pays. You sign a contract up to a set date, and the other person counts how much is left. Game theory studies exactly these situations: when the others think too. The course follows Marta. She roasts coffee and sells it online, and every chapter brings her a new game: a price war, Black Friday discounts, a supplier, a remote team, a fight for a hire, negotiations and a new "fast road" that makes things worse for everyone. Each game gets one rule, and a simulation on the page checks it.

≈ 45–60 minutes, in 5-minute parts if you like; your progress is saved. The maths is school fractions and simple probability.

Part 1 · no formulas

Your rival thinks too

Four situations where the decision is made once: a price, positioning, a Black Friday discount and guessing what the others will do. In each, your result depends not only on you but on what a rival who also does the maths decides. Each chapter starts with a question: guess first, then check.

1

A dollar cheaper

"The shop next door cut its price by a dollar. I'll go a dollar below them and the customers come back. What's there to think about?"

Marta roasts coffee and sells it online. The example is made up, but its numbers are honest. A pack costs Marta $8, and she sells it for $14. Another online shop sells the same arabica from the same farm, also for $14. Every week 100 customers look for this coffee and buy where it's cheaper, and at equal prices they split in half. So each shop sells 50 packs and makes $300 a week.

This week the rival went to $13 and took every customer. Either can cut by a dollar at any time, and customers see both prices in a second.

Both shops answer every cut as long as it pays them. At what price does it stop?

While the price is above $10, undercutting the rival by a dollar pays more than matching: the whole market instead of half. At $10 it's a draw, and the war stops. Each makes $100 a week instead of $300. And with cuts of a cent, the price would go almost down to $8. Play as Marta below.About $10. The price war week by week

You set Marta's price for the week. Next week the rival picks the price that earns him the most against yours.

Every step here is sensible: a dollar off buys a week of the whole market. But the rival does the same maths, and within a few weeks both sell at $10. The first to cut won one week, and both lost. Back in 1883 the French mathematician Joseph Bertrand noticed: if the product is the same and the customer goes where it's cheaper, two sellers are already enough to push the price down to cost. Raising it back doesn't work either: whoever raises first is left without customers.

  • When the product is the same, a price war eats both margins. Don't start it: you win a week, and the market stays at the bottom.
  • Compete on what can't be copied in a click: roast freshness, a subscription, service, your own blend. How to choose your place in the market is in chapter 2.
  • There is also a way to make undercutting pointless for the rival, but that's chapter 11.
Story In 1838 Antoine Cournot described two sellers choosing how much to produce and got a price well above cost. In an 1883 review Bertrand objected: sellers compete on prices, and then each undercuts the other until the profit is gone. Since then the "Bertrand paradox" is explained like this: the price stays above cost only when products differ, capacity is limited or the game repeats (part 2). Source: J. Bertrand, a review of Cournot's and Walras's books, Journal des Savants, 1883, pp. 499–508.

Everyone on the same beach

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “A dollar cheaper”

"Why do all coffee shops look alike: the same medium roast, the same landing pages, newsletters at 10 in the morning?"

After the price war (chapter 1) Marta decided to differ in taste. The roast can be chosen on a scale from 0 (very light) to 100 (very dark). Customers' tastes are spread evenly along the scale, and each buys the coffee nearest to their taste. Prices are equal. Right now Marta is at 25 and the rival at 75: each gets half the customers.

Why do rivals' products so often drift towards the same thing?

Here customers don't like the middle more than the ends: tastes are even. But whoever moves towards the rival keeps their own customers at the edge and takes some of his. This logic works wherever a customer can be "bitten off" from the neighbour: roast, pricing plans, the tone of a landing page, the send time of a newsletter. Each step towards the neighbour pays the one who takes it, and so the market pulls together into sameness. Customers at the edges lose: their taste is further from what's on offer.

But in this model prices are equal. If two coffees sit next to each other on the scale, they're almost the same to the customer, and the price war from chapter 1 begins. So when they compete on price too, sellers do better to move apart: d'Aspremont, Gabszewicz and Thisse showed this in 1979. Whoever differs can hold the price.

  • If your product sits where the rival's does, price is the only thing left to compete on.
  • Take an edge the rival doesn't want: a dark roast for espresso, a 7 a.m. newsletter for early risers.
  • Before copying a rival's move towards the "average", count how many edge customers you'll lose.
Sources H. Hotelling, "Stability in Competition", Economic Journal, 1929; C. d'Aspremont, J. J. Gabszewicz, J.-F. Thisse, "On Hotelling's 'Stability in Competition'", Econometrica, 1979.

Ice cream on the beach

A kilometre of beach and two ice cream sellers who can move their kiosks. Every customer walks to the nearest one. Where will the sellers stand?

The end of this game is both in the middle, at 50, each with half the customers, as at the start. But the average distance from taste to coffee doubles: 25 points instead of 12.5 when Marta and the rival stood at 25 and 75. The sellers gained nothing, and the customers lost. Harold Hotelling used this in 1929 to explain why shops, parties and churches resemble each other.

The discount dilemma

"Everyone discounts on Black Friday. Maybe skip it this time? If only the neighbour skips it too."

Black Friday is coming. Marta and the rival shop each decide on their own: a 20% discount or none. A pack costs $20 and $8 to make: without a discount Marta makes $12 a pack, with one (a $16 price) $8. Each shop has 100 regulars who buy at their own shop at any price. Another 300 bargain hunters go where it's cheaper, and at equal prices split in half. Agreeing isn't an option: that's price fixing, and nobody trusts anybody anyway.

Both shops count the money and decide once. What do they do?

If the rival doesn't discount, a discount gives Marta $3,200 against $3,000. If the rival discounts, a discount gives $2,000 against $1,200. Discounting is better whatever the rival does, and he reasons the same way. Both make $2,000, though without discounts they'd make $3,000 each. Check the table below.Both discount. This is the prisoner's dilemma, the best-known game in game theory. A discount here is a dominant strategy: it's better whatever the rival decides. The pair "both discount" is a Nash equilibrium: neither gains by changing their decision alone. And at the same time both are worse off than if both had held back. Each acted sensibly, and together it turned out badly.

  • Before working out whether a discount pays, add to the table what the rival will do. A discount that "pays" with the rival standing still may not pay once everyone discounts.
  • The way out of the dilemma isn't hoping the neighbour is honest but changing the game: loyal customers who don't chase discounts (chapter 2), a repeated game (chapter 5), commitment (chapter 11).
  • Agreeing prices with a rival is not an option: it's illegal almost everywhere.
Story The game was devised in 1950 at the RAND Corporation by the mathematicians Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher. That same year Albert Tucker, explaining it to psychologists at Stanford, retold the payoffs as a story of two prisoners, and the name stuck. Source: W. Poundstone, "Prisoner's Dilemma", 1992; M. M. Flood, "Some Experimental Games", RAND RM-789, 1952.

  • Nash equilibrium

    A set of strategies where nobody gains by changing their own while the others keep theirs.

  • Dominant strategy

    An option that gives a player more than any other option of theirs, whatever the others choose.

The traveler's dilemma

Two broken vases; each owner writes a price from $2 to $100. Both get the lower one, with a $2 bonus for writing it. What would you write?

The traveler's dilemma is the same trap stretched over a hundred steps: each "a dollar less" is sensible, and the chain leads to $2, the worst pair for both. But real people write 95–100 and earn more than the "rational" ones. Kaushik Basu described it in 1994 as exactly a paradox of rationality: logic that both know to the end hurts both.

Guess what the others will guess

"I'm running a contest for subscribers. I want to know in advance which number wins, so the prize doesn't go by chance."

Marta announces a contest in her newsletter: everyone names a whole number from 0 to 100, and whoever is closest to 2/3 of the average of all numbers wins. The prize is a kilo of coffee. The number depends only on how the others think. Whoever assumes the others pick at random (average 50) will name 33: that's one step. Whoever thinks everyone takes that step will name 22: two steps. Three steps give 15, and so on all the way down to 0.

How many steps of this chain should you take to win among ordinary subscribers?

In experiments people mostly take one or two steps, so the average is far from 0, and the target is one step past the majority. Zero would win only if almost everyone went to the end. The mix of players here is illustrative: those "at random" name a random number from 0 to 100, the rest 33, 22 or 0. Real contests are noisier still, but the picture is the same.

The most logical answer, 0, wins only among people who also go to the end. Among ordinary people it's far from the target. The winner isn't whoever thinks deepest but whoever guessed correctly how deep the others think and took one step more. In 1936 John Maynard Keynes compared the stock market to this: you should pick not what you like, but what others think others will like.

Business works the same way. A sale should start a little before most rivals, but not so early that customers aren't thinking about gifts yet. A price is set a little below what rivals will actually charge, not what they'd charge if they reasoned to the end.

  • Model how many steps your rivals and customers take, not the "rational" end of the chain.
  • Your best move is one step past the majority, not ten.
  • Check your assumptions about others with data: past seasons, tests, what rivals did before.
Sources J. M. Keynes, "The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money", 1936, chapter 12; R. Nagel, "Unraveling in Guessing Games: An Experimental Study", American Economic Review, 1995; R. H. Thaler, "From Homo Economicus to Homo Sapiens", Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2000.

Guess 2/3 of the average

Everyone names a whole number from 0 to 100. Whoever is closest to two thirds of the average of all the numbers wins. Which number do you name?

The real numbers. In Rosemarie Nagel's experiment (1995), students gave a first-round average of 36.73, and none named 0. In 1997 Richard Thaler ran the game in the Financial Times: 1,476 readers gave an average of 18.91, and 13 won. The most popular guesses were 33 and 22, that is one and two steps, though there were plenty of zeros too.

What's next In a game played once, logic pushes everyone to a bad outcome: the price at the bottom, everyone alike, everyone discounting. But business isn't one game. You meet the same supplier, rival and team every month, and that changes the maths. That's the second part.

Tit for tat with a supplier

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 3, “The discount dilemma”

"My supplier slipped me cheaper beans once already. Forgive or punish?"

Every month Marta buys green coffee from one supplier. Each of the two can play fair or cut corners: Marta can pay on time or drag out the payment, the supplier can ship the agreed grade or mix in a cheaper one. The monthly gain works like the discount table in chapter 3, so this is a prisoner's dilemma again: both fair, $300 each; both cutting corners, $100 each; whoever cheats a fair partner gets $500, and the fair one gets $0.

Unlike Black Friday, this game repeats, and each sees what the other did last month. The simplest rule for such a game is tit for tat: play fair in the first month, then do whatever the partner did last month.

Marta and the supplier both play tit for tat and work fairly. In month 4 the supplier's warehouse slips up, and a lower-grade batch arrives, though he didn't mean to cheat. What happens next if nobody explains anything?

In month 5 Marta copies the "trick" and drags out the payment. The supplier copies that in month 6, Marta answers again in month 7, and so to the end of the year they take turns being fair and not. The grudger plays fair until cheated, then cuts corners to the end. The forgiving rule punishes only after two tricks in a row. The bad batch happens with every supplier.

faircuts cornersMonths 1 to 12, the supplier plays tit for tat.

With no accidents, tit for tat and the grudger earn the same: both play fair with the fair and don't let themselves be cheated twice. One bad batch shows the difference. After it the grudger cuts corners to the end of the year, even with a fair supplier. Tit for tat falls into an echo: each answer becomes the reason for the next. And the forgiving rule lets one trick pass and loses almost nothing.

  • Don't cheat first, and answer a trick at once: the partner must see it doesn't go unnoticed.
  • Forgive as soon as the partner is back to fair play: long revenge costs more than the trick itself.
  • Before you punish, ask what happened, and own up to your own slips first: one accident between two tit-for-tat players starts an echo.
  • Say your rules out loud: "a late payment means prepayment next month". A clear rule works better than a clever one.
Source The four rules (don't cheat first, retaliate, forgive, be clear) are Robert Axelrod's conclusions from computer tournaments of the prisoner's dilemma. Later he and Jianzhong Wu showed that when random errors happen, more forgiving rules work better, as does the habit of correcting your own mistake at once. Sources: R. Axelrod, "The Evolution of Cooperation", 1984; J. Wu, R. Axelrod, "How to Cope with Noise in the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma", Journal of Conflict Resolution, 1995.

Axelrod's tournament

Programs play the prisoner's dilemma for 200 rounds, each against each. Who scores most: an eternal defector, a sneak, Tit for Tat or a coin toss?

In Axelrod's first tournament in 1980, programs played the prisoner's dilemma for 200 rounds, each against each. Both the first tournament (14 programs) and the second (62) were won by Anatol Rapoport's tit for tat, the shortest program of all. It didn't beat a single opponent in a match, since it never scores more than its partner, but it collected many points with those who played fair.

When the end is in sight

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 5, “Tit for tat with a supplier”

"A one-year contract, everything in writing. Why does the work slow down near the end?"

Marta hires a contractor for 12 months to run her ads and newsletters. Every month each decides how to play: the contractor can try hard or do a sloppy job, Marta can approve and pay the bonus on time or drag her feet. The gains are the same as with the supplier (chapter 5): $300 each for a fair month, $500 to whoever cheats a fair partner, $0 to the fair one and $100 each if both cut corners. Both know that month 12 is the last.

Both are purely rational and think ahead. By strict logic, from which month does the slacking start?

In month 12 nothing can reward fairness any more, so both cut corners. Then month 11 changes nothing about month 12 and becomes the "last" one itself, and so on back to the first. This is backward induction. Real people don't play it through: they cooperate almost to the end, and the slide starts a few steps before it. When there is no end date, every month there's a chance of another one. A trick gains $200 at once (500 instead of 300), but then the partner stops trusting, and each following month brings $100 instead of $300, that is, $200 less. If the work goes on with chance p each month, on average p/(1 − p) months lie ahead. A trick doesn't pay when that is at least one, that is, when the chance of a next month is at least 1/2. In general the threshold is (500 − 300)/(500 − 100), and with these numbers that's exactly 1/2.

So it's not about how long the work lasts but about whether the end is in sight. By strict logic a 12-month contract with a known date unravels completely, while work with no date that lasts only 2 months on average (a 1/2 chance of going on) already holds, just at the threshold.

  • Where you need effort to the last day, don't announce a hard end date, or make the end "soft": a chance of extension, a next project.
  • Tie the reward to the future: a bonus paid after the end, a reference, a share of the result.
  • If the end is fixed, pay for the result at the end rather than for the time: then the last month counts too.
From the author's practice "We signed a contract under which an employee was to carry out tasks and benefit from future earnings on what he was doing now. Towards the end of the contract term his motivation dropped sharply. We decided that from then on it's better not to state a clear end date for the work, because otherwise motivation swings." — Dmytro Kudrenko

The theory explains such a slide, though in a real contract ordinary money motives play a part too: near the end a person is already thinking about the next job.

Research Reinhard Selten and Rolf Stoecker had 35 people play the prisoner's dilemma 25 times for 10 rounds each, with a different partner every time. Experienced players cooperated almost to the end and then defected. A later review of experiments, with a new experiment of its own, found the same: once cooperation is established, with experience the first defection moves to an earlier round. Sources: R. Selten, R. Stoecker, "End behavior in sequences of finite Prisoner's Dilemma supergames", Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 1986; M. Embrey, G. Fréchette, S. Yuksel, "Cooperation in the Finitely Repeated Prisoner's Dilemma", Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2018.

  • Backward induction

    Solving a game from the end: the last move first, then the one before it, back to the first.

How many ties you have

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 5, “Tit for tat with a supplier”

"We're going remote. If I trust people in the office, I'll trust them online too, right?"

Marta has a team now: roasting, support, marketing. Helping a colleague means spending an hour of your own work to save them several. Let an hour of help save a colleague 5 hours: benefit b = 5, cost c = 1. Whoever helps, helps everyone they work with. Whoever doesn't, pays nothing but enjoys the help of others. People watch their neighbours and pick up the habit of whoever is doing better.

In the office everyone works with 4 people every day. After the move to remote, everyone has 24 contacts in chats and calls, a little with each.

In which team does the habit of helping survive, if 5 hours of benefit per 1 hour of cost is the same for both?

Hisashi Ohtsuki, Martin Nowak and colleagues found a simple rule: helping takes hold when the benefit divided by the cost exceeds the number of ties, b/c > k. Here 5 > 4, but 5 < 24. With few ties, helpers stick together and mostly help each other. With many, every helper also feeds dozens of non-helpers. Run the simulation below.With 4 contacts. At each step someone leaves the team, and the newcomer in that place takes the habit of one of its neighbours; the more that neighbour earns, the better its chance. Half help at the start, and the simulation runs 150 steps per person.

The rule b/c > k is an approximation: it was derived for a weak effect of earnings on choices and for large networks. On a grid your neighbours' neighbours are often your neighbours too, so in the simulation the threshold is a bit below k, especially for 24 ties. But the direction is the same: the more ties, the bigger the benefit of help must be for helping to survive. Helpers survive in clusters: inside a cluster they get help from their own, and at its edge they lose to those who only take.

  • Trust is built in small stable teams where people work together for a long time: help comes back from the same people.
  • When ties are many and shallow (remote work, freelance marketplaces, big chats), replace unconditional trust with conditional trust: tit for tat from chapter 5, fair first, but with an answer to a trick.
  • Make help cheaper or more valuable: the bigger b/c, the more ties cooperation can bear.
From the author's practice "The more outside ties, the more it pays to defect, as in the prisoner's dilemma: cooperation holds in closed groups. So when moving to remote work, unconditional trust can turn out to be a bad strategy." — Dmytro Kudrenko

Just a bit more and it's ours

"We've been fighting for this roaster for four weeks. One more week and he's ours."

The best roaster in town is looking for a new job, and two want him: Marta and a big coffee chain. To each he's worth $10,000 of extra profit. While the fight goes on, every week costs each side $500: the recruiter, counter-offers, an empty position. Whoever gives up gets nothing and doesn't get the money back. Whoever holds out longer makes the hire.

Nobody knows in advance how long the rival will hold out. Game theory has an equilibrium for such a fight: each side may give up at any moment with a small chance, set so that one more week of fighting is neither a gain nor a loss for the rival.

Both sides play this equilibrium. How much do they spend on the fight together, on average?

On average the fight lasts 10 weeks, each side pays $5,000, $10,000 together: the whole value of the hire. Each side breaks even on average: everything there was to win has been burnt on the fight. Once each side has spent $2,000 over four weeks, the next week seems a trifle: "another $500 and the hire is ours". But that $2,000 won't come back whatever happens, and the next week, like every week before it, is worth zero on average. The trap is thinking about what's spent, when the only decision is the next step. The same trap awaits buyers at an auction; chapter 2 of the auctions course is about it.

The price of a week only changes the length. The cheaper the week, the longer the fight drags on, and together the sides still spend $10,000: in equilibrium the war of attrition burns the whole value of the prize.

  • Decide where you'll get out before you start: an amount and a date. During the fight, what's spent pulls you to go on.
  • Leave what's spent out of the sum: ask only whether the next step is worth its money.
  • Look for a way out of the war itself: another candidate, another role, a deal with the rival. A war between equals eats the whole prize on average.
Story John Maynard Smith described the war of attrition in 1974 for animals competing for territory: whoever holds out longer wins, and time costs both. He showed that in equilibrium the length of the contest is random, and together the rivals spend on average the whole value of the territory. Source: J. Maynard Smith, "The theory of games and the evolution of animal conflicts", Journal of Theoretical Biology, 1974.

The dollar auction

A one-dollar bill is up for auction. Both top bidders pay, but only the winner gets the dollar. How much does the seller usually collect?

In Martin Shubik's dollar auction (1971) both top bidders pay, and the bidding usually goes past a dollar: each step seems worth it, because giving up loses your bid. Shubik wrote that total payments of 3 to 5 dollars for one dollar are not uncommon. The war of attrition is the same game with weeks instead of bids: in equilibrium the sides together burn the whole prize, and people who can't stop in time burn more.

What's next In a repeated game what matters is not only what you do but who moves first and what they have promised. Tit for tat works when each sees the other's move and can answer. But often someone moves first, someone writes the rules, and someone ties their own hands with a promise that can't be undone. That's the third part.

Who moves first and who writes the rules

Three rivals instead of two, yes-or-no negotiations, a promise that can't be taken back, and a new option that makes everyone worse off. Here what matters is not only what you do, but in what order the players move and who sets the rules of the game.

Three in the market

"Mega is taking my customers. Should I strike it first, before it's too late?"

Marta's coffee is now sold in three places in the city, and her niche has two more players: a mid-sized roastery, Brew, and a chain, Mega. Each of the three may go on the attack once a month against one rival: steep discounts aimed at its range, poaching its café clients. Marta's attack pushes a rival out of the niche with probability 1/3, Brew's with 2/3, and Mega, with its budget, never misses. They take turns: Marta, Brew, Mega and round again, until one is left in the niche. A month may also be skipped, attacking nobody.

It's a simplified model, but it catches the main thing: each knows the others' strength and asks who is the biggest danger to them.

Whom should Marta attack in the first month?

If Marta skips her turn, Brew and Mega attack each other: each is the main threat to the other. Whoever survives, the next move is Marta's, and she stays in the niche with probability 39.7%. Marta's first move and who stays in the niche

After Marta's first move everyone plays their best: Brew attacks Mega, Mega attacks Brew, and Marta skips while both are in, then attacks whoever is left.

Attacking the strongest seems logical, but Mega is under fire anyway. Brew strikes Mega, because while Mega is in the game its next attack is on Brew. And Mega strikes Brew, because Brew is more dangerous than Marta. Marta's weakness becomes her shield: nobody deals with her first. And a successful attack only hurts Marta: then she is left one-on-one with the survivor, who moves first.

  • In a three-way fight the weakest gains by staying out of the leaders' war: their fight works for them.
  • Before you attack, ask who will come after you next if the attack succeeds.
  • For leaders: a price war between the two of you often helps number three most. Watch number three.

The truel

Three shoot in turn: Ann hits 1 shot in 3, Bob 2 in 3, Sam always. Who has the best chance of being the last one standing?

The truel has the same numbers: Ann hits with 1 shot in 3, Bob with 2 in 3, Sam always. And the worst shot has the best chance of surviving if she fires her first shot into the air: 39.7% for Ann, 38.1% for Bob and only 22.2% for Sam the sharpshooter. Marta is the same: the weakest of the three, yet the one most often left in the niche.

Look from the end

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 6, “When the end is in sight”

"The recipe is mine, the brand is mine. Ten dollars should do for the partner: it beats nothing, right?"

A café chain wants to sell Marta's signature blend under both logos. The joint blend will bring $1,000 of extra profit a month, and nothing without both agreeing. The chain said it plainly: "Name the split once. If we agree, we launch; if not, we part." There's no haggling: this game is called the ultimatum.

Look from the end, as in chapter 6. The last move is the chain's: accept or refuse. Any amount beats zero, so a sensible partner will agree even to $10. Knowing that, Marta can offer $10 and keep $990. That's what logic says. And what do people say?

How much of the $1,000 should Marta offer the chain to earn the most on average?

About $400. People reject offers they find unfair, even when it costs them. In experiments in industrialised countries, offers below 20% were rejected 40–60% of the time. And in a review of 75 experiments the average offer was 40%, and such offers were almost always accepted. At $10 Marta will almost surely hear "no", and $500 gives away $100 too many. Reasoning from the end isn't wrong, it's just incomplete: the partner's payoff holds more than money. Turning down $10 costs the partner $10, but it gives the feeling of not being cheated and shows Marta she can't treat them that way. So solve the game from the end, but put a real person at the end: what they get if they say no, and what "fair" means to them.

  • First solve the negotiation from the end: what each side gets if there's no deal.
  • Then add the person: a split that looks unfair gets rejected, even at a loss. Fairness is part of the payoff.
  • The better the partner's alternative, the more they will ask. A stronger position is a better alternative of your own, not pressure.

Pirates and 100 gold coins

Five logical pirates split 100 coins, and a captain whose split is not backed by half the crew goes overboard. How many coins does he keep?

In the pirates' puzzle perfectly logical pirates reason from the end, so the captain keeps 98 coins and gives one each to pirates 3 and 5. Real people would hardly agree to such a split: one coin in a hundred is 1%, and that gets rejected even in the lab.

Tie your own hands

This builds on what you haven't done yet: chapter 1, “A dollar cheaper”chapter 3, “The discount dilemma”

"The rival's site now says: 'Find it cheaper and we'll match the price.' Will he lure away all the customers now?"

Remember the two shops from chapter 1: cost $8, price $14, and customers go wherever it's cheaper? And the discount dilemma from chapter 3: a discount pays for each shop alone, but both are worse off together. Now both shops print the same promise: "Find it cheaper and we'll match the price." The match is automatic, no screenshots needed.

What happens to prices once both shops make this promise?

A promise to match is a reply to a discount announced in advance: "cut, and we'll do the same at once." Then a discount wins no customers, it only shrinks the margin. With the prices from chapter 1, for every 100 customers each shop earns $300 at $14, and $250 after a cut to $13 that is matched at once. One promise isn't enough: against the shop that didn't promise to match, a discount still wins customers, and the dilemma returns. When both promise, "no discount" is never worse than a discount. Notice what the promise did: it gave the shop nothing now, it took away a choice. Thomas Schelling wrote about this as burning bridges: an army with nowhere to retreat convinces its opponent better than any words, because its threat is believable.

To a customer a price-match promise sounds caring. Economists see something else: in 1986 Steven Salop described such guarantees as a way for rivals to hold prices up without any collusion. But there's a limit: if a match means finding a receipt, sending a screenshot and waiting, some customers won't bother, a discount wins some of them again, and the promise "leaks".

  • A public promise that's hard to undo can be worth more than room to manoeuvre: it changes what pays for the rival.
  • A promise works only if it's believed: public, automatic, with no extra steps for the customer.
  • For customers: a lowest-price guarantee is a sign of a quiet market, not a cheap one. Compare prices yourself.
North Carolina supermarkets James Hess and Eitan Gerstner tracked prices after a North Carolina supermarket chain promised to match prices and its rival answered with the same promise. The authors concluded that the data support the theory: the promises helped the stores avoid price competition, and prices were generally higher. Newcomb's paradox

Transparent box A holds $1,000. A predictor that is right 99% of the time put a million in box B only if it foresaw you taking B alone. What will you take: only B or both?

In Newcomb's paradox the predictor puts the million in for those who predictably take one box: those who take only B leave with $990,000 on average, and those who take both with $11,000. It's the same mechanism as commitment: when others can predict your choice, it pays to be the one who has tied their own hands in advance.

A new road, worse for everyone

"I'll add free express delivery. Whoever wants it gets their parcel sooner, and the rest are no worse off, right?"

Marta now ships 4,000 orders a week across the country. Customers choose the delivery themselves and see a live estimate of how many hours it will take. There are two options. "Courier and post": Marta's courier takes the parcel to the post depot, and the more parcels, the longer it takes (number / 100 hours: 2,000 parcels, 20 hours), then the post, always 45 hours. "Post and courier": the post picks the parcel up at the roastery and carries it to the customer's city in 45 hours, where a local partner courier delivers it, also number / 100 hours. Customers have split evenly, 2,000 each, and every parcel travels 20 + 45 = 65 hours.

Now Marta arranges for her courier to hand parcels straight to the local partner at one depot, with no delay at all. The new option, "Courier to courier", is free, and every customer still picks the fastest one for themselves.

How long will a parcel travel on average once customers get used to the new option?

80 hours. While few choose express, it takes about 40 hours, and every customer who compares the estimates switches to it. But everyone does, and both couriers carry 4,000 parcels each: 40 + 40 = 80 hours. Nobody gains by switching back: the old option now takes 40 + 45 = 85 hours. Move the slider below.More: 80 hours. How many customers choose "Courier to courier"

A parcel travels on averageexact, the other customers split evenly

Each customer compares only their own hours and doesn't see that their parcel adds hours for everyone using the same courier. The best for all would be to let only 500 parcels take the new option: then 64.7 hours on average. Free choice gives 80. The ratio between what free choice gives and the best possible split is called the price of anarchy. In 2002 Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos proved that when the time on each leg grows linearly with the load, this ratio never exceeds 4/3. Here it is 80 / 64.7 ≈ 1.24.

  • Before adding a channel or an option, ask how everyone will react to it, not one customer.
  • Measure the average time of the whole system, not the time of the new option: it can be the fastest while the system gets slower.
  • Sometimes the best move is to remove an option or limit it: a quota, a price or a cap bring each person's choice closer to the best for all.
Sources D. Braess, "Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung", Unternehmensforschung, 1968 (the numbers come from the best-known example for it); T. Roughgarden, É. Tardos, "How Bad Is Selfish Routing?", Journal of the ACM, 2002.

Braess's paradox

4,000 drivers take 65 minutes to get to work. The city opens a free, instant shortcut between the routes. Will the trip get shorter or longer?

These are the same numbers as in Braess's classic example: 4,000 drivers, narrow streets at T/100 minutes, highways at 45, and a free shortcut turns 65 minutes into 80. Close the shortcut, and everyone gains 15 minutes. Delivery works the same way: removing "Courier to courier" gives every parcel back 15 hours.

A checklist before you decide

One question from each chapter. Go through them before you change a price, sign a contract or sit down to negotiate.

A one-off decision

  1. Is my product easy to compare on price? Then a price war eats both margins: be different.
  2. Am I moving towards the rival? The middle takes his customers but makes you the same.
  3. What will the rival do in reply? Count the whole table, not just your row.
  4. How many steps do the others think? One step past most people, not to the end of the chain.

A repeated game

  1. Will we meet again? Be fair first, answer cheating, forgive quickly.
  2. Is the end of the work known in advance? Tie the reward to what comes after it.
  3. How many ties does each person have? The more and the shallower they are, the more careful with unconditional trust.
  4. Am I escalating for what's already spent? Set the exit point before the fight begins.

Moves and rules

  1. Are there three of us in the market? The weakest often gains from staying out of the fight.
  2. How does the negotiation end? Solve it from the end, then add the human part: fairness is part of the payoff too.
  3. Can I make a commitment? A public promise that is hard to undo changes the rival's moves.
  4. How will everyone react to the new option? Each picks what suits them, and the average can get worse.

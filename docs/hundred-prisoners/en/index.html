The puzzle

A hundred prisoners, a hundred boxes, one chance

The computer scientists Anna Gál and Peter Bro Miltersen first studied the problem in 2003. It looks hopeless: each prisoner finds their number with probability 1/2, whatever they do. Yet together they can win almost a third of the time.

  1. 1

    Prisoners and boxes

    A prison holds 100 prisoners numbered 1 to 100. A room holds 100 boxes with the same numbers on their lids.

  2. 2

    Numbers inside

    The warden puts one slip with a prisoner's number in each box. The slips are shuffled at random, and each number is in exactly one box.

  3. 3

    The search

    The prisoners enter the room one at a time. Each may open 50 boxes to look for their own number. Then they leave, and the room is put back as it was.

  4. 4

    The win condition

    If every one of the 100 prisoners finds their number, all go free. If even one fails, all stay in prison. They may agree on a plan in advance, but cannot communicate once it starts.

Intuition says

"Each one succeeds with chance 1/2, so together it's (1/2)¹⁰⁰. That's zero"

For random search that's true. But the prisoners can agree on a strategy that links their results: either they all succeed together, or many fail at once. Then the overall chance is 31.2%.

The loop strategy

  • Open the box with your own number.
  • Read the number on the slip inside.
  • Open the box with that number, and repeat.

The slips are placed at random, and the warden doesn't know the plan. What happens if he does is in round 5.

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Each prisoner's own chance really is 1/2, whatever they do. But you may only multiply those chances if the results are independent. What if a strategy made the prisoners win or lose together?

  2. 2

    Try the loop strategy from the puzzle. The arrows "box → number inside" split the boxes into loops, and a prisoner finds their number if their loop is at most 50 long. So all win when there is no long loop at all.

  3. 3

    The chance that a random arrangement of 100 numbers has a loop of length k, for k above 50, is exactly 1/k. There cannot be two such loops. How do you get from there to the chance of no long loop?

Round 4 · Four explanations

Why the loop works

The strategy doesn't change any single prisoner's chance: it stays 1/2. It changes how those chances are linked to each other.

An arrangement is made of loops

An arrow from each box to the number inside

Exactly one arrow leaves each box and exactly one enters it, because each number is in exactly one box. So the arrows always close up into loops. Mathematicians call them the cycles of the permutation.

Your loop

You find your number if your loop is at most 50 long

You start at the box with your number. It sits on some loop, and you walk along it. The last box of the loop points back to yours, so it holds your number. You always find your number when the loop closes. The only question is whether 50 tries are enough.

So the prisoners win if the arrangement has no loop longer than 50. There can be at most one long loop: two loops of 51 don't fit into 100 boxes.

Results are linked

All win together, or many lose together

Random: each one independent7.9·10⁻³¹chance all 100 succeed
Loop: fates linked31,2%chance all 100 succeed

With either strategy, 50 prisoners fail on average. But with the loop strategy the failures bunch up in the same arrangements: everyone on a long loop fails together. When the prisoners lose, about 73 of them fail on average. In exchange, in 31% of arrangements nobody fails.

Counting long loops

The chance of a loop of length k is 1/k

For k from 51 to 100, the chance that the arrangement has a loop of length exactly k is 1/k. These events don't overlap, since there's only one long loop. So the chance of losing is 1/51 + 1/52 + … + 1/100 ≈ 68.8%.

For formula lovers

The exact answer and its limit

How many arrangements have a loop of length k longer than half. Count in three steps:

  • choose the k boxes of the loop: C(n, k) ways;
  • join them in a circle: (k − 1)! ways;
  • arrange the other n − k boxes any way you like: (n − k)! ways.
Permutations with a loop of lengthk>n/2 =C(n,k)·(k−1)!·(n−k)!=n!k

The chance of losing: a long loop of length k from 51 to 100 occurs with chance 1/k. Hn = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n is a harmonic number.

P(lose)=151+152+…+1100 =H100−H50≈0,6882

The chance that everyone goes free: there is no long loop.

P(all free)=1−(H100−H50)≈0,3118

What happens with very many prisoners: the difference of harmonic numbers tends to ln 2.

n→∞:Hn−Hn/2→ln2 ⇒P→1−ln2≈0,3069

For comparison: if every prisoner opens boxes at random.

P(random)=(12)100≈7.9·10−31

Here Hn = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n is a harmonic number. To build a loop of length k, choose k boxes, join them in a circle in (k − 1)! ways, and arrange the rest any way you like. This strategy is the best possible: no other plan gives better odds.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

2003

A puzzle from a research paper

Anna Gál and Peter Bro Miltersen described the problem in a paper on data structures at the ICALP 2003 conference. Their version used coloured strips of paper, not prisoners. Their Aarhus University colleague Sven Skyum pointed them to the loop strategy.

2006

It can't be beaten

Eugene Curtin and Max Warshauer proved that the loop strategy is optimal: no other plan gives the prisoners better odds. The proof appeared in The Mathematical Intelligencer in a paper called "The locker puzzle".

YouTube

The puzzle that went viral

The Veritasium channel brought the puzzle to a wide audience. The video's title calls it a riddle that seems impossible even once you know the answer.

9.33·10¹⁵⁷

That's how many arrangements exist

100 numbers can be placed in 100 boxes in 100! ways. That number has 158 digits. In about 31% of them the longest loop is at most 50.

≈ 73

They lose as a crowd

When the loop strategy fails, about 73 prisoners fail on average: everyone on the long loop. With random search about 50 fail on average, and almost never none at all.

30.69%

A million prisoners is fine

However many prisoners there are, if each opens half of the boxes, the chance that all go free never drops below 1 − ln 2 ≈ 30.69%. For 100 prisoners it's 31.18%; for 1,000 it's 30.74%.

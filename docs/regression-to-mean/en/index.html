Regression to the mean Praise them and they slip. Scold them and they improve. Does criticism work?The words did nothing.

A coach praises the best players and scolds the worst. After praise players do worse, and after criticism they do better. Does that mean criticism works and praise backfires? Lock in your answer, then check it with a game, a simulation and five explanations.

Every result is part skill and part luck. Whoever comes out on top was usually lucky, so next time they drift back towards the average. Whoever comes last usually moves up. It happens with praise, with criticism and with no feedback at all. Check it with a game, a simulation and five explanations.

The puzzle

Picture this: you coach a team

Francis Galton gave the effect its name in an 1886 paper on the heights of parents and children. First the model, then the question.

Francis Galton gave the effect its name in an 1886 paper on the heights of parents and children. The paradox lies in the conclusions we draw: we see a cause where there is only chance.

  1. 1

    The players

    Each player has a hidden skill. It does not change from round to round. Nobody can see it, not even the coach.

  2. 2

    A round

    A score in a round is skill plus luck. The luck is new every time: some have a good day, some don't.

  3. 3

    Feedback

    After round 1 the coach praises the best and scolds the worst. Or says nothing.

  4. 4

    The question

    What happens to the best and the worst in round 2? And does it prove anything about praise and criticism?

Intuition says

"The praised ones relaxed and the scolded ones pulled themselves together. So criticism works better"

In fact the best drop and the worst rise even when the coach says nothing. The best of round 1 are mostly the ones who got lucky. In round 2 the luck is fresh, and their score moves back towards their real level.

Model assumptions

  • Score = skill + luck.
  • Skill is the same in both rounds.
  • Luck is independent between rounds, zero on average.
  • Praise and criticism change nothing.

Scores have mean 50 and standard deviation 10. You can change how much of them is luck in round 3.

Round 1 · Guess

Does the instructor's shouting work?

A flight instructor praises cadets for good landings and criticises them for bad ones. He has noticed that after praise the next landing is usually worse, and after a telling-off it is usually better. He concludes that criticism works and praise backfires. What do you say?

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Think about who gets praised: a cadet who has just made an unusually good landing. Does one landing show pure skill, or is there a bit of luck in it too?

  2. 2

    Cadets are picked by an extreme result, and a result is skill plus luck. Next time the luck is fresh. What would happen to the best and the worst if the instructor said nothing at all?

  3. 3

    Imagine all cadets are equally skilled and a landing depends only on a roll of a die. Praise the one who rolled a 6 and scold the one who rolled a 1. What will they roll next time?

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why extremes drift back to the middle

The key is that we select people by their score, and a score mixes skill with luck. Here are five ways to see it. The numbers in the first two cards come from the round 3 simulation.

Take the best players of round 2 and look at how they did before. They were worse then too. Time plays no role here, and nobody got "tired of success". Selecting on one measurement shows regression in the other whenever luck plays a part.

A control group

The effect shows up with no intervention

The best, praised−7.2
The best, not praised−7.2

To know whether praise works, compare two similar groups: one is praised, the other is not. The effect of praise is the difference between the two changes. Here it is zero. Without a control group, the 7-point drop is easy to blame on the praise.

Not a "law of balance"

Luck does not pay back

Regression does not mean bad luck must follow good luck. Luck in round 2 is zero on average. The best fall back to their real level and, on average, no further. Their round 2 is still above the overall average.

And the spread does not shrink: round 2 has new leaders and new laggards, who were lucky or unlucky this time.

For formula lovers

Skill, luck and correlation

Each round's score is the mean μ plus a skill S that stays the same plus a luck E that is new every time.

X1=μ+S+E1 X2=μ+S+E2

The correlation r between rounds equals the share of skill in the spread of scores.

r=Var(S)Var(S)+Var(E) =1−luck share

The expected round 2 score of a player who scored X1 in round 1.

E[X2|X1]=μ+r·(X1−μ) μ=50,r=0.5,X1=70 50+0.5·20=60

How much of the round 1 deviation was luck.

E[E1|X1]=(1−r)·(X1−μ) 0.5·20=10points of luck

The top 10% in round 1: their average score, then their expected round 2 score.

μ+σ·φ(z)0.1=50+10·1.755=67.5 50+0.5·17.5=58.8

The expected round 2 score sits only a fraction r as far from the mean as round 1 did. The rest of the gap was luck. With no luck (r = 1) there is no regression. With pure luck (r = 0) the expected round 2 score is the overall mean, whatever round 1 was.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1886

Galton and height

Francis Galton compared the heights of adult children with those of their parents. Children of very tall parents were tall on average, but shorter than their parents, and children of short parents were taller than theirs. In an 1886 paper he called this "regression towards mediocrity".

y = a + bx

Where "regression" got its name

Linear regression, one of the main tools of statistics, takes its name from Galton's work on height. The word stuck, even though most regression models have nothing to do with drifting back to the mean.

2011

Kahneman and the flight instructors

In "Thinking, Fast and Slow" (2011) Daniel Kahneman tells how he explained to Israeli Air Force instructors that praise teaches better than punishment. One instructor objected: after praise cadets flew worse, and after shouting they flew better. Kahneman realised the man was seeing regression to the mean.

SI

The cover jinx

In the US people talk about the "Sports Illustrated cover jinx": after appearing on the magazine's cover, an athlete often does worse. Athletes make the cover after an exceptional run in which luck was also on their side. The next run is closer to their usual level.

1933

"The Triumph of Mediocrity in Business"

In a 1933 book the economist Horace Secrist found that the most successful firms became more ordinary over time and the weakest ones improved, and concluded that business tends towards mediocrity. In a review, the statistician Harold Hotelling pointed out that this was regression to the mean.

1948

Why drug trials need a control group

The trial of streptomycin for tuberculosis, published by the UK Medical Research Council in 1948, is regarded as one of the first randomised controlled trials. Comparing with a control group separates the effect of a drug from regression to the mean and the placebo effect.

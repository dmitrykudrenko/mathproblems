Paradoxes in real life · Dmytro Kudrenko · 27 November 2026

Game theory at the movies Squid Game. How do you reach the final when you don't know the next game's rules?

456 players, six games, and no way of knowing in advance what comes next. In Squid Game you pick your partner, your team and your number before the rules are announced, and then your partner may turn into your opponent. Let's look at which strategies make sense for the whole game rather than one round, work them out on simple models, and play thousands of tournaments in a simulation.

Squid Game is a South Korean Netflix series in three seasons (2021, 2024 and 2025). In each season 456 people deep in debt agree to play children's games where losing means death. Every eliminated player adds 100 million won to the pot, and the winner takes it all, 45.6 billion.

This post continues the article The Hunger Games and game theory. There we looked for the best strategy in an arena where there is one game and its rules are known. Here it's the same idea with a new twist: nobody knows the rules of the next game.

There are three big differences from the Hunger Games. First: nobody knows what the next game will be, and key decisions have to be made before the rules are announced. Second: the players can stop the game by majority vote. Third: every death visibly adds money to the pot hanging over the heads of everyone still alive. So a strategy here is not a move in one game but a rule for the whole game: whom to pair with, where to stand in line, how to vote.

Here is the plan: eight situations from the series, each in a simple model, and in section 9 a simulation of a whole tournament. In it the games come in random order, just as they do for the characters. You create your character once, then either play through a tournament game by game, deciding before each one, or compare rules over thousands of tournaments.

Careful: spoilers ahead for all three seasons of Squid Game.

Who is who
  • Gi-hun, player 456: the main character, a man deep in debt. He wins the first season and comes back in the second to stop the games.
  • Sang-woo, player 218: Gi-hun's childhood friend, once the pride of the neighbourhood, now a financier wanted by the police.
  • Il-nam, player 001: the oldest contestant of the first season.
  • Sae-byeok (067), Ji-yeong (240) and Ali (199): first-season players Gi-hun goes through the games with.
  • The Front Man: the masked overseer of the games. The VIPs: rich spectators who bet on the players.

1. Choosing blind

The cleverest thing in the series is not the games themselves but what players do before them. The organisers ask everyone to choose something, and only then announce what it was for.

GameChosen blindWhat it turned out to be
DalgonaA shape: circle, triangle, star or umbrellaThe difficulty: you must cut that shape out of a sugar candy with a needle in 10 minutes
Tug of warA team of 10The team that pulls against another over a drop
MarblesA partnerAn opponent: you play against your partner, and only one walks away
Glass bridgeA number on a vest, from 1 to 16Your place in line for a bridge where half the panels are traps
Six-legged pentathlonA team of 5Five different games: each team member plays one, legs tied together

When you don't know the task, the best choice is the one that does well in almost any task. A circle is the easiest shape for almost any game with a candy; an umbrella is the hardest. Gi-hun, the main character, took the umbrella and was saved by his wits: he started licking the back of the candy to soften it. But a team, a partner or a number can't be chosen that simply: what is good for one game is bad for another. Each of these choices gets its own section below.

Not everything is chosen blind. In the third season's hide and seek the colour of your ball is drawn at random: blues hide, reds seek with knives and must kill. But before the game you may swap with someone of the other colour if they agree. That creates a market for risk: whoever isn't ready to kill looks for someone willing to take the knife instead. A swap happens only when both sides think it pays, so before you swap, ask yourself why the other one agrees.

At work

We choose blind all the time: we hire for a product that doesn't exist yet, sign a contract a year ahead, pick a technology before all the requirements are known. The same rule applies: while the task is not visible yet, pick what won't fail whatever the task, and keep the option to change your mind. The best choice for one scenario is often the worst for another.

2. Whom to pair with when you don't know the game

Before marbles, the players are told to form pairs. Everyone assumes it's a team game and looks for someone reliable: Gi-hun takes old Il-nam, player 001, and two young women, Sae-byeok and Ji-yeong, team up. Then the rule is announced: you play against each other, and losing means death.

So everyone choosing a partner faces two opposite possibilities. If the game is a team game, you want the strongest partner. If the partner becomes your opponent, you want the weakest. What do you choose when you don't know which?

A simple model

Let each player's strength be one number: the share of players they are stronger than, from 0 to 1. In a duel, as in the Cornucopia model, the stronger one wins more often the bigger the gap: a player of strength x beats a player of strength y with probability (1 + x − y)/2. A team game works the same way, but compares the teams' average strength. The next game is a team game with probability T (you and your partner against a random pair), a duel with your partner with probability D, and otherwise a solo game, where your partner makes no difference.

Let's work out how your chance changes as your partner gets stronger. In a team game your partner is only half the team, so each unit of their strength adds a quarter of a unit to your chance. In a duel they are your entire opponent, and each unit of their strength takes away half a unit. So the answer is simple: take the strongest if team games are more than twice as likely as duels with your partner, and the weakest if less. In a team of k people the bar is even higher: team games have to be k times as likely, since each partner is only 1/k of the team. Curiously, your own strength plays no part in the answer: the threshold is the same for the strong and the weak. Your strength decides something else, who will agree to pair with you, as we'll see below.

In the first season, games where a partner helps outnumber those where a partner hurts. But the model hints at two more things that the series shows better than any formula.

Strength is not one number

Il-nam is the oldest and weakest-looking player. Gi-hun pairs with him almost out of pity. But marbles is won by wits and nerve, not muscle, and the old man plays better than he seems. Gi-hun survives only because Il-nam hands him the last marble. "Weak-looking" is not the same as "easy to beat". Someone without strength has often put themselves into something else.

The strong pick the strong

Before tug of war everyone wants strong men on their team, and there is no room for women and old people. Anyone stronger than you isn't keen to pair with you: they can pick someone stronger themselves. Every market where both sides choose works this way. Back in 1962 David Gale and Lloyd Shapley showed that such a market always has a stable matching, one where no two people would both rather leave their partners for each other. And when everyone wants the same thing, the strongest, a stable matching pairs the strong with the strong and the weak with the weak (Gale, Shapley, 1962). So "whom would I like" is a less useful question than "who will say yes, and what are they good at".

At work

A business partner can also be both teammate and rival: a reseller or agency that sells your product today may launch its own tomorrow. The model suggests one thing: a partner's strength alone doesn't make them the better choice. It helps you where you're on the same side and works against you where you're on opposite sides. So the question is not "how strong are they" but "in how many scenarios will we be on the same side".

How cooperation with a partner holds up when the game repeats, and why it breaks when the end is known, is the Axelrod tournament puzzle.

3. A team when you don't know the game: strength or variety

In tug of war Gi-hun's team looks the weakest: old Il-nam and several women. But the old man played this game when he was young and knows the technique, and Sang-woo, Gi-hun's friend, comes up with a trick: at the key moment the whole team suddenly takes three steps forward, and the opponents, pulling with all their might, lose their balance. The team wins not by strength but by knowledge and cunning.

Whether the trick would work in real life is an open question: YouTubers who tested it on ordinary teams found the stronger team still won. But the idea matters: when the game is unknown, picking a team on one quality alone is a bet on one scenario. The second season shows this even more clearly. The six-legged pentathlon is five different games: ddakji (flipping a paper tile), flying stone, gonggi (jacks), a spinning top and jegi, a Korean shuttlecock kicked with the foot. Each team member plays just one. Five strong players lose here to five different ones.

Which of these wins out when the games come at random we'll check in the simulation in section 9: there you can build your team on strength or on variety and see what happens.

At work

Variety doesn't always pay, only when the tasks really need different skills. In our tournament one game of nine is like that, and the simulation shows that overall a team of the strong does better (section 9). So before hiring for "variety", count honestly how much of the work ahead will need different skills and how much will need one main skill.

4. The line: first, in the middle or last

Before the glass bridge, the 16 remaining players take vests numbered 1 to 16, and only then is it announced that the number is their place in line. The bridge has 18 pairs of glass panels: in each pair one is tempered and holds a person, the other is ordinary and breaks. They have 16 minutes for all of it.

The first player guesses each pair at random and walks until they get one wrong. The next player then knows all the steps covered so far and guesses on from there. So player number k crosses if 18 coin tosses produce at most k − 1 failures. That is the binomial distribution, and it can be computed exactly.

The first five numbers almost certainly die: number one has a 1 in 260,000 chance. The last four almost certainly cross. Numbers 9 and 10 are close to a coin toss: 41% and 59%. On average 7 of 16 players cross. In the series only three made it. The model leaves out what happened there: players lost time, afraid to step, so the minutes ran short; the glassmaker, player 017, could tell tempered glass by eye, so the organisers turned off the lights; and Sang-woo pushed the glassmaker to make him reveal the next panel with his own body.

On the bridge, last is best. But not in every game. In "Red Light, Green Light" it's safer to go not first but in the crowd, hiding from the doll's camera behind other people's backs. Yet not at the very back either: there are 5 minutes to cross the field, and whoever doesn't make it is out. The pattern repeats from game to game: going first is dangerous because you teach the others at your own expense; going last is risky because time may run out.

At work

This is the debate about first-mover advantage. Peter Golder and Gerard Tellis went through the history of 50 markets and found that 47% of the companies that entered first later failed, and the leaders were more often the ones who came later and learned from others' mistakes (Golder, Tellis, 1993). But the glass bridge reminds us of the other side too: the last in line can run out of time. Going second pays while the market is still open.

5. The vote: does yours decide anything

By the rules the game stops if a majority of players votes for it. After the first game of the first season 201 are alive. They vote one by one, openly, and the count reaches 100 to 100. Player 001, Il-nam, votes last, and his vote stops the game. In the second season there's a vote after every game: "O" means play on, "X" means go home and split the money already in the pot among the living. After the second game the count is 139 to 116 to play on.

When does your vote decide anything? Only when everyone else splits exactly evenly. If each person votes independently, that chance is easy to compute. And it depends surprisingly heavily on how divided the room is.

When the room is split exactly in half, with 201 players your vote decides with probability 5.6%: not that small. But let the balance tilt to 45 to 55 and the chance drops to 2%, and at 40 to 60 to 0.1%. The fewer people vote, the more each vote weighs: on a board of 21 with an even split, 18%.

Open voting one by one changes the game even more. Il-nam votes last and sees the count: his vote is certain to decide. So in the series each vote before his is also a signal, and the vote turns into a campaign: in the second season the "O" and "X" camps split the dormitory in two and end up fighting.

And now what comes out only in the first season's finale: Il-nam is not just an old player but the creator of these games, who decided to play them himself. So the deciding vote at 100 to 100 was cast by the owner of the game. In the second season the organiser again plays as number 001: the Front Man signs up as a player under a false name and votes with everyone else. When the author of the rules is among the voters, the count shows more than the players' will.

At work

At the general meeting of a big company a single vote decides almost nothing; on a board of seven it often does. Hence two tips. If you want your vote to matter, look for the smaller circle where decisions are made. And if the vote is open, whoever votes last sees the count and knows for sure whether their vote will decide anything.

6. The pot: money that grows with others' deaths

A big transparent piggy bank hangs over the dormitory, and after every game money pours into it: 100 million won per eliminated player. After the first game of the first season 255 people are dead, so the pot holds 25.5 billion. If the game were stopped and the money split, as the second season's rules allow, each of the 201 survivors would get about 127 million.

That is why the piggy bank hangs in plain sight. Each survivor's share = 100 million × the dead / the living, and every new death raises the share of everyone still alive. With 100 alive, one more death adds about 4.6 million to each of the rest. So in the first season, on the night after the second game, players attack each other, and for a long time the guards don't step in: it suits the organisers.

Here is a trap for intuition. If you plan to win, killing at night gains you nothing: the winner gets 45.6 billion only once everyone else is out, whoever got rid of them and whenever. Each extra death only raises the share in a split, so it matters to someone counting on leaving mid-game. Meanwhile the killer risks their own life every time. A night fight is the move not of someone who wants to win but of someone who wants to leave with a bigger share.

The third season shows this logic most bluntly. After jump rope the VIPs enter a baby into the game as player 222, and the rest, as Netflix puts it, vote to play one more game because otherwise the child would get a share. A new player shrinks everyone's share, and the players vote for a game in which someone has to die.

A growing pot also keeps people in the game. After every game there's more in it, and each next game looks like a good bet: the share keeps growing. But it's a bet where losing costs everything, and it has to be made again and again. How to think about repeated bets where you can lose it all is the coin puzzle: intuition fails there too. And how loot is split when everyone can vote against is the pirates' puzzle.

At work

Any bonus pool split among those who remain works like this piggy bank: everyone who drops out raises the others' share. In the 2000s Microsoft graded people on a curve: in every team someone had to get a low rating. According to employees quoted in Kurt Eichenwald's article (Vanity Fair, 2012), strong people avoided strong teams, and colleagues hid information from each other. Whoever sets the prize decides what game people play.

7. The final: someone has to go

The final of the third season is the "Sky Squid Game": three tall towers with platforms shaped as a square, a triangle and a circle. In each round the players must push at least one of them off, and if nobody has fallen when the countdown ends, everyone is out. Among the finalists is a baby, player 222: the mother died in the previous game, and the child took her place.

This is a game with forced elimination: someone goes every round, the only question is who. Intuition says the weakest is pushed first. But the players think further ahead, from the end of the game back, as in backward induction: get rid of the weak one now, and next round you're alone with the strongest. Whether it pays to be the strongest when two others can team up against you, test on the truel, a duel for three.

8. Change the game itself

The strongest moves in the series, as in the Hunger Games, are not the best play within the rules but attempts to change the rules themselves.

A rebellion alongside the organiser

In the second season Gi-hun urges players to vote "X", and when the vote can't be won, he leads a rebellion against the guards. Among the rebels is player 001, the Front Man himself under a false name. At the key moment he leaves the rebels, and the rebellion fails. Changing the rules together with the one who writes them is the worst possible alliance: he knows your plan and loses if it works.

Leave the game so someone else wins

In the third season's final Gi-hun could win, but he jumps from the tower himself, and the baby becomes the winner. Game theory counts what the players value. The organisers built a game where everyone values only themselves, but Gi-hun values something else, so the game ends differently from their plan.

At work

Sometimes the organiser of a game plays in it too. A marketplace that sells its own goods next to sellers' goods sees their sales data. That is why in 2020 the European Commission sent Amazon preliminary objections, alleging it used sellers' non-public data for its own retail business (press release IP/20/2077). Before optimising your play on such a platform, take into account that the owner sees more than you and can change the rules.

9. Play it yourself: from one tournament to thousands

Each model above took one moment of the game. Now let's put a whole tournament together. 456 players, and you are one of them. Five of the series' nine games come up in random order, then the final. First you create your character once, and their qualities won't change till the end. Then there are two ways to go: play through a tournament game by game, deciding before each one, or let the simulator play thousands of tournaments and compare the rules you might follow for those decisions.

Every player has four qualities from 1 to 10:

  • Strength: you can see it at a glance. It decides tug of war, the shoving in Mingle and, for the seeker, hide and seek.
  • Dexterity: hands and feet: the candy, jumping the rope, the pentathlon games.
  • Wits: marbles, technique in tug of war, where to hide in hide and seek.
  • Nerve: freezing on "red light", steady hands over the candy, keeping cool in marbles.

What you decide before each game

As in the series, you are first told only what to choose, and the game itself comes after.

How the model counts — the formulas in plain words

The players

  • You spread 20 points across four qualities, 1 to 10 in each.
  • The other 455 get 16 to 24 points at random, 20 on average, so 5 per quality: each quality starts at 1, and the model throws the rest one at a time into random qualities. Each has their own habit for lines, chosen at random: first, in the middle or last.
  • Whoever put points into strength had fewer for the rest. In the model, as in the series, strong-looking doesn't mean sharp.

How you build a team

Before a team game you ask the 20 players near you in the order your rule sets:

  • Strong: from the strongest-looking down.
  • Varied: each time whoever best covers the quality the team lacks. That needs more than strength: after a few days in the dormitory people roughly know who can do what.
  • Weak: from the weakest-looking up.
  • Suited to you: the strong, but only those whose wits and nerve together don't beat yours: every point of their lead in those two qualities moves them down your list like two points of strength. This rule takes your own qualities into account: the partner helps in a team game, and you can still beat them at marbles.

Anyone more than 1 point stronger than you agrees only with a 10% chance: the strong pick the strong. Empty places go to whoever is left. The other players split into teams at random.

The chance of surviving a game

In each game your chance rises smoothly with the qualities that matter: every point adds a few percentage points but can never reach 100%. The numbers for a player with fives across the board:

Example. You have fives across the board, so wits and nerve together make 10. You asked the strongest-looking player to pair up: strength 8 and 4 in the rest, so wits and nerve make 8. At marbles you are 2 ahead, and your chance is 67%. You asked the weakest-looking: strength 2, dexterity 5, wits 7, nerve 6, so 13. Now they are 3 ahead, and your chance is only 26%. This is Il-nam in numbers.

The vote and the pot

After every game but the last, everyone alive votes. Each other player votes "X", to go home, with probability 47%: in the series the room split almost evenly every time, 100 to 100 and 116 against 139. If "X" gets more than half, the game ends and the living split the pot: 100 million won per dead player.

The final

Whoever survives five games goes to the tower final. Each round one player goes, and the weaker by total of qualities is pushed more often: a player with a total of 16 is pushed 2.7 times as often as one with 20, and one with 24, 2.7 times less often. Rounds go on until one is left.

How your chance is counted

When you play through a tournament yourself, the model draws lots: you survive or you don't. When it compares rules, it works differently. It plays out the other players in full: who teams with whom, who survives. Your own chance it doesn't play out but computes: it multiplies your chances of surviving each game against the rivals still in it. That gives a more precise result: an event that happens to one player in a hundred would otherwise come up only a few times in a thousand tournaments.

Before you compare, guess

For a player with fives across the board, whom is it better to ask onto your team: the strong-looking, a varied mix, the weak-looking, or "suited to you"? And where to stand in line: first, in the middle or last?

What the simulations showed — open once you have tried it yourself

Fives across the board. The best is to ask the strong-looking onto your team and stand last in line: you reach the final in 3.7% of tournaments, 1.6 times as often as an ordinary player, and win about one tournament in 650. "Suited to you" is close, 3.2%. The worst is to stand first and ask the weak: 0.25%, nine times less often than an ordinary player.

Weak-looking is not an easy opponent. You'd think a weak partner is insurance against marbles: easier to beat. In the model it's the other way round. With a partner chosen as the weakest-looking, you survive marbles 41% of the time; with the strongest-looking, 52%. Whoever didn't put points into strength put them into wits and nerve, and those decide marbles. On top of that, with the weak you almost surely lose tug of war: 13% against 66%.

"Suited to you" wins marbles but pays in tug of war. This rule gives the best marbles, 63%, because you filter out anyone sharper than you. But there are few strong people nearby who are also less sharp than you, and tug of war falls to 45%. For all fives this is a little worse than simply asking the strong.

Varied partners win the pentathlon but lose marbles. A team where someone covers each quality passes the pentathlon 75% of the time, the "strong" team only 66%. But whoever covered wits and nerve for you beats you at marbles: your chance falls to 31%. Only one game of the nine rewards variety, and two punish it.

The best rule depends on you. For a strongman (strength 9, the rest 4, 3, 4) "suited to you" works best: tug of war 82%, marbles 52%; asking just the strong would drop marbles to 35%. But for an "old man" with strength 2, wits 8 and nerve 6 the rule hardly matters: all four give 2.4–2.9%, because the strong won't take him, and he wins marbles three times out of four anyway.

The line matters most. A habit of going first ruins almost any profile, because on the bridge the first five numbers die. Last pays better than the middle, even though the middle is better in Red Light and jump rope: the bridge, when it comes up, outweighs both of them together.

The most valuable points go to dexterity and nerve. With one quality at 8 and the rest at 4, and the best rules, you reach the final in 4.0% of tournaments with dexterity, 3.5% with nerve, 2.9% with strength, 2.6% with wits. Dexterity and nerve are needed in most solo games, strength and wits mostly in team games.

Finalists are not like everyone else. All players start with 5 in each quality on average. Those who reach the final have strength 5.0, dexterity 5.6, wits 5.3, nerve 5.6. The games filter on dexterity and nerve, and strength gives almost no edge. Learn only from the finalists, and you might decide they were special from the start, when the games simply selected them.

What carries over to business. Not the numbers, and not which quality is "most valuable": those are properties of our made-up model. Two things carry over. First: the same rule gives different results to different players, so a stronger competitor's strategy shouldn't be copied without adjusting it to yourself. Second: if you study only those who reached the final, it's easy to credit their qualities for success, when the game simply selected part of them. Jerker Denrell showed that this is how popular management myths arise: we learn from successful companies and hardly see those with the same traits who failed (Denrell, 2003).

The exact numbers depend on the model's rules: change the weights and they shift. But the main point holds: when you don't know the next game, what matters most is not any one quality but the rules you apply to every game, whom to go with and when to step into line. And those rules are worth fitting to yourself.

Even with the best rules a player reaches the final in only a few percent of tournaments, and every game is a new bet where losing costs everything. How quickly such a run of bets ends is the roulette puzzle. And why you shouldn't learn strategy only from the winner, like Gi-hun, is the puzzle of the planes that came back.

What to take away

  • When you choose blind, choose what won't fail in any scenario, not what's best for one.
  • Before you take the strongest as a partner, count in how many scenarios you'll be on the same side and in how many on opposite sides.
  • Weak-looking isn't necessarily weak: whoever didn't invest in strength often invested in something else.
  • The strong pick the strong. Ask not "whom would I like" but "who will say yes, and what are they good at".
  • Don't go first where the first teach the others at their own expense. But don't wait till the end either: time is limited.
  • Your vote decides only when the others split evenly. If you want weight, find a smaller circle or speak last.
  • A pool split among those who remain turns colleagues into rivals. Whoever sets the prize decides the game.
  • Check whether the one who writes the rules is playing alongside everyone else.
  • Fit your rules to yourself: what's best for a strongman isn't best for the sharp one.

Paradoxes in real life · Dmytro Kudrenko · 28 November 2026

From my work Chasing a competitor. Can you catch up by running to where they already are?

We see where a competitor is now and run there. But they decided to run there long ago, and by the time we arrive the market has changed. And we think we know why they succeed, when the real cause is usually not the one we can see. Let's take both traps apart with exact models and see when copying makes sense and when it doesn't.

Real story

I have seen this trap from the inside. When one of our products was starting out, we were looking for our niche, while competitors were already growing fast and showing their growth charts in posts and articles. They seemed ahead because they did what we didn't. We took their moves apart and set a strategy: repeat their success. It didn't work. They had already taken that niche, and becoming first where there already is a first is much harder. And what looked to us like their unique trait, they had already dropped by then. So we look for our own opportunities, not someone else's.

This question has an exact mathematical version. In 1732 the French mathematician Pierre Bouguer solved the pursuit problem: a ship chases another and at every moment turns its bow to where the other one is now. The path is called a pursuit curve. Here are three ways to chase a leader, from the most common to the rarest.

1. Run to where you can see them

What it means in business

What we see of a competitor is their past. A shipped feature, a new pricing page, a campaign everyone talks about: all of these are decisions they made months ago. When we copy what we see, we aim not at them but at the place where they were.

The model: the leader runs straight ahead, you start off to one side, and at every moment you run towards the point where they were a little earlier. Here the maths gives a hard answer: you close in on exactly that point in the past, so you stay behind by your delay, however fast you run. Speed only gets you to that fixed distance behind sooner.

Try it in the animation

Try it in the ship animation at the top: choose "Where seen". With a half-unit delay, even 20% faster than the leader you stay 0.5 behind, and no speed changes that. To catch up you have to aim not at what you saw but at the leader themself, that is, know what the competitor is doing now, not what they have shown.

2. Run to where they are now

Suppose you see the competitor with no delay. That is Bouguer's problem, and it isn't comforting either.

  • At the same speed you never catch up. The gap shrinks to exactly half the starting one and then stays there. It takes one line to prove and a simulation to check: when both run at the same speed, the distance between you plus how far you trail along their path stays constant, and over time those two quantities become equal.
  • Even the faster one runs longer than they need to. Being 20% faster, you catch up in 2.7 units of time. Running straight to where the leader will be would take 1.5, almost half as long. The curve eats the rest: you keep chasing a position that has already moved. True, you don't have to be at their exact spot: you get close, within a tenth of the starting distance, by 2.2. But a straight course would get you there by 1.4.

Try it in the animation

And what if the leader changes course too? Switch the leader in the ship animation to "Turns". At the same speed the gap no longer freezes at half but keeps shrinking: at every turn you cut the corner. A leader who holds their course can't be caught by an equally fast pursuer; one who keeps turning lets them get ever closer.

What it means in business

So a vision you stick to protects a lead as well: a leader who holds course can't be caught even by an equally fast rival.

Try it in the animation

Now give yourself inertia: you can't turn on the spot, and your smallest turning radius is 0.8 of the starting distance. Against a leader who holds course this changes nothing: the pursuit curve bends more gently than you can turn, so you catch up just as fast. But a leader who turns more sharply than you can is no longer catchable: you can't keep up with the turns, and instead of shrinking the gap grows again.

What it means in business

Inertia is how fast a company can change course. So chasing a competitor's every turn makes sense only if you turn at least as fast as they do.

Case

This happens a lot. We see a company's big breakthrough, and half a year or a year later, when we roll out the same thing, it turns out the strategy was bad and they have already abandoned it. A public example is social audio. In February 2021 the Clubhouse app was downloaded 9.6 million times, and the big platforms rushed to build their own voice rooms: Twitter Spaces opened in May to every account with at least 600 followers, Facebook Live Audio Rooms and Spotify Greenroom launched in June. But by April Clubhouse was down to about 0.9 million downloads, about a tenth of its peak (Sensor Tower data). Facebook closed its rooms in 2022, Spotify shut its app in April 2023. They were catching up with a point the market had already left.

3. Run to where they will be

The shortest path is straight to the point where you will meet. But that requires knowing where the leader is heading and how fast. In other words, having your own model of where the market is going. And if you have such a model, a simpler question comes up: why aim at the point they chose rather than the one you think the market is heading for?

What it means in business

In business this idea is often retold with the hockey player Wayne Gretzky's words: "skate to where the puck is going, not to where it has been". But critics of the metaphor note that everyone who reads the same game skates to where the puck is going. And in business there is more than one puck. So it isn't enough to predict where the market is heading; you need your own point, not the one everyone sees.

What it means in business

Besides, the market is not a competitor running in a straight line. It turns by itself. Whoever aims at a competitor chases a point that moves twice: with the competitor and with the market. The conditions change too: what worked when the competitor did it may no longer work now. As the saying goes, you can't step into the same river twice.

Try it in the animation

You can try this way in the ship animation at the top: choose "Forecast". You see the leader with the same delay as in the first way, but instead of sailing to where they were, you carry their visible course one unit of time forward and sail there. If the leader holds course, even old data tells the truth: 20% faster, you catch up at 1.5, almost as with perfect knowledge, not at 2.7 like the one aiming at the leader themself. A real leader is exactly like that: they don't wander, they know where they are going. If the leader changes course, a forecast from old data gets you close fast, but you keep overshooting.

What it means in business

The goal doesn't have to lie on the competitor's path. If they are successful, the point you need is probably not far from them, but with your own view of the market you can get there first. The geometry is simple: at equal speed, whoever is closer to a point gets there first. So a point next to the leader but closer to you is yours, if you sail straight to it rather than after them. And the real win comes when the competitor heads the other way, while you notice what changes the market and go exactly there.

4. Why they succeed: not the cause you can see

The second trap is not where to run but what to copy. We look at a successful competitor, see their traits and decide that success comes from them. The maths shows at least three reasons why that conclusion is often wrong.

We only see the survivors

The same traits may have belonged to dozens of companies that disappeared, but nobody writes about them. A trait equally common among winners and losers explains nothing. The classic example is the planes that came back from combat.

Real story

A friend told me this story. When he was taking one of his products to the Brazilian market, there was, unlike in other markets, no similar service there. The niche looked free, and he already had success in competitive niches in other countries.

But having no competitors doesn't mean a niche is free. When you analyse a market, you often need to look not at the leaders but at its graveyard: who died on the way to success?

Very often, where there are few competitors, there is simply an extremely large graveyard.

Winners are mostly the ones who took risks

Let half the companies play it safe and half take risks, with the risky strategy even slightly worse on average but with three times the spread of results. See who then fills the top of the ranking.

Among the top 10%, risky companies make up 96%; among the top 5%, nearly all. Whoever learns from market leaders learns from exactly them, and adopts a strategy that is worse on average and fails more often. The organisational scholar Jerker Denrell showed that this is how many popular management myths arise (Denrell, 2003).

Success paints everything in fine colours

When a company succeeds, its culture is called strong and its decisions bold. When the same company falls, the same traits are called rigidity and recklessness. Phil Rosenzweig called this the halo effect and showed that it contaminates the data of many famous studies of "what makes great companies" (Rosenzweig, 2007). Add regression to the mean: the best year of the best company was partly luck, so the next one is usually worse. Among the 11 "great" companies in Jim Collins's bestseller Good to Great (2001) was the retailer Circuit City, which went bankrupt a few years later.

Case

Sometimes it isn't the cause that is false but the success itself. In 2015–2017 many media outlets "pivoted to video": Facebook showed that people were watching video longer and longer, and newsrooms cut writers to make clips. In 2016 Facebook admitted that average viewing time had been inflated by 60–80%, because only views longer than three seconds were counted. In 2019 the company agreed to pay $40 million to settle the advertisers' lawsuit, without admitting wrongdoing. A whole industry was chasing a trend that didn't exist at that size.

Real story

Sometimes you misjudge the cause even of your own success. When Stripo entered the market there were almost no competitors, and the whole topic was thought unlikely to take off. Even the leaders were not moving fast. We easily took the top positions in organic search traffic. Later competitors started fighting for it too, but we grew together with the leaders and kept the top spots. For a new product today this is much harder to repeat: competition for organic traffic was already strong, and with AI, organic traffic means something different in SaaS, and promotion has changed a lot. So a success that was possible then can't be repeated today. And we don't even know for sure what exactly let us break out back then. Comparing what companies do today with the past, when the starting positions were different, makes no sense.

5. When everyone copies everyone

If everyone decides to copy the leader, the market bunches into one point. This is Hotelling's classic model of two ice-cream sellers on a beach charging the same price: every step towards the rival pays on its own, and in the end both stand side by side in the middle splitting the same customers. What then sets your product apart, apart from price?

6. When copying does make sense

What it means in business

The pursuit model hints at the answer too. Your lag equals your delay times the leader's speed. So copying is cheap where the leader barely moves: in what has become standard for everyone and no longer changes. You can catch up there, but you can't win there either, because it's only the minimum without which nobody considers you. Where the leader is running, a copy is always behind.

Case

To be fair, research doesn't say that coming second always loses. Gerard Tellis and Peter Golder traced 50 consumer product categories from their start: pioneers failed in 47% of cases and lead today in only 11% of categories, while early leaders entered on average 13 years after the pioneers (Tellis & Golder, 1996). So those who came later often won. But not by copying: among the causes of enduring leadership the authors name a vision of the mass market first, then persistence, financial commitment, relentless innovation and leveraging their assets. You can come later; coming later to someone else's point is the problem.

7. Your own vision

My view: copying is mostly a losing strategy. You arrive where the competitor was, late, and with the wrong idea of why they succeeded there. It matters more to have your own vision of where the market is going, and to go there. The maths of pursuit sides with vision: if you know where the target will be, the shortest path leads there, not to where it is now.

Try it in the animation

In life you are rarely faster than the leader. So in the ship animation at the top set your speed to "−10%" and choose "Where success is": you don't chase the leader, you sail straight to where you believe success is. Chasing a faster leader gets you nowhere, wherever you aim: you never catch them. But you reach your own goal in 2.9 units of time, though you are slower. The flag in the animation is only an illustration: where your goal is comes from your view of the market, not from the model.

Case

A good example of your own point is Mailchimp. Back when the company was still private, its co-founder Ben Chestnut told how they started. SaaS wasn't popular yet, few believed in it, investors weren't interested, and betting on small business instead of big corporations impressed nobody. Mailchimp didn't bend to a few big clients: it saw that small businesses' problems were shared by a huge number of people, and went there. With no venture investment (TechCrunch, 2019).

In 2021 Intuit bought Mailchimp for $12B and named the mid-market as one of its goals from the start (Intuit, 2021). In November 2026 Intuit agreed to sell Mailchimp to Bending Spoons for $3.68B, a third of the price (the deal, 19 Nov 2026). Why it went this way is hard to say from outside, and guessing would be the false-cause trap again. But the course of events is a reminder: you don't have to follow the lighthouse. Often it's better to follow your own dream and vision.

Real story

I have also seen this from the other side. At Stripo we built modules, but our vision was wider than saved chunks of an email, like snippets. We planned to make them smart modules, where data, layout and design are kept apart, and we released this step by step. The first version was a success, and similar solutions later appeared on the market. But from outside you see only the first version; the architecture underneath can't be seen, and we had built a flexible foundation from the start. Anyone repeating only the visible version has to change course later, and that costs more than going straight to where you need to be.

I think the same often happens with investment in AI. Some invested a lot, others nothing. The first made many mistakes, and once the right path becomes clear, those who waited may have better chances than those who already did the work: the first now have legacy to maintain. But waiting is not the same as copying: whoever waited wins only by then going their own way.

What to take away

  • What you see of a competitor is their past. Copying what you see keeps you behind by your delay, however fast you run. Section 1 ↑
  • Even with no delay, a chase at the same speed never ends, and for someone slightly faster it takes almost twice as long as the straight path. Section 2 ↑
  • Aiming where the market will be needs your own model of the market, and then it's simpler to head for your own point. Section 3 ↑
  • A successful competitor's traits aren't necessarily the cause of success: look also at those who had the same traits and failed. Section 4 ↑
  • Leaders are mostly the ones who took risks. Copying a leader, you often copy the risk, not the skill. Section 4 ↑
  • When everyone copies everyone, the market bunches into one point, and price is all that's left to compete on. Section 5 ↑
  • Copy only what no longer changes: it's the minimum, not an advantage. Section 6 ↑

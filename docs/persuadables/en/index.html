Who gets the coupon? 250 coupons for the likeliest buyers. How many sales do they add?Fewer than none.

An online shop has 250 discount coupons and a model that knows perfectly which visitors are most likely to buy. The coupons go to the 250 best candidates. How many extra purchases does that bring compared with a day without coupons? Lock in your answer, then play against two bots, watch the simulation and read the explanations.

The likeliest buyers would buy without a discount, and some of them are put off by a coupon. So 250 coupons for the "best" visitors give −22.5 purchases, while the same coupons for the people they actually move give +61. The game, the simulation and the explanations below show how to tell the difference.

The puzzle

Picture this: a shop and 250 coupons

A discount coupon appears in a pop-up on the site. It nudges some people to buy, leaves others cold, and some, faced with yet another "great offer" window, simply close the tab.

  1. 1

    The visitors

    A thousand people visit the site in a day, of four kinds. Nobody's kind is written on their forehead, but this is how they behave:

    Sure things100 peopleno coupon 90% · coupon 90%
    Persuadables150 peopleno coupon 10% · coupon 50%
    Lost causes600 peopleno coupon 2% · coupon 3%
    Sleeping dogs150 peopleno coupon 40% · coupon 25%

    The percentages are the chance of buying. Sleeping dogs are people who find one more coupon pop-up annoying: they leave, or remember they have long meant to unsubscribe.

  2. 2

    The model

    The analysts trained a "who will buy" model. It is perfect: for each visitor it knows exactly their chance of buying without a coupon. It doesn't see a person's kind, only that chance.

  3. 3

    The coupons

    There are 250 coupons. They go to the 250 visitors with the highest chance of buying according to the model.

  4. 4

    The question

    How many extra purchases do these 250 coupons bring compared with a day with no coupons at all?

Intuition says

"The model is perfect and the coupons go to the best customers. Sales are bound to go up"

In fact the 250 likeliest buyers are the 100 sure things (90%) and the 150 sleeping dogs (40%). The coupon does nothing for the first and cuts the second from 40% to 25%. In all, 150 × (−0.15) = −22.5 purchases.

Assumptions

  • The four kinds and their percentages are a model, not data from a real shop. They were chosen to show the mechanism, and are typical of marketing, where a coupon moves only a few people.
  • We assume the "who will buy" model is perfect. That is exactly why it fails: it gives a precise answer to the wrong question.
  • Visitors don't influence each other, and each person gets at most one coupon.

Round 4 · Explanations

Who will buy, and who will change their mind

The "who will buy" model answers the question "what will this person do?". A coupon asks a different one: "what changes if I give them a coupon?". The answers differ, and are often opposite.

The core · not the whole story

A coupon counts the difference, not the chance

Of the model's first 100 coupons, went to people who would buy anyway90
Of the uplift ranking's first 100 coupons10

Someone who buys 90% of the time will buy without a discount. The coupon adds nothing; it only cuts revenue by the discount. The "who will buy" model puts such people first, because they really are the likeliest buyers. But a coupon is needed not by those who will buy, but by those who will buy because of the coupon. This card only explains why coupons are wasted; why purchases even go down is the next card.

Why it goes negative

Let sleeping dogs lie

After the 100 sure things the model takes the next highest chance: the sleeping dogs with their 40%. The persuadables at 10% and the lost causes at 2% look hopeless to the model, so their turn never comes.

For the sleeping dogs the coupon does harm: the chance drops from 40% to 25%. Of 150 such people, 60 would buy without a coupon and 37.5 with one. That is where −22.5 comes from. The saying "let sleeping dogs lie" means "leave well alone".

The model made no mistake

A precise answer to someone else's question

The model in the puzzle is perfect: every "who will buy" forecast it makes is right. A better model would not fix anything. The mistake lies in the question: picking people by their chance of buying when what matters is how much that chance changes.

The difference between "with a coupon" and "without" is called the uplift. For persuadables it is +40 points, for lost causes +1, for sure things 0, for sleeping dogs −15.

Where to get the uplift

Each person lives in only one world

In the game you saw both worlds; in real life you never do: a person either got the coupon or didn't. So the uplift of one person can't be measured. For a group it can: some similar visitors get a coupon, some at random don't, and the difference between the groups shows what the coupon did.

The control group

How to measure an uplift you can't see

The uplift is defined as the chance of buying with a coupon minus the chance without one. For any single person only one of the two parts is available. The way out is a randomised experiment: among visitors with the same features, half get a coupon at random and half don't. At random means the groups are on average the same in everything except the coupon, so the difference in their purchase rates is the uplift.

It is the same A/B test, only the result is split by people's features: whom the coupon helped, whom it didn't, whom it hurt. How much noise such a comparison carries is on the "Two identical pages" page. A "who will buy" model needs no control group, which is why it is built far more often.

The uplift curve

The Qini curve: how models are compared

uplift ranking "who will buy" model at random

Along the axis is the share of people who got a coupon; upwards is the total uplift in purchases. The "at random" line joins zero with the result of coupons for everyone. The higher a curve rises above that line, the better the model finds the people a coupon moves. The area between the curve and the line is called the Qini.

Theory

The fundamental problem of causal inference

In 1986 the statistician Paul Holland named this obstacle in "Statistics and Causal Inference" (Journal of the American Statistical Association). To know what a coupon did to a particular person, you would need to see their outcome both with the coupon and without it, which is impossible: a person lives only one of the two. Holland called this the fundamental problem of causal inference.

He also set out the statistical solution: instead of the effect for one person, estimate the average effect for a group, by comparing groups formed at random. Uplift models do exactly that, only separately for each combination of features.

History

From "differential response" to the Qini

In 1999 Nicholas Radcliffe and Patrick Surry, at a credit scoring conference in Edinburgh, described "differential response analysis": modelling not the response to a mailing but the difference between the response of those who got it and of a control group. They later called it uplift modelling.

In 2002 Victor Lo, in SIGKDD Explorations, proposed the "true lift model": a single regression on the data of both groups, in which the "got the mailing" flag enters both on its own and multiplied by the other features. In 2007 Radcliffe described the incremental gains curve and the Qini measure for comparing such models.

Round 5 · Scaling up

Money, customer retention and elections

What happens when you count money instead of purchases; why the same logic stands behind a control group; and where sleeping dogs bite in real companies.

A discount is not free

Coupons to Coupons Extra purchases Profit

The same shop with 1,000 visitors. Each purchase brings $100 of profit; the coupon gives $20 off to everyone who uses it. Profit is measured against a day without coupons.

The maths

Uplift as a conditional average effect

In statistics the uplift is called the conditional average treatment effect (CATE): the average difference between "with a coupon" and "without" among people with the same features. The simplest way to estimate it is with two models: one learns the chance of buying on people who got a coupon, the other on the control group, and the forecasts are subtracted. That is how the uplift bot counted in the game.

When the discount costs money, the criterion changes again: a coupon pays if $100 × uplift is more than $20 × the chance of buying with a coupon. A coupon is worth +$30 per persuadable and −$18 per sure thing: they buy, but at a discount. So coupons for all 1,000 visitors add 43.5 purchases and at the same time lose $60. Level 3 of the game is built on this.

The logic

The second world can only be borrowed

The question "did the coupon help?" really compares two worlds: one where the person got the coupon and one where they didn't. Nobody sees the second world; it can only be rebuilt on average from similar people who, at random, got no coupon. A control group is that borrowed second world.

Without it, it is easy to credit the coupon with purchases that would have happened anyway: "250 people got a coupon, 127 of them bought, so the coupon works". In fact without the coupon 150 of those 250 would have bought. The same trap lies in judging ads, medicines or training by those who got them without looking at those who didn't. How to set up a fair comparison is on the A/B test page.

Practice

Retention that drives people away, canvassing that backfires

Discount pop-ups are the everyday version of the puzzle: it is easiest to show them to people who already have something in the cart, and those are often the people who would buy without a discount.

The best-known sleeping dogs are in customer retention. A mobile operator calls the customers at highest risk of leaving, and the call reminds them that their contract has ended and they are free to go. Radcliffe and Surry (2011) describe a retention campaign after which churn rose from 9% to 10%. An uplift model found the 30% of customers on whom the call really worked; a campaign aimed only at them cut churn to under 8% and cost 70% less.

The same goes for election campaigns. Barack Obama's 2012 campaign, according to the journalist Sasha Issenberg (MIT Technology Review), gave voters a "persuasion score" from 0 to 10: how likely a conversation with a volunteer was to pull the person towards the candidate. The organisers warned outright that a conversation could also hurt the candidate.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

30% → 0

The US Bank mailshot

US Bank mailed its customers paper letters offering a high-value product. Mailing everyone was profitable. Mailing the 30% chosen by a response model gave almost exactly zero extra sales: the model found people who would have bought anyway. An uplift model picked a different 30%, and they produced 90% of the extra sales of mailing everyone.

1986

A problem in capitals

In his paper Paul Holland writes "Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference" in capitals and sets it out as a statement of its own: you cannot observe both outcomes on the same unit, and therefore you cannot observe the effect itself.

1999

Born in credit scoring

Radcliffe and Surry's first paper on modelling the uplift was presented not at a marketing conference but at "Credit Scoring and Credit Control VI" at the University of Edinburgh. Back then the method was called "differential response analysis".

2002

Four names for one idea

Victor Lo called his approach the "true lift model". Since then the same idea has gone by uplift, net lift, incremental and differential response modelling, and in politics persuasion modelling.

Qini

A relative of the Gini coefficient

Radcliffe described the Qini measure in 2007 as a generalisation of the Gini coefficient: the Gini compares a response model with random choice through the area under a curve, and the Qini does the same for the uplift curve.

64 000

Hillstrom's challenge

In March 2008 the analyst Kevin Hillstrom published data from an email test on 64,000 customers: a random third got an email about men's merchandise, a third about women's, and a third got nothing. The data are still the standard teaching example for uplift modelling.

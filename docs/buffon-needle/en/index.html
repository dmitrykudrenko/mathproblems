Buffon's needle Drop a match on the floor. How often does it cross a seam?Get π.

A floor of equal boards, and a match as long as one board is wide. Drop it at random and count how often it crosses a seam. There are no circles anywhere, yet the share of crossings is 2/π. So thousands of drops give you the number π. Check it with a game, a million drops and five explanations.

The puzzle

Picture this: a box of matches and a wooden floor

The French naturalist Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, posed the problem in 1733 and published the solution in 1777. It is one of the first problems in geometric probability. There is no paradox here. There is a surprise: a random experiment with no circle in sight produces the number π.

  1. 1

    The floor

    The floor is made of identical boards of width d. The seams between them form parallel lines spaced d apart.

  2. 2

    The match

    The match, or needle, has a length l no greater than d. We start with the simplest case: l = d, a match exactly as long as a board is wide.

  3. 3

    The drop

    The match lands at random. Its centre can end up anywhere and it can point in any direction. All positions and angles are equally likely.

  4. 4

    The question

    What share of drops will cross a seam between boards? And how do you get π from that share?

Intuition says

"About half. And where would π come from, with no circles around?"

In fact the share of drops that cross a seam is exactly 2/π ≈ 63.7%. The circle hides in the angle: the direction of the match is a point on a circle, and that is where π comes from.

Model assumptions

  • The floor is endless, so walls don't get in the way.
  • The match is a line segment with no thickness.
  • Centre position and angle are independent and uniform.
  • A match that only touches a seam has probability zero.

Short matches (l < d) come up in round 3, long ones (l > d) in round 5.

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Only two things matter: how far the centre of the match is from the nearest seam, and its angle. Compare the extremes: along the boards it never crosses, straight across it always does.

  2. 2

    Fix the angle θ between the match and the seams. With l = d it crosses when its centre is within (d/2)·sin θ of a seam, which has chance sin θ. Now average that over all angles.

  3. 3

    The average of a function over an interval is the area under its graph divided by the interval's length. The angle is uniform from 0 to π/2. What is the area under the sine curve there?

Round 4 · Six explanations

Where π comes from in dropped matches

Every drop can be described by two random numbers. The chance of a crossing then becomes the area of a shape under a sine curve, and that area is where π enters.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

Across and along

It all comes down to the angle of the match

Lay a board-length match across the boards and it always crosses a seam. Lay it along them and it never does. At an angle it spans only part of its length across the boards, and the chance of a crossing equals exactly that part. The angle is random, so the chance has to be averaged over all directions, and a direction can be pictured as a point on a circle. That is where π comes from: on average a match spans 2/π ≈ 64% of its length across the boards, and that is the chance of a crossing. The cards below show how to compute it exactly.

Buffon's noodle

A ring always gives two crossings

Bend a match or a wire any way you like. The average number of crossings depends only on its length: each tiny piece of wire acts like a tiny match, and averages add up. So the average equals c·L for some constant c. A ring of diameter d has length πd and always crosses the seams at exactly two points. Hence c·πd = 2, so c = 2/(πd). For a straight match of length d that gives 2/π.

Two random numbers

Distance to the seam and the angle

x: the distance from the centre of the match to the nearest seam, from 0 to d/2. θ: the angle between the match and the seams, from 0° to 90°. Both are uniform and independent. Half the match reaches sideways by (l/2)·sin θ. The match crosses a seam when that reach gets to it: x ≤ (l/2)·sin θ.

The area under a sine curve

Every drop is a point in a rectangle

The integral, the noodle and the error

The chance of a crossing is the area under the curve divided by the area of the rectangle.

For a match as long as a board is wide (l = d) the chance is 2/π. That gives an estimate of π from N drops and H crossings.

l=d:P=2π≈0.6366 π≈2NH

The average number of crossings for any curve of length L. A ring has length πd.

The typical error of the π estimate after N drops.

The integral of sine from 0 to π/2 equals 1, so the area under the curve is just l/2. The area of the whole rectangle contains π/2, because the angle runs from 0 to π/2. That denominator is where π appears.

Why it is so slow

Each new digit costs 100 times more drops

DropsTypical errorReliable decimals

The share of crossings wobbles around 2/π, and its typical deviation shrinks like 1/√N. To get one more correct digit you need a 10 times smaller error, which takes 100 times more drops. That's why nobody computes π with matches: it's a way to see π, not to calculate it.

Round 5 · Variations

Long needles, Laplace's grid and points in a square

What if the match is longer than a board? What if the floor is square tiles? And is it faster to find π by throwing points into a square? Every formula here is checked by simulation.

A long needle: l greater than d

At least one crossing
Crossings on average

The chance of at least one crossing for a short needle.

l≤d:P=2lπd

The same for a long needle.

l>d:P=1+2π·(ld−(ld)2−1−arcsin(dl))

The average number of crossings for a needle of any length.

anyl:E=2lπd

A long needle can cross several seams, so the chance of "at least one crossing" grows more slowly than the average and heads towards 100%. The average number of crossings stays 2l/(πd). So you can estimate π with a needle of any length, as long as you count every crossing.

The most accurate is the square grid that Pierre-Simon Laplace studied: almost every drop crosses a line, and π is set by the small share of misses. The classic Monte Carlo method with points in a square converges faster than the needle on lines, but more slowly than the grid.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1733

A naturalist's puzzle

Buffon is better known as a naturalist, the author of the many-volume Histoire naturelle. He posed the needle problem in 1733 and published the solution only in 1777, 44 years later.

3408

Too good to be true

In 1901 the Italian Mario Lazzarini reported 3,408 drops of a needle (l/d = 5/6) with 1,808 crossings. That gives π ≈ 355/113 = 3.1415929, six correct decimal places. Such accuracy from 3,408 drops is widely seen as rigged: the number of drops looks chosen to stop exactly at 355/113.

355/113

Zu Chongzhi's fraction

The fraction 355/113 was found by the Chinese mathematician Zu Chongzhi back in the 5th century. It gives π to six correct decimal places. To reach that accuracy honestly with a needle you would need tens of trillions of drops.

1860

Barbier's theorem

In 1860 Joseph-Émile Barbier developed the "noodle" idea and proved that every curve of constant width w has perimeter πw. The argument is the one from round 4: such a curve dropped on parallel lines always gives two crossings, just like a circle.

1940s

The Monte Carlo method

Buffon's needle is often called one of the first examples of the Monte Carlo method: computing with random experiments. The name itself appeared in the 1940s at Los Alamos, where Stanislaw Ulam, John von Neumann and Nicholas Metropolis developed the method.

3/π

Laplace's grid

Pierre-Simon Laplace moved the problem to a grid of rectangles. For square tiles of side d and a needle of length d, the chance of crossing at least one line is 3/π ≈ 95.5%. You can check it in round 5.

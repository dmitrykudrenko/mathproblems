The puzzle

Picture this: you and your friends

The friendship paradox was described by the sociologist Scott Feld in 1991. It isn't a contradiction. It's called a paradox because the conclusion sounds like an insult, yet it follows from simple arithmetic.

  1. 1

    The network

    There is a group of people: a class, a company, a social network. Some pairs of people are friends. Friendship is mutual: if Anna is friends with Boris, Boris is friends with Anna.

  2. 2

    Your friends

    You count how many friends you have. Say 4. Mathematicians call this number the degree of a node.

  3. 3

    Your friends' friends

    You ask each of your 4 friends how many friends they have and take the average. For example, 3, 5, 9 and 15 average 8.

  4. 4

    The question

    For how many people in the network do their friends have more friends, on average, than they do? And does a random friend have more friends than a random person?

Intuition says

"Friends are ordinary people too. On average it should come out even"

In fact, in any network where some people have many friends and others few, a random friend is always more popular than a random person on average. They are equal only when everyone has the same number of friends.

Model assumptions

  • Friendship is mutual, and each pair is counted once.
  • Nobody is their own friend.
  • People with no friends are left out: there is nobody to compare them with.

For follows, as on Twitter/X, where links go one way, a similar effect applies. More in round 5.

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why friends are more popular

The key is how we pick the people we compare with. When we pick a friend, popular people come up more often, because they are friends with many. Here are five ways to see it.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

The smallest example

A star: one person and five friends

The hub: 5 friends1friend for each of the hub's friends
Each of the 5: 1 friend5friends for their only friend

Five of the six people have fewer friends than their friend. The network's average is 10/6 ≈ 1.67 friends. But pick one of the 10 entries in everyone's friend lists at random, and that friend has 30/10 = 3 friends on average.

A biased sample

A person with 50 friends is counted 50 times

A random person6friends on average
A random person's friend16friends on average

When everyone lists their friends, a person with 50 friends appears on 50 lists, and a person with one friend on just one. So in the list of "friends", popular people show up as many times as they have friends. The numbers come from the 3,000-person Barabási–Albert network in round 1.

When the paradox disappears

It needs inequality

If everyone has exactly k friends, every friend has exactly k friends too, and there is nothing to compare. As soon as some people have more friends than others, the average over friends shifts up. The bigger the spread, the stronger the effect: no difference in the fair network, about one extra friend in the random one, and 2.5 to 3 times as many in a network with "stars".

Almost every real network is unequal: friends, followers, co-authors, flights between cities.

For formula lovers

The average over friends and the average over people

The ordinary average over people: how many friends a person has on average. M is the number of friendships.

E[d]=d1+d2+…+dNN=2MN

The average for a random friend: each person is weighted by their number of friends. The gap from the ordinary average equals the variance divided by the mean.

random friend=∑di2∑di=E[d2]E[d] =E[d]+Var(d)E[d]≥E[d]

Example: a star, one person and five friends.

E[d]=106=53,E[d2]=306=5 Var(d)=5−259=209 →53+20953=53+43=3

Another way to count: for each person, compare their friends' average with their own number of friends. The differences add up to at least zero.

∑i(friends' meani−di) =∑friendshipsij(di−dj)2di·dj≥0

The first line is the ordinary average. In the second, each person is weighted by their number of friends, so d becomes d². The gap between them equals the variance divided by the mean. It is zero only when the variance is zero, which means everyone has the same number of friends. The last line shows the same for the other way of counting: take each person's friends' average, then average over people, and the result is still at least E[d].

Round 5 · Scaling up

Classes, buses and early epidemic warnings

The same biased sample shows up wherever we look "from the inside": a student sees their class, a passenger their bus, and a friend belongs to many circles. It can also be put to use: friends of randomly chosen people get sick earlier on average.

The class-size paradox

Average class (as reported)
Average class a student sees

Why it's the same paradox

The university averages over classes: each class counts once. Students average over themselves: a class of 210 is surveyed 210 times, a class of 10 only 10 times. It's the same weight d as with friends, and the same formula E[s²]/E[s].

Bus: 9 trips with 5 passengers and 1 rush-hour trip with 95. The average per trip divides all passengers by the number of trips.

average per trip=14010=14

As a passenger sees it: each trip counts as many times as it has passengers.

average a passenger sees =9·52+952140≈66

That's why buses, restaurants and gyms feel crowded more often than they are: you tend to be there exactly when many people are, because all those people are there too.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1991

Named in a paper

The sociologist Scott Feld described the effect in "Why Your Friends Have More Friends Than You Do", published in the American Journal of Sociology in 1991. He showed the cause is the maths of averages, not envy or low self-esteem.

> 90%

Facebook confirmed it

In 2011 Johan Ugander, Brian Karrer, Lars Backstrom and Cameron Marlow analysed the Facebook friendship network. More than 90% of users had fewer friends than their friends had on average.

2009

Flu at Harvard

During the 2009 flu epidemic, Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler followed Harvard students. Friends of randomly chosen students got the flu earlier, on average, than the chosen students themselves. That makes friends an early warning signal for epidemics.

30 → 150

Classes bigger than reported

Nine seminars of 10 students and one lecture of 210 give an average class of 30. But 70% of students sit in the lecture, and the average class a student sees is 150. Both numbers are correct; they just average different things.

14 → 66

The bus is always full

If 9 trips carry 5 people each and one carries 95, the average bus has 14 passengers. But the average passenger rides with about 66 people. It's the same reason a café seems busy exactly when you go there.

Twitter

Followers too

Studies of Twitter found that most users have fewer followers than the accounts they follow. This "generalised friendship paradox" works for other traits too, such as the citations of your co-authors.

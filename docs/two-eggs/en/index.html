Two eggs A 100-floor building and two eggs. How many drops do you need?Just 14.

There is a 100-floor building and two identical eggs. Somewhere in it is a critical floor: an egg dropped from it or higher breaks, and from below it stays whole. An unbroken egg can be dropped again. You are looking for the highest floor from which an egg does not break, and you must find it for sure. How many drops do you need in the worst case if you play it best?

It looks as if you should halve the building (50 drops) or try every tenth floor (19 drops). But the first drop should be from floor 14, and each next jump one floor shorter: 14, 13, 12… Then any outcome is found in 14 drops, because 14 + 13 + … + 1 = 105 ≥ 100.

The puzzle

A hundred floors, two eggs, a guarantee

A classic interview puzzle for programmers: William Poundstone presents it as a Google interview question (2012), and in "Cracking the Coding Interview" it is problem 6.8. It tests not formulas but the habit of thinking about the worst case and counting the price of every failure.

  1. 1

    The building

    100 floors, numbered 1 to 100. Somewhere there is a threshold: below it an egg survives the fall, from it and above the egg breaks.

  2. 2

    The eggs

    Two identical eggs. An unbroken egg can be dropped again, and the fall does not weaken it. A broken egg is gone.

  3. 3

    The answer

    You must name the highest safe floor. It may be 0, if an egg breaks even from floor 1, or 100, if it survives even floor 100. That is 101 possibilities.

  4. 4

    The cost

    Every drop counts. The strategy may depend on what happened before, but it must work whatever the critical floor is.

  5. 5

    The question

    What is the smallest number of drops that guarantees you find the highest safe floor?

Intuition says

"Binary search: drop from 50, then from 25 or 75…"

Binary search is great when eggs are unlimited. With two eggs, the first one broken at floor 50 leaves one egg for 49 floors, and they have to be checked one at a time: 50 drops. Dropping from every tenth floor gives 19. The best strategy gives 14, and nothing does better.

Assumptions

  • The eggs are identical, and an egg that survives a fall is as strong as a new one.
  • If an egg breaks from floor x, it breaks from any higher floor; if it survives, it survives any lower floor.
  • The critical floor can be anywhere, and we know nothing about its probabilities.
  • We count the worst case: how many drops it takes to know the answer whatever the critical floor is.

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Binary search is the trap: once the first egg breaks, the second has to climb one floor at a time. Think about the worst case, not the typical one.

  2. 2

    If the first egg breaks at floor x, you may need x − 1 more drops. So make each next jump of the first egg one floor shorter than the last.

  3. 3

    Say the first drop is from floor d. If the egg survives, the next jump is d − 1 floors, then d − 2, and so on. How many floors can that cover, and which d does 100 need?

Round 2 · Game

Drop the eggs while an evil building tries to slow you down

The critical floor is not fixed in advance here. The building decides whether the egg broke the moment you drop it, and always picks the answer that leaves you more work even if you play perfectly from then on. A ready-made strategy it knows in advance and plays against it. It does not cheat: every answer agrees with all the earlier ones. This is how we test exactly the worst case of your strategy.

Round 3 · Check

All 101 cases and 100,000 random buildings

The evil building shows the worst case. What if the critical floor is simply random? The chart shows how many drops each strategy needs for each of the 101 possible outcomes. The table compares the exact average with a simulation.

"Halving": the first egg goes to the middle of the untested stretch (50, 75, 88, 94…) until it breaks, then the second climbs one floor at a time. "Every 10th": 10, 20, 30… and then one at a time. After an egg breaks, the second always climbs from the last safe floor.

Round 4 · Five explanations

Why exactly 14, and why 13 is not enough

The key is not how fast you narrow the search but what each failure costs. While you have two eggs, a broken egg is no disaster. Once you have one left, every floor has to be checked on its own.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

Worst case vs average

On average the gap is small, in the worst case it is big

Every 10th floor19worst, 10.89 on average
14, 13, 12…14worst, 10.37 on average

If the critical floor is random, both strategies spend about 10–11 drops on average. The difference shows up in the worst case: "every 10th" pays 10 drops with the first egg and 9 more with the second if the threshold is in the 90s. The best strategy evens out these costs so that no case is too expensive.

The price of the first drop

A drop from floor x costs up to x drops

If the first egg breaks at floor x, the second must walk floors 1, 2, …, x − 1 one at a time: break it any other way and you never learn the answer. So a first drop from floor x can cost x drops. To fit into d drops, the first one must be no higher than floor d.

Shrinking stairs

Each next jump is one floor shorter

After the first drop survives, one drop is spent, so the second jump has d − 1 drops left and can be at most d − 1 floors long. The third is at most d − 2, and so on. With d = 14 the first egg falls from floors 14, 27, 39, 50, 60, 69, 77, 84, 90, 95, 99, 100.

Rigoroustables, charts, formulas

The lower bound

13 drops cover only 91 floors

The same count also proves that you cannot do better. With d drops the first jump is at most d floors, the second d − 1, …, the last 1. In total no more than 1 + 2 + … + d = d(d + 1)/2 floors. For d = 13 that is 91: not enough for 100 floors under any strategy. For d = 14 it is 105, with 5 floors to spare.

For formula lovers

Triangular numbers and the smallest d

Two eggs and d drops cover at most the sum of the first d whole numbers. It must be at least the number of floors N.

1+2+…+d=d(d+1)2≥N

For N = 100: 13 is too few, 14 is enough.

13·142=91<100≤105=14·152 d=⌈8N+1−12⌉=⌈13.65…⌉=14

The first egg's floors: jumps of 14, 13, 12, …, with the last one cut at 100.

14,27,39,50,60,69,77,84,90,95,99,100

In general, for e eggs: the smallest d at which the sum of binomial coefficients reaches N.

∑i=1e(di)≥N

The 5 spare floors mean there is more than one optimal strategy: the first drop can be from any floor from 9 to 14 and still fit into 14. Below 9 it fails, because more than 91 floors would remain above, and two eggs have only 13 drops for them.

Sub-theory · dynamic programming

How many floors e eggs and d drops can cover

f(e, d) = f(e − 1, d − 1) + f(e, d − 1) + 1
f(e, d) = C(d, 1) + C(d, 2) + … + C(d, e)

The first drop splits the building into three parts: if the egg breaks, f(e − 1, d − 1) floors remain below for e − 1 eggs; if it survives, f(e, d − 1) remain above; plus the floor of the drop itself. That gives the recursion, and its solution is a sum of binomial coefficients: a row of Pascal's triangle cut after the e-th term. For one egg f = d, for two f = d(d + 1)/2, the triangular numbers. Marked cells: the first d that covers 100 floors.

Round 5 · Scaling up

More eggs, more floors, expensive failures

The eggs are only a prop. The puzzle is really about a search in which every failure has a price. In mathematics it grows into binomial sums, in logic it becomes a trade of failures for time, and in practice it tells you how to load-test a service or break specimens in a lab.

From triangular numbers to binary search

A third egg saves more than you would think

For 100 floors: one egg gives 100 drops, two give 14, three give 9, four give 8. With five it is already 7 drops, which is ⌈log₂ 101⌉, the limit of binary search: 7 yes-or-no answers tell apart at most 2⁷ = 128 cases, and there are 101. Beyond that, eggs no longer help.

Four eggs miss by a hair: f(4, 7) = 7 + 21 + 35 + 35 = 98 floors, two short. For large N, k eggs need about (k! · N)1/k drops: for two eggs and a million floors that is √2,000,000 ≈ 1,414, and the exact answer is 1,414 too.

Logic

Failure budget versus time

Floors

Eggs as a failure budget

Every allowed failure buys a longer jump

The number of eggs is how many times you are allowed to fail, that is, to drop above the threshold. With no failure to spare you can only walk floor by floor. One spare failure lets you jump and cuts the time to about the square root of N. Each further failure takes another root, and once you can fail as often as binary search needs, the time falls to log₂ N.

It is a general trade: the cheaper a failure, the bolder the jumps you can take. Binary search is optimal only when failing costs nothing.

From eggs to servers and labs

45 tests with two crashes, 10 with no limit

We look for the highest of 1,000 load levels the service survives. If it may crash only twice, it takes 45 tests, since 44 · 45/2 = 990 < 1,000 ≤ 1,035 = 45 · 46/2. The first test is at level 45, then jumps of 44, 43, … If it may crash as often as you like (a test environment restarts itself), binary search needs only 10 tests.

git bisect runs the same binary search: among 1,000 commits it finds the one that broke a program in about 10 steps, because a "bad" commit destroys nothing. In materials and crash testing, though, specimens are few and each costs money: a specimen that fails is a broken egg, so the tests are planned as stairs, as in this puzzle.

A related idea with binary codes is worked through in the poisoned wine and the rats.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1996

A puzzle in a puzzle book

Wikipedia's egg-dropping section cites Konhauser, Velleman and Wagon's collection "Which Way Did the Bicycle Go?", published in 1996 by the Mathematical Association of America. The puzzle is on p. 53.

2003

Eggs in a dynamic programming class

Moshe Sniedovich devoted a paper to the puzzle, "The Joy of Egg-Dropping in Braunschweig and Hong Kong", in INFORMS Transactions on Education (vol. 4). It is number 4 in his "OR/MS Games" series on teaching games.

36

Floors on Wikipedia

Wikipedia's article on dynamic programming uses a 36-floor building. That is the triangular number 8 · 9/2, so two eggs need exactly 8 drops, with not a single floor to spare.

887

"Super Egg Drop"

On LeetCode, where programmers train for interviews, the general version with k eggs and n floors is problem 887, marked Hard.

1,414

Drops for a million floors

With two eggs, 1,000,000 floors take 1,414 drops, since 1,414 · 1,415/2 = 1,000,405. With 20 eggs, 20 drops are enough, as in binary search.

2013

Google against brainteasers

In June 2013 Laszlo Bock, Google's head of People Operations, told The New York Times that "brainteasers are a complete waste of time… They serve primarily to make the interviewer feel smart." His examples, though, were estimation questions like how many golf balls fit in a plane.

The puzzle

A stop, buses with no timetable, and you

In probability theory this puzzle is called the waiting time paradox or the inspection paradox. The setup is very simple.

In probability theory this puzzle is called the waiting time paradox or the inspection paradox: when you "inspect" the line at a random moment, you see not a typical gap but a longer one.

  1. 1

    The bus line

    Buses on one line reach your stop every 10 minutes on average. Over five hours, from 7:00 to 12:00, 30 of them come by on average.

  2. 2

    No timetable

    There is no timetable. Each bus arrives independently of the others, so the gaps vary a lot: sometimes two buses come 2 minutes apart, sometimes nothing shows up for 25 minutes. This kind of stream is called a Poisson process. A Geiger counter clicks with the same kind of randomness.

  3. 3

    You arrive

    You don't check any app. You walk up to the stop at an arbitrary moment that has nothing to do with the buses, and you take the first bus that comes.

  4. 4

    The question

    How many minutes do you wait for a bus on average?

Intuition says

"Half the gap, so 5 minutes"

In fact it is 10 minutes. Five minutes would be right for buses that keep a strict timetable. When the gaps are random, you land in long ones more often, and the wait doubles.

What we assume

  • The average gap between buses is 10 minutes.
  • Buses are independent: when the last one came tells you nothing about the next.
  • Your arrival is unrelated to the buses: you can't see them coming and don't know when the last one left.
  • You board the first bus, and there is room for everyone.

One hour of such a line might look like this: seven buses, gaps from 2 to 22 minutes.

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    You are not picking a bus, you are picking a moment. If you turn up at random, which gap are you more likely to land in: a short 2-minute one or a long 25-minute one?

  2. 2

    Long gaps take up more of the clock, so you land in them in proportion to their length. Also, these buses have no memory: however long you have already waited, the next bus is no closer.

  3. 3

    Take three gaps: 2, 3 and 25 minutes, 10 on average. You land in each with chance length/30 and wait half of it. Work out 2/30 · 1 + 3/30 · 1.5 + 25/30 · 12.5 and compare it with 5.

Round 4 · Explanations

Long gaps catch more riders

The slip is not in the arithmetic but in the sample. "The average gap" is computed over buses, while a rider sees a gap picked by time.

In plain wordspictures, analogies, small numbers

“In plain words” is hidden on all puzzles.

Length-biased sampling

A long gap covers more of the clock

Four buses in half an hour give three gaps: 2, 3 and 25 minutes, 10 on average. Spread 12 riders evenly, one every 2.5 minutes. The short gaps get one rider each, the long gap gets ten. A rider picks a moment, not a bus, and the long gap takes up 25 of the 30 minutes.

So the gap you land in is chosen with weight proportional to its length. That is what length-biased sampling means.

Doing the numbers

2, 3 and 25 minutes give 10.6, not 5

Inside a gap you wait half of it on average. But the average has to be weighted by the chance of landing there: (2 · 1 + 3 · 1.5 + 25 · 12.5) / 30 = 319/30 ≈ 10.6 minutes. The gap you land in is (2² + 3² + 25²) / 30 ≈ 21.3 minutes long on average.

Half the average gap, 5 minutes, would only come out if all three gaps were 10 minutes.

The same effect everywhere

The average class as the dean sees it and as students see it

A university runs five courses: four seminars of 10 students and one lecture of 160. The dean computes the average course size: (4 · 10 + 160) / 5 = 40. Now ask all 200 students how many people are in their course. 40 students say "10" and 160 say "160". The average answer is (4 · 10² + 160²) / 200 = 130.

It's the same arithmetic as with the buses: a big course shows up in the survey as many times as it has students, just as a long gap "catches" riders in proportion to its length. And in the friendship paradox a person with many friends appears on many friend lists, so your friends have more friends than you on average.

No memory

The bus doesn't know how long you've waited

For Poisson buses there is an even shorter argument. Buses are independent, so in every minute the chance of a bus is the same, however long ago the last one came. Already waited 10 minutes? The expected remaining wait is still 10.

So the moment you arrive changes nothing: from any point to the next bus is, on average, as long as from one bus to the next, 10 minutes. This property is called memorylessness. Among continuous distributions only the exponential has it.

Back and forward

Where 20 minutes comes from

Look both ways from the moment you arrive. Forward to the next bus: 10 minutes on average. Back to the previous one: also 10, because a Poisson stream played backwards looks the same. Together, the gap you are standing in lasts 20 minutes on average, twice an ordinary gap.

No contradiction: an ordinary gap really is 10 minutes on average, but you are not standing in an ordinary one.

The wait equals half the average gap only when there is no spread (CV = 0). Each unit of CV² adds another half of the average gap. In a Poisson stream the standard deviation of a gap equals its mean, so CV = 1 and the wait doubles. When buses bunch up, the spread is even larger and you wait longer than the average gap.

Round 5 · Going further

From a timetable to buses that travel in pairs

Poisson buses are just one point on a scale. Real lines can be steadier, or worse. And biased sampling hides in many places besides bus stops.

The average gap is always 10 minutes. Only the spread changes. Gaps follow a gamma distribution with shape k: k = 1 gives Poisson buses, large k approaches a timetable, k < 1 gives buses that clump together. The curve shows 5 · (1 + CV²).

Why buses bunch

Delays feed themselves

Even a line with a timetable rarely stays even. If a bus runs a little late, more people are waiting at each stop. Boarding takes longer, so it falls further behind. The bus behind it picks up fewer people, runs faster and catches up. In the end two buses arrive back to back, and then there is a long gap with none.

This is called bus bunching. For riders it is worse than plain random buses: with the same 10-minute average gap, the wait goes above 10 minutes. Slide to the right and watch the numbers.

The left end shows why transit agencies care about regularity. Going from random buses to a timetable cuts the average wait by 5 minutes without adding a single bus.

Where else the inspection paradox hides

The mechanism is always the same: something long, big or crowded is more likely to end up in our sample, because we "inspect" the world at a random moment or through a random person.

Gym, flight, café"It's always packed"A busy hour holds more visitors, and each of them sees a crowd. Hardly anyone sees the empty room.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

1966

A paradox from Feller's textbook

The second volume of William Feller's classic textbook "An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications" has a section called "Waiting time paradoxes". It works through buses with exponential gaps: a "symmetry" argument gives half the average gap, while memorylessness gives the whole gap. The second answer is the right one.

31 → 56

"The Inspection Paradox Is Everywhere"

That is the title of Allen Downey's 2015 post on his blog "Probably Overthinking It". Using Purdue University's class-size data for 2013–14, he worked out that the average course has about 31 students, while the average student sits in a course of about 56.

7.8 → 8.8

The real Boston subway

In the same post Downey recorded the arrival times of 70 trains on Boston's Red Line between 4 and 5 pm over several days. The average gap between trains was 7.8 minutes, while the gap a rider lands in averaged 8.8 minutes. Trains run more evenly than Poisson buses, so the effect is smaller, but it is there.

1991

Why your friends have more friends

The sociologist Scott Feld published "Why your friends have more friends than you do" in the American Journal of Sociology. The friendship paradox runs on the same mechanism as the buses: people with many friends get "inspected" more often because they are on many lists.

1 + 1

Buses in pairs

Bus bunching, when buses on one line arrive in groups, is a well-known transit problem. It is also called clumping, convoying or platooning. A late bus picks up more riders and falls further behind, while the one following catches up. So if you waited a long time and then two buses came together, it was most likely no coincidence.

Screening

A bias in medicine

In medical statistics, length-biased sampling goes by the name length time bias. Routine screening is more likely to find tumours that grow slowly and stay without symptoms for a long time. As a result, survival among screen-detected cases can look better even when screening itself does not extend lives.

The ant on a rubber rope The end of the rope runs 100,000 times faster than the ant. Will the ant ever get there?It will, after about 1043,429 seconds.

A rubber rope 1 km long. An ant sits at one end and crawls toward the other: 1 centimetre a second. And every second the rope is stretched evenly by another kilometre, carrying the ant along with it. The end runs away a hundred thousand times faster than the ant crawls. Will it ever reach the end?

The stretch carries the ant along with the rope, so it never takes away the share of the way already covered. Every second the ant adds a crumb: 1 cm of a rope that keeps getting longer. The crumbs shrink, but their sum grows without limit, like the harmonic series 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … So the ant gets there, only after ≈ 1.6 × 1043,429 seconds. The universe is about 4 × 1017 seconds old.

The puzzle

An ant, a rope and an end that runs away

A classic puzzle popularised by Martin Gardner. It is about how a sum of ever smaller pieces does not have to stop at some limit.

  1. 1

    The rope

    Made of rubber, 1 km long at the start. One end is tied to a post, the other is being pulled. The rubber stretches evenly: every bit of it gets longer by the same factor, like a drawing on a balloon as it is blown up.

  2. 2

    The ant

    Starts at the tied end and crawls toward the other at a steady 1 cm a second relative to the rubber under its feet. It holds on to the rope, so when the rubber stretches, the ant rides along with the bit it sits on.

  3. 3

    Every second

    First the ant crawls its 1 cm. Then the rope is instantly stretched by another kilometre. After the first second it is 2 km long, after the second 3 km, after the hundredth 101 km. The end runs away at 1 km a second, 100,000 times faster than the ant crawls.

  4. 4

    The question

    Will the ant ever reach the far end of the rope? If so, when?

Intuition says

"The end runs away a hundred thousand times faster. The ant will never catch it"

It will catch it. The stretch pulls the ant along too: everything it has covered stretches with the rope. So count not metres but the share of the rope. And the share grows every second, if only a little, and those gains together exceed any number.

Assumptions

  • The ant is a point, crawls without stopping and lives forever. The rope never breaks.
  • The stretch is even along the whole length: a bit near the post and a bit near the end get longer by the same factor.
  • The ant's speed of 1 cm/s is relative to the rubber it walks on.
  • Stretching in jerks once a second keeps the counting simple. Pulled smoothly at 1 km a second, the answer is almost the same (round 4).

Hints

Hard to decide? Open the hints one at a time: the easy one first, then stronger ones.

  1. 1

    Do not count the metres to the end, count the share of the rope the ant has covered. What does an even stretch do to that share?

  2. 2

    In second number k the rope is k km long, so 1 cm is 1/(100,000 · k) of it. The ant arrives when these shares add up to 1.

  3. 3

    The sum 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n: is there a number it never passes? Group the terms: 1/3 + 1/4, then 1/5 + … + 1/8.

Round 4 · Why

The end runs away, yet the share grows

The whole trick is to count not metres but the share of the rope. The metres to the end really do grow. But the share the ant has covered only grows, and the only question is whether it reaches one.

The stretch keeps what is done

An ant at one third stays at one third

Before the stretch1 / 31 km of 3 km
After the stretch1 / 31⅓ km of 4 km

The rubber stretches evenly, so an ant that sat at one third of the rope is at one third again after the stretch: that third is simply longer now. The end runs away, but the ant rides forward with the rubber too. So the thing to watch is the share. Every second the ant adds 1 cm to a rope that is k km long, that is 1 / (100,000 · k) of the rope. This card does not explain why such crumbs ever add up to the whole rope. The next card and the strict layer do.

A small rope

4 metres, a 1-metre step, a 1-metre stretch

A 4 m rope, the ant covers 1 m per step, after each step the rope gets 1 m longer. The shares: ¼, then ⅕, ⅙, 1/7… Each is smaller than the one before, yet their sum passes 1 at the seventh step. After the sixth step the ant was only 4 cm short. Our puzzle works the same way, only each share is 100,000 times smaller, and the wait is unbelievably long.

The harmonic series

The share after n seconds

1/100 000 · (1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n) = Hn / 100 000

In second k the rope is k km long, and 1 cm is 1/(100,000 · k) of its length. Add them up: you get the harmonic series divided by 100,000. The ant arrives when the sum 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … passes 100,000. And it passes any number, because the terms can be grouped: ½, then ⅓ + ¼ > ½, then ⅕ + ⅙ + 1/7 + ⅛ > ½, and so on. Each next group is twice as long and gives more than ½. There are infinitely many groups, so the sum is infinite. Nicole Oresme found this proof around 1350.

How slowly

The sum grows like a logarithm

Across is time in seconds, each mark 10 times more. The sum 1 + ½ + … + 1/n is almost a straight line here: it is about ln n + 0.5772. To add one to the sum you must multiply the time by e ≈ 2.718. That is why after a year the sum is only 17.8, after the age of the universe 41.2, and it reaches 100,000 only at n ≈ e100,000 − 0.5772 ≈ 1.6 × 1043,429.

For formula lovers

Any rope, in jerks and smoothly

Rope L, the ant covers v per step, after each step the rope gets longer by s. During step k it is L + (k − 1)s. The share after n steps:

θn=∑k=1nvL+(k−1)s=vs(ψ(Ls+n)−ψ(Ls))

ψ is the digamma function, "the harmonic number for fractional arguments". In our puzzle L = s = 1 km, v = 1 cm, so the share is just Hn / 100,000, and Hn ≈ ln n + γ, γ ≈ 0.5772.

HN≥100 000⇒N≈e100 000−γ≈1,58 × 1043 429

Pulled smoothly at speed c (length L + ct), the share grows by dθ/dt = v / (L + ct). Hence a logarithm and an exact answer:

θ(t)=vcln(1+ctL),T=Lc(ec/v−1)=(e100 000−1) s≈2,8 × 1043 429 s

In jerks and smoothly the answers differ only by a factor of eγ ≈ 1.78: 1.6 and 2.8 × 1043,429 seconds. Both numbers have 43,430 digits. The page checked the formula against the step-by-step simulation in round 3.

History

From Gardner to today

Martin Gardner spread the puzzle widely: it appeared in Scientific American, and in 1982 he included it in his book of paradoxes "aha! Gotcha: Paradoxes to Puzzle and Delight" (pp. 145–146). In some retellings a worm on a rubber band takes the ant's place. Who first came up with the puzzle, the sources we checked do not say, so we do not claim it.

The puzzle is still being extended: in 2013 Mark McCartney devoted a paper to it, "Extending the rubber rope: convergent series, divergent series and the integrating factor", in the International Journal of Mathematical Education in Science and Technology. As the title says, in the generalisations the series can diverge or converge. A converging series means the ant never gets there. That is round 5.

Analogy · the universe

Light is the ant

When people say the universe is expanding, they mean roughly our rope: distant galaxies are not flying through space, the space between them stretches, and distances grow proportionally. Light from a distant galaxy is the ant: it moves at the same speed relative to the space it travels through, while that space stretches beneath it.

If the expansion goes like our rope, without acceleration, light from any galaxy reaches us eventually, if only after a very long time. But if the expansion accelerates, and observations say our universe does, there is a limit for light emitted today: the event horizon. What lies beyond it will never reach us. Round 5 shows this on a model.

Round 5 · Scaling up

When the ant fails, books over the edge and the cosmic horizon

Mathematics: if the rope is pulled with acceleration, the ant may never get there, and the boundary is exact. Logic: a sum of ever smaller pieces can be infinite, and you can build it from books. Practice: the same calculation decides which galaxies we will never see.

Where the boundary is

What matters is how fast the denominators grow

A 10 m rope, the ant covers a percentage of those 10 m per step. The share is the sum v/L₁ + v/L₂ + … If the rope grows by the same amount each time, the denominators grow like k and the sum is the harmonic series: infinite, the ant gets there whatever its step. If the length grows like k², the sum 1 + 1/2 + 1/5 + 1/10 + … is finite: with L = 10 + 10(k − 1)² it equals (1 + π coth π)/2 ≈ 2.077 ant steps per 10 m, so the ant gets there only if its step is at least 48.2% of the rope. If the rope grows by 10% each time, the sum is a geometric series with limit 11 · v/L, and the threshold is 9.1%.

The general rule for a length growing like kp: for p ≤ 1 the ant always gets there, for p > 1 only if it is fast enough. The boundary runs exactly between the harmonic series and anything that grows even slightly faster.

Logic

Books over the table edge

Top book beyond the edge–

Smaller and smaller, yet no limit

The same series, made of books

Stack identical books so the top one sticks out over the next by ½ a length, that one over the third by ¼, then ⅙, ⅛… Each stack holds, because the centre of gravity of everything above sits exactly over the edge of the book below. The total overhang is half the harmonic series: (1 + ½ + ⅓ + … + 1/n) / 2. Just 4 books carry the top one fully past the table edge, 31 by two lengths, 227 by three.

The logic is the ant's: the terms shrink to zero, but that is not enough for the sum to be finite. They must shrink fast enough. Compare the fly between two trains: each leg is five times shorter than the one before, and infinitely many legs add up to a finite 75 km.

The event horizon

A rope the size of the universe

Two toy models with today's expansion rate H₀ ≈ 70 km/s per megaparsec. Uniform, like our rope: the distance to a galaxy grows by the same number of kilometres every second. Then light gets through from any distance, and the time is the ant's formula: (1/H₀)(eH₀D/c − 1). Accelerating: the distance is multiplied by the same factor each time. Then the share of the way light covers has a limit, and from galaxies farther than c/H₀ ≈ 14 billion light years, light emitted today never arrives.

The real universe is more complex: the expansion first slowed down and now speeds up because of dark energy. By Lineweaver and Davis's estimate (Scientific American, 2005), the event horizon is now about 16 billion light years away. Light that galaxies beyond it emit today will never reach us, although we do see their old light. Galaxies held together by gravity, like our Milky Way, do not stretch.

Bonus · Fun facts

Things to tell your friends

43 430

Digits in the answer

The number of seconds until the ant finishes takes 43,430 digits to write. Printed in small type, it fills a dozen pages or so. The age of the universe in seconds has 18 digits.

10 km

On the first day

In a day the ant itself crawls only 864 m. But the stretch carries it along with the rubber, so at the end of the day it is already 10.3 km from the post. By then the rope is 86,401 km long, and 0.012% of it is done.

c. 1350

Oresme's proof

The French philosopher Nicole Oresme proved around 1350 that the harmonic series has no limit: he grouped the terms so that each group exceeds ½. It is one of the oldest proofs about series and is still taught from textbooks.

12,367

Terms to reach 10

The sum 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … exceeds 10 only at the 12,367th term. To reach 100 takes about 1.5 × 1043 terms. The series is endless, but it crawls slower than the ant.

1982

"aha! Gotcha"

Martin Gardner included this puzzle in his book of paradoxes "aha! Gotcha" (W. H. Freeman, 1982). That is the source the Wikipedia article on the puzzle cites.

16 bn

Light years to the horizon

The event horizon of our universe is now about 16 billion light years away (Lineweaver and Davis, Scientific American, 2005). Light that galaxies beyond it emit today will never reach us. An ant on a rope pulled ever faster is in the same position.
